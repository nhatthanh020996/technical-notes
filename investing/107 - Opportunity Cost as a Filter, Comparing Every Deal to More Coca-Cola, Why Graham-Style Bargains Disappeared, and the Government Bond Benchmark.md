# Opportunity Cost as a Filter, Comparing Every Deal to More Coca-Cola, Why Graham-Style Bargains Disappeared, and the Government Bond Benchmark

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bTHzpqhsDg4

Recorded: a mid-to-late-1990s Berkshire annual meeting, perhaps 1995–1998 (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger: Why You Must Never Have Too Many Stocks In Your Portfolio". It is misleading: Munger never says you "must never" own too many stocks. He says that using opportunity cost as a filter leads to a concentrated portfolio, "which we don't mind," and wonders why more institutions don't do it. Most of the clip is about comparing every new idea against what you already own (Coca-Cola, Gillette) or a government bond, why Graham-style bargains got harder to find, and how Berkshire's size shrinks its universe. The clip is from a Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence: numbered "zones" for microphones, "I knew we'd get in trouble after lunch"). The year is uncertain. The clues: Berkshire still owns Gillette (before 2005); Munger refers to "that talk I gave at the USC business school" (his 1994 talk on elementary worldly wisdom), so it is after 1994; Buffett mentions "the last 40 years that we've been up on a podium" and treats Coca-Cola and Gillette as the model holdings, which fits the mid-to-late 1990s. My guess is 1995–1998. It is not clear whether all segments come from the same meeting.

## Part 3: Why the Graham-style bargains got harder to find ([02:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bTHzpqhsDg4&t=165))

**Charlie Munger:** Well, I will say this: that the concept of intrinsic value used to be a lot easier, because there were all kinds of stocks that were selling for 50% or less of the amount at which you could have easily liquidated the whole corporation if you owned the whole corporation. Indeed, in the history of Berkshire Hathaway, we bought things at 20% of then liquidating value. And in the old days, the Ben Graham followers could run their Geiger counters over corporate America, and they could [unclear: "spell out"] a few things, and you could easily see, if you were at all familiar with the market prices of whole corporations, that you were buying at a huge discount. Well, no matter how bad the management, if you're buying at 50% of asset value, or 30%, or so on down, you have a lot going for you.

And as the world has wised up, and as stocks have behaved so well for people that stocks generally have gone to higher and higher prices, that game gets much harder now, to find something at a discount from intrinsic value. Those simple systems ordinarily don't work.
