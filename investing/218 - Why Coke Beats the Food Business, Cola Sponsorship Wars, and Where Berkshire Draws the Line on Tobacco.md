# Why Coke Beats the Food Business, Cola Sponsorship Wars, and Where Berkshire Draws the Line on Tobacco

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vtv-K4EKBPk

Recorded: compilation. Part 2: 1997 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence); Parts 3-4: around 2001 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence); Parts 5-7: around 2015 interview at a Coca-Cola event (moderate confidence); Part 8: probably mid-to-late 1990s, venue unknown (low confidence); Parts 9-10: Berkshire annual meetings, year undated; Part 1: teaser montage, mostly excerpts of later parts

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Coca-Cola Is My Favourite Business For The Next Decade". It is misleading: Buffett never calls Coca-Cola his favourite business for the next decade, and no part of the clip makes a ten-year forecast for Coke. The closest he comes is saying Berkshire has never sold a share, that "if you're looking for a wonderful business it was hard to beat Coca-Cola", and that you would never get "quite the inevitability" in the food business that you get with Coca-Cola. The clip is a compilation of several sources, which the uploader does not say. Dating, part by part:
>
> - **Part 1** is a teaser montage. Most of it repeats Parts 4 and 5. The lines about "some 400 million shares" and "1.9 billion eight ounce servings a day" are not repeated later; 400 million shares fits Berkshire's holding after Coca-Cola's 2012 stock split, and 1.9 billion servings a day fits roughly 2013-2016, so these are probably from the same setting as Parts 5-7 (low confidence).
> - **Part 2** is from the **1997** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence): Buffett counts "about 111 years" from 1886.
> - **Parts 3-4** are probably from the **2001** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence). The clues: Coke took Venezuela from Pepsi (the 1996 Cisneros deal) "about five years ago"; Doug Daft (Coca-Cola CEO 2000-2004) is the current CEO; Dasani (launched 1999) had gains of "like 95 percent" last year; the Kodak/Fuji Los Angeles Olympics (1984) was "20 years ago or so".
> - **Parts 5-7** are an interview, apparently at a Coca-Cola event ("that's why I'm here"; "this company has done that throughout its history"), probably around **2015** (moderate confidence): the interviewer refers to "your annual report this year" discussing wonderful businesses at fair prices versus fair businesses at wonderful prices (the 2014 letter, published in 2015), Buffett says the US has been "coming back since the fall of 2009" and mentions talk of a double dip "a few years ago", and Coke is described as having about three thousand products.
> - **Part 8** follows on in the captions without a break, but the numbers do not fit 2015: Berkshire owning "eight percent of 900 million plus" servings a day points to the mid-to-late 1990s, when Coke sold roughly a billion servings a day and Berkshire owned about 8% (low confidence; venue unknown).
> - **Part 9** (a shareholder question with applause and a peanut brittle joke) and **Part 10** (tobacco) are from Berkshire annual meetings; I could not date them. Part 10 refers to RJ Reynolds's LBO (1989) as past.
>
> In Part 10 the line "you wouldn't buy a company that made the tobacco and concocted the advertisements" follows a `[Music]` cut in the captions; I have guessed it is Munger interjecting, but it could be a follow-up from the questioner.
>

It was sort of interesting. About five years ago or thereabouts, Coke took Venezuela essentially away from Pepsi. But in any event, Pepsi was very upset. And so the University of Nebraska pouring rights came up very shortly thereafter. And the universities, as you know, bid out these things to get sort of an exclusive to a given university. And Pepsi came in and bid about twice as much for the University of Nebraska pouring rights as was sort of the standard, in terms of per student, at universities throughout the country, at Penn State or something. And I like to think that they were trying to stick it in the eye of Coke by doing that in Nebraska. Now, the question is, people [unclear: "cold called"] me and they said, you know, [unclear: "you want us to go up against us"]. And I said, you know, no. So there is this bit where one organization or the other, particularly if they've lost one in the immediate past, may overbid a little for the next one. And, you know, for United Airlines, the question is how far do you let United, or whomever it is, drive you in terms of making that specific deal.
