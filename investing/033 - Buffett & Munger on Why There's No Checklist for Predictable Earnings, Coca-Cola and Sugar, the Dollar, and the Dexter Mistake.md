# Buffett & Munger on Why There's No Checklist for Predictable Earnings, Coca-Cola and Sugar, the Dollar, and the Dexter Mistake

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Mxyuo23fM0

Recorded: 2015 Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: How To Predict Future Company Earnings". It is misleading: asked for five characteristics that let him predict earnings 10 years out, Buffett says they have no list of five and no one-size-fits-all formula; he only says they look for businesses where they have "some reasonable fix" on the next five or 10 years and that most of their thinking is about filters that stop a deal. IBM, named in the question, is not discussed. The rest of the clip covers other questions the title does not mention: "talking your book" and buybacks, networking without a business-school network, whether sugar concerns are narrowing Coca-Cola's and Kraft Heinz's moats, the dollar as reserve currency, and Buffett's Dexter Shoe mistake. The clip is from the **2015** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence). The clues: Buffett says "Charlie's 91" (Munger turned 91 on January 1, 2015); the Heinz–Kraft merger is described as current ("now we're going to own those brands"); Van Tuyl (2014) is cited as a past deal; a questioner refers to "the last 50 years" and thanks them for their essays on the past, present and future of Berkshire, written for the 2014 annual report (Berkshire's 50th anniversary); Munger says "we just came through" a period when money printing was needed.

## Part 1: No five-point checklist for predictable earnings; "talking your book"; networking without business school ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Mxyuo23fM0&t=0))

*(The clip starts at the end of the question; the questioner's name is not included.)*

**Questioner:** My question is: can you name at least five characteristics of a company that gives you confidence to predict its earnings 10 years out in the future? And can you also use IBM as a case study, how it checked all those boxes?

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie, what are your five?

We don't have a one-size-fits-all system for buying businesses. They're all different. Every industry is different. And we also keep learning, so what we did 10 years ago, we hopefully are doing better now. But we can't give you a formula that will help you.

Now if you're looking at the BNSF railroad, as we were in 2009, or if you're looking at Van Tuyl in 2014, there are a lot of things that go through our minds, and most of the things that go through our minds are things that will stop us from going further. I mean, the filters are there, and there are a lot of things that, if we see it in a business, including maybe who we're dealing with, will stop us from going on to the next layer. But it's very different in different businesses.

We are looking for things where we do think we've got some reasonable fix on how it's going to look in five or 10 years, and that does eliminate a great many businesses. But it's not the same five questions at all. Certainly when we're buying a business where we're going to have somebody that's selling it to us continue to run it for us, a very big question is: do we really want to be in partnership with this person, and count on them to behave in the future, when they don't own the business, as they behaved in the past when they do own the business? And that stops a fair number of deals. But I can't give you a five. We don't have a list of five, or if we do, Charlie's kept it from me.

Incidentally, there [is] one thing I always find interesting. We get asked questions about investments we own, and people think we want to talk them up. We have no interest in encouraging other people to buy the investments we own. I mean, we are better off [...] because either we or the company is likely to be buying stock in the future. Why would we want the stock to go up if we're going to be a buyer next year and the year after and the year after that?

But the whole mentality of Wall Street is that if you buy something, even if you're going to buy more of it later on, or if the company's going to buy its own stock, people seem to think that they're better off if it goes up the next day or the next week or the next month. And that's why they talk about "talking your book." If we talked our book from our standpoint, we would say pessimistic things about all four of the biggest holdings we have, because all four of them are repurchasing their shares, and obviously the cheaper they repurchase their shares, the better off we are. But people don't seem to get that point. You have any idea why, Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Warren, if people weren't so often wrong, we wouldn't be so rich.

**Warren Buffett:** He's finally explained it to me. Okay. Okay, station four.

**Questioner ([unclear: "n Patel"], [unclear: "hail"], Massachusetts):** Hello Mr. Munger and Mr. Buffett. What advice would you give to someone who's trying to network with influential people but doesn't have access to the alumni network of a top business school?

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie's very Old Testament on this. [He] didn't get much past Genesis. If it was [...] his question [was] that he didn't have a lot of associations because [...] well, he'd like to have you help him tap into [...] [unclear: "T do well without business school training"].

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. And actually I would say the business school training, particularly in investments, was a handicap about 20 years ago, when they were preaching efficient market theory, because essentially they told you it didn't do any good to try and figure out what a company was worth, because the market had a price perfectly already. Now imagine paying 30 or $40,000 a year to hear that. Yes, you were very lucky to avoid a lot that you've avoided. How do you feel about your law school training, Charlie, while we're on it?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, I have a son-in-law who recently explained how modern profit-obsessed law firms work. He says it's like a pie-eating contest, and if you win, you get to eat a lot more pie.

## Part 2: Sugar, Coca-Cola, and Heinz: are the moats narrowing? ([05:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Mxyuo23fM0&t=332))

**Questioner:** Over the last 50 years, we Berkshire shareholders have effectively been long sugar consumption, through directly owned companies such as See's Candies, Dairy Queen, [unclear: "in funding"] Heinz, and in publicly traded investments such as Coca-Cola. Today, yet, from improvements in scientific research, we as a society have become increasingly attuned to the true cost of greater sugar consumption in the form of rising health care costs. We are seeing this awareness of sugar's impact in changing consumer behavior. Carbonated soft drink volumes are declining, and consumer packaged goods companies focused on the center aisles of supermarkets are struggling with organic growth. Have we reached an inflection point in human behavior and how consumers view sugar consumption? And do you think Coca-Cola's moat, and potentially that of Heinz or Kraft's, is narrowing? And if not, what news would it take you to be convinced that it is?

**Warren Buffett:** Well, I think it's an enormously wide moat, but I think it's also true that the trends you described are happening. But there will be 1.9 billion 8-ounce servings of Coca-Cola products (not Coca-Cola, but Coca-Cola products) consumed in the world today. And I don't think you're going to see anything revolutionary, and I think you will see all food and beverage companies adjust to the expressed preferences of the consumers as they go along. No company ever does well ignoring its consumers.

But there will be [...] I would predict 20 years from now there will be more people [...] there will be more Coca-Cola cases consumed than there are now, by some margin. Back in the late 1930s, Fortune ran an article saying that the growth of Coca-Cola was all over. And when we bought our Coca-Cola stock in 1988, people were not that enthused about growth possibilities for the product.

I sit here as somebody who, in the last 30 years, one quarter of all the calories I've consumed come from Coca-Cola, and that is not an exaggeration. I am one quarter Coca-Cola. I'm not sure which quarter. And [...] I don't think there is this choice [...] I think there's a lot to be said about being happy with what you're doing. If I'd been eating broccoli and Brussels sprouts and all that all my life, I don't think I'd live as long. Every meal I would approach thinking it's like going to jail or something again.

Now I think Charlie [...] Charlie's 91, and his habits aren't that different than mine.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* They're slightly better, there's no question about it. The way I look at it is sugar is an enormously helpful substance. It prevents premature softening of the arteries. And the way I look at it is that if I die a little sooner, that'll just be avoiding a few months of drooling in a nursing home.

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie and I have enjoyed every meal we've ever had, virtually, except when I was eating my grandfather's [unclear: "he maybe eat those damn green vegetables"].

There obviously are some shifts in preferences, although it's remarkable how durable items are in this field. We, Berkshire Hathaway, I believe, was the largest shareholder of General Foods from about 1981 or thereabouts to about 1984, when it was bought by Philip Morris. And that's 30-plus years ago, and those same brands [...] they went through Philip Morris, they got spun out as Kraft, they broke Kraft into two pie[ces], but now we're going to own those brands, and they're terrific brands. But Heinz goes back to 1869. The ketchup came out a little later. They went bankrupt, actually, when they were counting on horseradish or whatever it was, but the ketchup came out in the 1870s. And Coca-Cola dates to 1886. And it's a pretty good bet that an awful lot of people are going to like the same thing. And when I compare drinking Coca-Cola to something that somebody would sell me at Whole Foods, I don't know, I don't see smiles on the faces of people with Whole Foods. And so I like the brands we're buying.

Andrew?
