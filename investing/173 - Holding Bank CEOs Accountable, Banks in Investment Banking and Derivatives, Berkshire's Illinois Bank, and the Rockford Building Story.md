# Holding Bank CEOs Accountable, Banks in Investment Banking and Derivatives, Berkshire's Illinois Bank, and the Rockford Building Story

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GL949y1LJhc

Recorded: 2023 Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren and Charlie: The Truth About Bank Fraud". It is misleading: nobody talks about bank fraud. Buffett says CEOs and directors, not future shareholders, should pay when a bank gets into trouble; Munger says he preferred banks before they went back into investment banking, and both object to banks in derivative trading; Buffett answers a question about big versus regional banks after Silicon Valley Bank by describing the Rockford, Illinois bank Berkshire owned from 1969 and was forced to divest under the 1970 Bank Holding Company Act, and says he sold bank stocks; and Buffett tells the story of a would-be successor at the Rockford bank who wanted a taller building. The clip is from the **2023** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence). The clues: the question names the events at Silicon Valley Bank (March 2023); Becky Quick reads the question; Buffett says Berkshire sold bank stocks "when the pandemic broke out and then ... in the last 6 months" and still holds Bank of America; Charlie Munger is present.

## Part 3: Big banks vs. regional banks after Silicon Valley Bank; Berkshire's Illinois bank ([04:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GL949y1LJhc&t=278))

**Warren Buffett:** Okay, Becky.

**Becky Quick:** This question comes from Davis Hance in Houston, Texas. He writes: "What do you think about the business models of the big banks as compared to the regional banks in the wake of the events at Silicon Valley Bank? And how does the perceived implicit guarantee of all deposits at all banks affect big banks and those regional banks?"

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, well, I can say this. If you follow sound banking methods, which mean not doing some things that other people do, a bank could be a perfectly decent investment.

In fact, Charlie and I, well, me originally, in 1969 we bought a bank at Berkshire, and we had $19 million invested in that bank, and we had 17 million, I think, invested in our insurance companies. And if the Bank Holding Company Act of 1970 hadn't been passed, we might have ended up owning a lot of banks instead of a lot of insurance companies. We were looking at more banks, and Harry Keefe was taking us around Chicago, and there were other things we could do. And then, bingo, they passed the 1970 Bank Holding Company Act, and we had to divest ourselves of that bank in 10 years.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Which, by the way, never had a bad debt.

**Warren Buffett:** Oh, it never had an unnecessary cost. It made nothing but money with no risk, and never presented any deposit insurance risk to the government. Zero. It was a lovely, sound, constructive institution in this community, and any person who really deserved credit could get credit.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Yeah.

**Warren Buffett:** We were going to buy more banks. We were forced out of it. And we were going to buy more banks, and if we bought more banks, we probably wouldn't have expanded the insurance business. But the law changed, and so we divested, and we've done okay on insurance. But banking was more attractive to us. It was bigger, and there were more targets to buy. And you could run a perfectly sound bank then, and no negotiable certificates, all these things, all the inventions that came later. And you could still run it today, and you can earn a lot, you could earn good money, very good money. And we would have found more banks, but we're precluded from doing that.

And I would say that you've had something less than a perfect communication between various people and the American public.
