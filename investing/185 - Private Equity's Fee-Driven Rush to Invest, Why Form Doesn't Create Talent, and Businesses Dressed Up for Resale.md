# Private Equity's Fee-Driven Rush to Invest, Why Form Doesn't Create Talent, and Businesses Dressed Up for Resale

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r1EEixDB988

Recorded: compilation. Parts 1–3: probably Berkshire annual meetings of about 2005–2007 (low–moderate confidence); Part 4: a later Munger answer, probably 2010s, venue unknown (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why Private Equity Is Bad For Investors". It is somewhat misleading: Buffett and Munger do not say private equity is bad for the people who invest in it. They say a 2% fee pushes funds to invest fast so they can raise the next fund, that no form (hedge fund, private equity, mutual fund) produces results by itself, that businesses bought by financial buyers and quickly resold are not attractive buys for Berkshire, and that leveraged buyouts make it very hard for Berkshire to buy businesses. The only comment about investor returns is Munger's on **managed futures** funds ("somewhere between lousy and negative").
>
> Date: The clip has no chapters and no upload date, and it appears to be stitched from several answers. Parts 1–3 talk about "this frenzied activity" in private equity, a $20 billion fund, a profit flow to finance that has "no precedent", and funds reselling businesses to each other "three or four times". This fits the 2005–2007 buyout boom, so probably Berkshire meetings of those years (low–moderate confidence). Part 4 uses the term "shadow banking", which became common only after 2007, and Munger says the buyout people now "say they do private equity". So it is probably a later Munger answer from the 2010s, possibly at a Berkshire or Daily Journal meeting (low confidence).

## Part 1: The 2% fee and the compulsion to invest quickly ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r1EEixDB988&t=0))

*(The clip starts in the middle of Buffett's answer; the question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** There's one other aspect, of course, of this frenzied activity, you might say, in private equity, is that if you have a 20 billion dollar fund and you're getting a two percent fee on it, or 400 million dollars a year, which seems like chump change to those that are managing them but sounds like real money [in] Omaha. If you're getting 400 million a year from that 20 billion dollar fund, you can't start another fund with a straight face until you get that money pretty well invested. It's very hard to go back to your investors and say, "Well, I've got 18 billion uninvested and I'd like you to give me money for another fund." So there's a great compulsion to invest very quickly, because it's the way to get another fund and another bunch of fees coming in. And those are not competitors for businesses that Charlie and I are going to be particularly effective in competing with. I mean, we are going to own anything we buy forever, the math has to make sense to us, we're not given to optimistic assumptions, and we don't get paid based on activity.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Yeah, of course a lot of the profits are not in the manufacturing sector or the retailing sector either. A lot of them are in this financial sector. So we've had a huge flow of profit to banks and investment banks, you know, investment management groups of all kinds, including various kinds of private equity, and that has, I think, no precedent. I don't think it's ever been as extreme as it is now. Do you agree with that?
