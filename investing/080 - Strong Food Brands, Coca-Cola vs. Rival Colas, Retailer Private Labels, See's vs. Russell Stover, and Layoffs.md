# Strong Food Brands, Coca-Cola vs. Rival Colas, Retailer Private Labels, See's vs. Russell Stover, and Layoffs

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q7yInbYLBX8

Recorded: most likely a 2008–2010 Berkshire annual meeting, probably 2009 (low-moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett Explains Why You Should Own Food Stocks". It is misleading: Buffett does not tell anyone to own food stocks. He says most big food companies with leading brands are good businesses, but that the attributes are "pretty well recognized", so you are "not going to get super rich", and that what Berkshire buys depends on price and management. Most of the clip is about how hard it is to attack a strong brand (Coca-Cola, Kool-Aid, Gillette, See's) and the constant fight between brands and retailers' private labels. The clip ends with a short, separate fragment about layoffs. The year is not stated. Clues: the question appears to be about Kraft (Berkshire held a large Kraft stake from 2007); Richard Branson's Virgin Cola came to the US "seven or eight years ago, 10 years ago" (it launched in the US in the late 1990s); Sam Walton sent Buffett the first six-pack of Sam's Cola "20 years ago"; the closing fragment on layoffs fits the 2008–2009 recession. This points to roughly 2008–2010, with 2009 a guess. "Station nine" at the end is the Berkshire meeting microphone call. "Don kill" in the captions is possibly Don Keough, the former Coca-Cola president (editor's guess).

## Part 1: Leading branded food products are good businesses ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q7yInbYLBX8&t=0))

*(The clip starts at the beginning of the answer; the question itself, apparently about Kraft, is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** Well, I would say most of the big food companies are good businesses, in that they earn good returns on tangible assets. And I don't want to get into, particularly, the specifics on Kraft, but if you own important branded products in this country, whether it's [unclear: "a regulation"] or Mars or Coca-Cola or a number of the Kraft brands or See's Candy, you have good assets. It's not easy to take on those products.

Just imagine taking on Coca-Cola. [It] will sell a billion and a half eight-ounce servings of its product around the world today. There's something in every person's [unclear: "thing"], in every person's mind, virtually, in the globe about Coca-Cola. That product, since 1886, has been associated with happiness and good value in terms of refreshment and all that. It's just about impossible, in my mind anyway, to take on a product like that. It clearly satisfies people, everywhere on the globe.

And it may not be the same [unclear: "craft for example as"] Kool-Aid in the powdered soft drink business. I don't think I'd want to try and take on Kool-Aid. I'd rather have Coca-Cola, but it's a tough product. And to get implanted, just think of [...] to get implanted in people's mind. RC Cola, around the world. RC Cola has been around a long, long time. It isn't going to go anyplace. I mean, that is very, very difficult.

And actually Richard Branson came over [to] this country [...] They say that a brand is a promise. I mean, there's a promise involved in picking up a Milky Way or picking up a Coca-Cola as to what it's going to deliver to you. Richard Branson came over seven or eight years ago, 10 years ago, a fellow with a famous airline and all of that, and he came out with something called Virgin Cola. ## Part 2: Brands vs. retailers, and protecting a brand (Sam's Cola, Gillette, See's vs. Russell Stover) ([03:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q7yInbYLBX8&t=192))

**Warren Buffett:** Okay. The strong brands, just look at the ones that General Foods had in the 1980s and the ones that Kraft has now that come from that same company. And I mean, Coca-Cola sold more cases of beverages last year than in a year in their history, and they'll sell more this year. I mean, a strong brand is really potent stuff. I mean, take Heinz Ketchup or something of the sort. It's 60 brand share in the United States, but it's much higher in many other countries.

So you'll always have the fight between the retailer and the brand, and the retailer is going to use all the pressure they've got. And therefore the brand has to stand for something in the consumer's mind, because in the end the retailer may want to shift him to a house brand, private label. And private labels have been around forever in the soft drink field. I mean, I can remember when I was looking at Cott Beverage and all of those and thinking, you know, what will it do to us?

I remember when Sam Walton sent me the first six-pack. He told me it's the first six-pack of Sam's Cola, 20 years ago. And believe me, Walmart has plenty of power. So does Coca-Cola.

And the brand, you've got to nourish them. You've got to take very, very, very good care of them. They have to stand for the promise that's in people's mind about them. But a lot of people have tried to [...] I don't know how many dozens or maybe hundreds of cola beverages there have been over the years. RC Cola, they came up with the first diet product back in the early '60s, and that looked like a big, big maneuver. Wilkinson came up with the blade back in the '60s, after Gillette, and [...] But Gillette ends up with 70 percent by dollar value worldwide of razor blades after 100 years.

So you've got to protect a brand. You've got to enhance it in every way. You've got to get a promise in people's minds that gets delivered that way.

But that's the question Charlie and I faced in 1972 when we looked at See's. See's was selling for 1.95 a pound. Russell Stover was selling a little cheaper, and [unclear: "he"; context suggests "we"] had to decide how much damage could a Russell Stover do if they came after See's and they copied our shops and all that sort of thing.

If you protect a brand, if you've got a terrific brand and you protect it, it's a fabulous asset. But you'll always have trouble dealing with Costco and Walmart and the rest of the guys, Kroger, you name it. They're tough too. But the great brands will survive, and the great retailers will do well.

Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, we've almost exhausted this topic.
