# Buffett & Munger on Private Equity Fees, Inflated Returns, and Pension Fund Risks

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r3_41Whvr1I

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Private Equity Firms Are Typically Very Dishonest". It is somewhat exaggerated but not far off. Buffett does say that some private equity return calculations are not done in a way he would regard as honest, and Munger says the industry is "lying a little bit to make the money come in". He does not say private equity firms are "typically" dishonest. The clip most likely comes from the 2019 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting: the questioner (Brandon) works for a pension fund in Winnipeg, and Buffett refers to the problems at the Omaha Public Schools retirement fund and to a U.S. stock market worth "something over 30 trillion". Confidence in the year: moderate.

---

## Part 1: Too much money chasing private businesses, and dishonest return figures

*(The clip starts mid-answer. From the context, a pension fund manager from Winnipeg asked about investing in alternative assets such as private equity.)*

**Warren Buffett:** I would not get excited about so-called alternative investments. You can get all kinds of different figures, but there's probably at least a trillion dollars committed to, in effect, buying businesses. And if you figure they're going to leverage them two for one on that, you may have three trillion of buying power trying to buy businesses. The U.S. market is maybe something over 30 trillion now, but there are all kinds of businesses that aren't for sale. So the supply and demand situation for buying businesses privately and leveraging them up has changed dramatically from what it was 10 or 20 years ago.

I'm sure it doesn't happen with your Winnipeg operation, but we have seen a number of proposals from private equity funds where the returns are really not calculated in a manner that I would regard as honest. If I were running a pension fund, I would be very careful about what was being offered to me.

If you have a choice on Wall Street between being a great analyst or being a great salesperson, the salesperson is the way to make it. If you can raise 10 billion dollars in a fund, get one and a half percent, and lock people up for 10 years, you and your children and your grandchildren will never have to do a thing, even if you were the dumbest investor in the world. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, I think what we're doing will work more safely than what he's doing, but I wish you well.

## Part 2: Public pension funds and the Omaha Public Schools example

**Warren Buffett:** Brandon, you actually sound like a guy that I would hope would be working for a public pension fund, because frankly most of the institutional funds... Well, we had this terrible thing right here in Omaha. You can get the story of what happened with our Omaha Public Schools retirement fund. They were doing fine until the manager started going in a different direction. The trustees were perfectly decent people, and the manager had done okay to that point.

**Charlie Munger:** They were smarter in Winnipeg than they are here.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, well, that was pretty bad here. It's not a fair fight, actually, when a bunch of public officials are listening to people who really just get paid for raising the money. Everything else is gravy after that. If you get even one percent of a billion, you're getting 10 million dollars a year coming in, and if you've got the money locked up for a long time, it's a very one-sided deal.

I told the story of asking a guy one time in the past why in the world he could ask for two and twenty when he really didn't have any kind of evidence that he was going to do better with the money in the next fund. And he said, "Well, that's because I can't get three and thirty."

---

## Part 3: Why the reported returns are not as good as they look

**Charlie Munger:** You know what I don't like about a lot of the pension investment in this? I think they like it because they don't have to mark it down as much as it should be in the middle of the panics. I think that's a silly reason to buy something, because you're given leniency in marking it down.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. And when you commit the money in the case of private equity, often they don't take the money, but you pay a fee on the money that you've committed. And of course you really have to have that money available to come up with at any time. It makes their return look better if you sit there for a long time in Treasury bills, which you have to hold because they can call you up and demand the money. They count it in terms of getting a fee on it, but they don't count it in terms of the so-called internal rate of return. It's not as good as it looks.

**Charlie Munger:** And I really do think that when you have a group sitting as a state pension fund, all they're doing is lying a little bit to make the money come in.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, that sums it up.

## Part 4: Munger's horse story

**Charlie Munger:** This man has this wonderful horse. It's just a marvelous horse: it's got an easy gait, it's good looking, and everything works wonderfully. But occasionally it just gets dangerously vicious and causes enormous damage and trouble, and breaks arms and legs for others, and so on. So he goes to the vet and says, "What can I do about this horse?" The vet says, "That's a very easy problem, and I'm glad to help you." He says, "What should I do?" And the vet says, "The next time your horse is behaving well, sell it."

Well, think of how immoral that is. And haven't I just described what private equity has to do?
