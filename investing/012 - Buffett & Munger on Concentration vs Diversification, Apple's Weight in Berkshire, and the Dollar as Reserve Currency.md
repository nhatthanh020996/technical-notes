# Buffett & Munger on Concentration vs Diversification, Apple's Weight in Berkshire, and the Dollar as Reserve Currency

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZNGIA3htIto

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger: Why Diversification Is Total Nonsense". It is somewhat misleading. Munger calls the idea that *vast* diversification is mandatory "insane", and argues for owning your best ideas if you know the limits of your competence. He does not say diversification in general is nonsense, and only the first part of the clip is about it. The second part is about U.S. debt, money printing, and whether the dollar will stay the world's reserve currency. The clip is from the 2023 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting. Confidence is high: the questioner cites a national debt of about $31 trillion, Berkshire owns about 5.6% of Apple, Buffett mentions selling some Apple shares "a couple years ago" (2020), and Munger is still alive (he died in November 2023). The clip starts in the middle of a question, so its beginning is cut off.

---

## Part 1: Is Apple too large a share of Berkshire's portfolio?

*(The clip starts mid-question. From the context, the question was relayed on behalf of Professor Aswath Damodaran, probably read by the moderator. The first sentence appears to be the end of a quote the questioner was reading.)*

**Questioner:** "...One of the inane things that's taught in modern university education is that vast diversification is absolutely mandatory in investing in common stocks." Professor Damodaran mentioned that he is not comfortable with positions becoming a large part of his portfolio, for example when they reach 25 to 35%. He mentioned that Apple is now 35% of Berkshire's portfolio and thinks that is near a danger zone. He wonders if Warren and Charlie can comment.

**Warren Buffett:** I'd like to make one comment first, but Charlie will come up with...

**Charlie Munger:** I think he's out of his mind.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, I knew that was coming. But Apple is not 35% of Berkshire's portfolio. Berkshire's portfolio includes the railroad, the energy business, GEICO *[the transcript reads "G animals"; GEICO is the most likely reading]*, See's Candies, you name it. They're all businesses.

The good thing about Apple is that they buy in their stock. They've got about 15 billion 700-some million shares outstanding. They get down to 15 and a quarter billion, and without us doing anything, instead of owning 5.6%, we've got 6%. We can't own more than 100% of BNSF. We can't own more than 100% of GEICO or See's Candies. We'd love to own 200%, but that just doesn't work.

But they're all the same: they're good businesses. To think that our criteria for Apple are different from those for the other businesses we own is wrong. Apple just happens to be a better business than any we own. We put a fair amount of money in it, but we haven't got more money in it than we've got in the railroad. And Apple is a better business. Our railroad is a very good business, but it's not remotely as good as Apple's business.

Apple has a position with consumers where they pay maybe $1,500 or whatever it may be for a phone, and these same people pay $35,000 for a second car. If they had to give up their second car or give up their iPhone, they'd give up their second car. It's an extraordinary product. We don't have anything like that which we own 100% of. But we're very, very, very happy to have 5.6% or whatever it may be, and we're delighted with every tenth of a percent it goes up. It's like adding $100 million to our share of the earnings. They use the earnings to buy out our partners, and we're glad to see them sell out too. The index funds have to sell out. They bring the number of shares down.

We went up slightly last year. I made a mistake a couple of years ago and sold some shares, when I had certain reasons why gains were useful to take that year from a tax standpoint. It was a dumb decision. Charlie, you've already given your comment about it. But we do not have 35% of Berkshire's portfolio in Apple. Berkshire's portfolio is the funds we have to work with. We want to own good businesses, and we also want to have plenty of liquidity. Beyond that, the sky's the limit, or our mistakes, who knows. Charlie, do you want to add anything to your earlier comment?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, I think that is an insane idea. It's not that easy to have a vast plethora of good opportunities that are easily identified. If you've only got three, I'd rather be in my best ideas instead of my worst. Now, some people can't tell their best ideas from their worst. In the act of deciding that an investment is good, they get to think it's better than it is. I think we make fewer mistakes like that than other people, and that is a blessing to us.

We're not so smart, but we kind of know where the edge of our smartness is. That is a very important part of practical intelligence. A lot of people who are geniuses on IQ tests think they're a lot smarter than they are, and what they are is dangerous. But if you know the edge of your own ability pretty well, you should ignore most of the notions of our experts about what I call "diworsification" of portfolios.

## Part 2: Will the U.S. dollar stop being the world's reserve currency?

**Questioner:** The U.S. national debt is currently at an estimated $31 trillion, about 125% of U.S. GDP. Meanwhile, over the past few years, the Federal Reserve has telegraphed that it intends to monetize the debt by printing trillions of dollars, even as it insists that it is fighting inflation. Other major economies such as China, Saudi Arabia, and Brazil are moving away from the dollar in anticipation of this. My question is: are we likely to face a time in the future when the U.S. dollar is no longer the global reserve currency? How is Berkshire prepared for this possibility, and what can we do as American citizens to shelter ourselves from what's beginning to look like the beginnings of de-dollarization?

**Warren Buffett:** Well, I should ask you to come up here and answer some questions. It's very interesting. We are the reserve currency, and I see no option for any other currency to be the reserve currency. I think nobody understands the situation better than Jay Powell, but he's not in control of fiscal policy, and every now and then he drops a few hints.

There was no question that when the pandemic broke out, it was a semi-warlike situation. But nobody knows how far you can go with a paper currency before it gets out of control, particularly if you're the world's reserve currency. Nobody knows the answer to that, and you don't want to try to pick out the point where it does become a problem, because then it's all over. I think we should be very careful.

We all learned Keynesianism, and we applied it in World War II to the advantage of the country *[the transcript says "World War I", but the context is World War II]*. We did everything we could to prevent inflation during the war. Then the war ended in August of '45. I'm not giving you exact figures at all now, but in January '46 I think the rate of inflation was something like 1% or thereabouts, and by the end of the year I think it was like 15%. Again, I'm doing this from long memory.

America can do a lot. But if we do too much, it's very hard to see how you recover once you let the genie out of the bottle and people lose faith in the currency. They behave in an entirely different manner than when they feel that if they put some money in the bank or have a pension plan or whatever it may be, they're going to get out something with roughly equal purchasing power. It changes the economy, and all kinds of things can happen then. I can't predict them and nobody else can predict them, but I do know they aren't good. We will see.

I've voted for both parties, and this is not limited to politicians of either party or anything of the sort. People take positions. Some of them understand what they're doing, and some of them don't. If they put me on some medical board, I wouldn't understand what I'm doing. There's nothing wrong with the fact that you can't master everything. You can't all be Isaac Newton. But you can't go around pretending you do, or making decisions on it.

We are not as well off as we were earlier in relation to curbing inflation expectations, which become self-fulfilling. Berkshire is better prepared than most investments for that kind of a period. I said this in the annual report, but we aren't perfectly prepared, because there's no way to perfectly prepare. You don't know what course of action will occur. It's a very political decision now. It's a tribal decision to some degree. You hope for leadership that recognizes the problem and actually will do something. America is an incredible society. It's rich, and we've got everything going for us. But that doesn't mean we can just print money indefinitely. It'll be interesting to see how it turns out. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, at some point printing money to buy votes will be counterproductive.

**Warren Buffett:** Yep.

**Charlie Munger:** We don't know exactly where that comes. And if something is going to be dangerous and unproductive, you ought to keep it a fair distance away. Now, if you have a culture that is exceptionally strong, like Japan, they have done some strange things there.

**Warren Buffett:** But they couldn't have been the reserve currency.

**Charlie Munger:** No, of course not. But Japan bought back most of the national debt and a lot of the common stocks. The central bank owns practically everything in Japan, and the country is working. It's had 30 years of economic stasis, but it's not going to hell. I really admire Japan, but I don't think we should try to imitate it. I don't think we're as good as Japan at taking it. They have a cohesive culture and we don't.

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie, that's exactly right.

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah. In Japan, everybody's supposed to suck it up and cope, and in America we complain.

**Warren Buffett:** So I hope you come next year with a tougher question. Thank you. And I would love to be born again today in the United States. We can do a lot of dumb things and get away with it. We can't do an unlimited number. There are people who care about that, and you have to be willing to be extraordinarily unpopular. Paul Volcker did it. There were other Federal Reserve chairpeople who would not have done what he did. It's just too uncomfortable.

There used to be a politician in Nebraska, and if you asked him some really tough question, like how he stood on abortion, he would look you right in the eye and say, "I'm all right on that one," and then he'd move on. Well, that's what people have done basically on inflation. One way or another, they say "I'm all right on that," and then they don't really think about what the consequences of their actions could be. And it's so much more fun, when there are 435 of you, to just be one of 435 instead of being the person actually responsible.

**Charlie Munger:** *[The first words are unclear in the transcript.]* ...the question of two superpowers, and when you get into really destroying the planet, or destroying the reserve currency of the world, when there's really no substance... And forget about all the toys. It's a joke to think of any tokens of that sort. That's madness. But it's also madness to just keep printing money.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, and we know how to do it. We actually came from a money-printing economy in World War II, which was required, and we suffered significant inflation. There are a million ways to judge it, but the price level is maybe 10 times what it was then, or something like that. That's getting close to the edge where you don't want to hold dollars anymore. You want to hold something else. You want to hold real estate, or an interest in a business.

But your best defense is your own earning power. If you're the best doctor in town, the best lawyer in town, the best teacher in town, or even the 10th best, you're going to make a good living. The economy is productive, and you will succeed with your talents. You won't succeed by hoarding dollars. You'll succeed because your value to the community, which is a rich community overall, is sustained. So the best investment is always in yourself. That's the answer I would give you.

**Charlie Munger:** Well, we have a situation where we've learned to print money in gobs and let a big chunk of our young people go right into wealth management.

**Warren Buffett:** Like we did.

**Charlie Munger:** Yes, we've been bad examples.

**Warren Buffett:** I want to say that I didn't realize wealth management was going to get so big when I went into it, and I want to apologize for what's happened.

**Charlie Munger:** Well, anyway, you did well.
