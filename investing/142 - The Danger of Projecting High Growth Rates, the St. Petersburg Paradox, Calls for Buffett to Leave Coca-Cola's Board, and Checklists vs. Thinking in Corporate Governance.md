# The Danger of Projecting High Growth Rates, the St. Petersburg Paradox, Calls for Buffett to Leave Coca-Cola's Board, and Checklists vs. Thinking in Corporate Governance

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xVTLTC7r_Ws

Recorded: compilation of Berkshire annual meetings. Parts 3-5 (Coca-Cola board): 2004 (high confidence); Parts 1-2 (growth rates): around 2004-2005 (low-moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why It's Very Risky To Own Growth Stocks (In 2025)". It is misleading on two counts. Buffett does not say owning growth stocks is risky; he says it is dangerous to *project* very high growth rates far into the future in a valuation (a growth rate above the discount rate gives an infinite value). And nothing here is from 2025; Munger speaks in every part. More than half the clip is about something else entirely: activists who argued Buffett should leave Coca-Cola's board because of conflicts of interest, and why governance checklists are no substitute for thinking.
>
> Dating. Parts 3-5 are from the **2004** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence): in 2004, proxy adviser ISS and CalPERS recommended withholding votes from Buffett as a Coca-Cola director on conflict-of-interest grounds (Coke used Berkshire subsidiaries such as FlightSafety and Dairy Queen), and Berkshire's 200 million Coke shares being worth about $10 billion fits Coke's price then. Parts 1-2 are a separate question; the clues are the remark that Fortune is "commemorating" 50 years of the Fortune 500 (its 50th anniversary was marked around 2004-2005) and the suggestion to search Google, so around **2004-2005** (low-moderate confidence). Buffett places the St. Petersburg paradox paper "probably 30 years ago"; David Durand's paper is from 1957, so that is just his rough recollection.

## Part 1: The danger of projecting high growth rates ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xVTLTC7r_Ws&t=0))

*(The clip starts at the beginning of an answer; the question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** It gets very dangerous to project out high growth rates, because you get into this paradox. If you say the growth rate of a company is going to be 9% between now and judgment day, and you use a 7% discount rate, it goes off, you know, you get into infinity. And that's where people get in a lot of trouble. The idea of projecting out extremely high growth rates for very long periods of time has caused investors to lose very, very large sums of money.

There aren't many companies [...] Just take a look at the Fortune 500. Go back 50 years. They're commemorating that. And look at the companies that were there, and how many have really maintained rates much above 10%. It's not an easy hurdle. And when you get up to 15, you're in a rarified atmosphere.

So there's a real danger in projecting out high growth rates, and Charlie and I will very seldom, well, virtually never, get up into high digits. You can lose a lot of money doing that. You may miss an opportunity sometime, but I haven't seen people who have been consistently successful doing that. And you do run into this paradox you mentioned, Charlie.

**Charlie Munger:** Well, you're obviously right, when you get a mathematical result that is infinity, to back off and realize that can't happen. **Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* If you're using a present value discount formula, and you put in a growth rate that is higher than the discount rate, as you have postulated, the answer of course will be infinity. That precise subject was covered in a paper called the St. Petersburg Paradox by a fellow named Durand, probably 30 years ago. And somewhere we probably have a copy at our office. ## Part 3: Should Buffett leave Coca-Cola's board? Shareholders as owners ([02:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xVTLTC7r_Ws&t=174))

**Questioner:** I wondered if you could comment on the views of those people who have stated that, because of so-called conflicts of interest, you should leave the board of Coca-Cola, and whether you had any intention of doing so.

**Warren Buffett:** That we should do what with the board?

**Questioner:** Leave the board. That you personally should leave the board of Coke.

**Warren Buffett:** I would say that whoever suggested that should do 500 sit-ups. Actually, Charlie and I [...] certainly I have [...] I'll let Charlie speak for himself.

We like the idea, and we've encouraged the idea, of shareholders behaving like owners. I mean, shareholders have too often behaved like sheep in this country, and they got shorn in many cases. And big institutional shareholders have sat on the sidelines while some things that might possibly have been corrected, had they gotten active, took place. And so we actually applaud the idea of shareholders behaving like owners.

The question is whether they can behave like intelligent owners. And I think that in the last year or two, as they've sort of woken up, they've searched for checklists of one sort or another to determine whether directors are appropriate in a given company or not. And frankly, checklists are no substitute for thinking. The real job of the directors is to come up with the right CEO for a company and prevent him or her from overreaching. If they do that job well, the rest takes care of itself. And you have to think some to determine whether that's taking place. You can't solve it by just running down a little checklist.

I think it was Bertrand Russell who said, "Most men would rather die than think. Many do." And I think we've seen a little bit of what he was thinking about in some of the voting.

## Part 4: Proportionality: $10 billion of Coke stock vs. a little business with Coke ([05:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xVTLTC7r_Ws&t=316))

**Warren Buffett:** I think it's absolutely silly, frankly, if Berkshire Hathaway owns 200 million shares of Coca-Cola, 10 billion dollars' worth, to not be able [...] It's a little silly not to think that the interest that Berkshire Hathaway has in selling some hours of training at FlightSafety or something would cause me to do something counter to the interests of the shareholders, when we have $10 billion riding on that side of the table. I mean, it's almost absurd, and somebody doesn't understand proportionality at all when they come to that sort of conclusion.
