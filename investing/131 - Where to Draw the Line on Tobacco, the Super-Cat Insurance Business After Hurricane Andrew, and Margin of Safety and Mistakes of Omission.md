# Where to Draw the Line on Tobacco, the Super-Cat Insurance Business After Hurricane Andrew, and Margin of Safety and Mistakes of Omission

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_iR4gXyLsI

Recorded: Part 1 most likely late 1990s Berkshire annual meeting (low confidence); Part 2 most likely 1997 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence); Part 3 most likely mid-1990s Berkshire annual meeting (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Don't Invest In Tobacco Stocks". It is misleading: Buffett never says not to invest in tobacco stocks. He says Berkshire has owned tobacco stocks in the past, that "we may have made a mistake by not owning a lot of them", that tobacco is not on a boycott list, and that the one line he drew was not wanting to own a chewing tobacco manufacturer, a line he admits he cannot justify precisely. The tobacco answer is under four minutes; the rest of the clip is about the super-catastrophe reinsurance business after Hurricane Andrew and about margin of safety, repeated mistakes and mistakes of omission. All three answers are from Berkshire annual meetings (the "zone" microphones), but the clip may be a compilation, so each part is dated separately:
> - **Part 1 (tobacco):** most likely the late 1990s (low confidence). Costco is said to be the company "which used to be called Price Costco" (the PriceCostco name was dropped in the late 1990s), the RJR Nabisco bonds are "some years back", and UST is asked about as "beaten down lately". A guess, not from the captions: if the "hundred percent owned subsidiary" that is "part of the distribution chain" refers to McLane, the answer would be from 2003 or later.
> - **Part 2 (super-cat insurance):** most likely the **1997** meeting (moderate–high confidence). Buffett refers to what he "wrote in the report" about the California Earthquake Authority policy (written in 1996), says 20th Century added reserves for the Northridge quake "at the end of 1996", and the questioner is a recent shareholder.
> - **Part 3 (margin of safety and mistakes):** most likely the mid-1990s (low confidence). The questioner quotes the 1991 and 1992 letters, and Buffett mentions having met Bill Gates (which happened in 1991).

## Part 1: Tobacco stocks, and where to draw the moral line ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_iR4gXyLsI&t=0))

**Questioner:** My question deals with tobacco stocks, which have been beaten down lately. Does Berkshire own any tobacco stocks, and are some of these stocks attractive now that prices are down on some of them, and in particular a company called UST? Thank you.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, we have owned [...] we won't comment on what we own now, but we have owned tobacco stocks in the past. We've never owned a lot of them, although we may have made a mistake by not owning a lot of them. But we've owned tobacco stocks in the past, and I've had people write me about whether we should do it or not. We own a newspaper in Buffalo. It carries tobacco advertising. We don't [...]

Well, actually, Charlie's a director of the sensational warehouse chain called Costco, which used to be called Price Costco. You know, they sell cigarettes. So we are part of the distribution chain [unclear: "in with a hundred percent owned subsidy here"]; we are part of the distribution chain with a hundred percent [unclear: "on subsidiary"].

And so we have felt that I would invest in tobacco stocks. We made a decision some years ago that we didn't want to be in the manufacture of chewing tobacco. We were offered the chance to buy a company that has done sensationally well subsequently, and we sat in a hotel in Memphis, in the lobby, and talked about it, and finally decided we didn't want to do it. It wasn't because we thought it wouldn't do well. We knew it was going to do well.

But now, why would we take the ads for those companies, or why would we own a supermarket, for example, that sells them, or a 7-Eleven, you know, a convenience store that sells them, or something of the sort, and not want to manufacture them? I really can't give you the answer to that precisely, but I just know that the one bothers me and the other doesn't bother me, and I'm sure other people would draw the line in a different way.

So the fact that we have not been significant holders of tobacco stocks, it's not been because they've been on a boycotted list with us. It just means that overall we were uncomfortable enough about their prospects over time that we did not feel like making a big commitment in them. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, I think each company, each individual has to draw its own ethical and moral lines, and personally I like the messy complexity of having to do that. It makes life interesting.

**Warren Buffett:** I hadn't heard that before. Well, we'll make him in charge of this decision.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Oh, no.

**Warren Buffett:** But I don't think we can justify our call particularly. You have to draw the line somewhere between what we're willing to do and what we're not, and we draw [it] by our own lights.

We owned a lot of bonds at one time of RJR Nabisco, for example, [unclear: "meant"] some years back. And should we own the bonds, not the stock? Should we, you know, be willing to own the stock but not be willing to own the business? And those are tough calls. Probably the biggest distributor, the biggest seller of cigarettes in the United States is probably Walmart, but just because they're the biggest seller of everything. They're the biggest seller of Gillette products, and they're huge. And, you know, do I find that morally reprehensible? You know, I don't. If we owned all of Walmart, we'd be selling cigarettes at Walmart. But other people might call it differently, and I wouldn't disagree with them.

## Part 2: Did Hurricane Andrew destroy the super-cat business? ([03:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_iR4gXyLsI&t=235))

**Questioner:** I'm a recent stockholder of Berkshire. I'm sorry to say that, but it does not diminish my admiration for past performance or my confidence in future performance. I heard recently a remark by, I think, a very successful investor who I think worked with the Bass family in Texas for a while, and one of his comments, if I understand it correctly, said something like this: "Hurricane Andrew destroyed the super-cat industry." And that's about all he said. And I know we're into it. I'm interested in its importance to Berkshire and your comments on it, Mr. Buffett and Mr. Munger.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, I guess I would say I don't fully understand, or even partially understand, why he would say that. I mean, we are in business in the super-cat business. And I should explain: the super-cat business is very much like it sounds. I mean, we write insurance for other insurance companies, other reinsurance companies, to protect them, to pay them at a time when something really big comes along, a super catastrophe. And Hurricane Andrew was certainly a super catastrophe, but that's the reason people do business with us. So we pay off infrequently, but we pay off big. And we paid off about 120 million at Hurricane Andrew. But if Hurricane Andrew happened today, well, at least [on] one of the policies [unclear: "and steps"] we have, we would certainly be paying off at least, what, six, seven hundred million, something like that. And if it happens five years from now, we'll pay off a lot more than that, because we will undoubtedly be writing more business at that time.

So it's just part of the game. And there will be super-cats of various kinds. There will be, you know, huge quakes. There will be more hurricanes than huge quakes. And when that time comes, we will write a big check. But that doesn't [...] You know, prices may be firmer after such an event. They may not be. They didn't firm as much as you might expect after Andrew happened.

Andrew was a huge surprise to people. As a digression, you know, people in the insurance business thought that they all had these models, and some of them were prepared by reinsurance brokers and some of them by various research institutes, [as to] how much they would lose under certain kinds of circumstances, and they couldn't have been further off with Andrew or with the Northridge earthquake. And fortunately we don't rely on those. I don't know what exactly we do rely on, but we don't rely on those.

And Hurricane Andrew was, you know [...] that's part of business with Berkshire, and we will have another one, and we'll have another one after that. So every three or five or seven years, or who knows what, we will lose significant money in the super-cat business, and we expect that over 20 or 25 years we will make more money than we lose. We bring some real advantages to that business, as I wrote about in the report, and it makes sense for us to be in it. It only makes sense for us to be in it when the premium prices are right, but when they are appropriate, we're more than willing to step up and take on a fair amount of risk.

As I wrote in the report on the California Earthquake Authority, [we could] [unclear: "awaken tomorrow"] face a demand for roughly a billion dollars, and we are prepared to write a check that day to take care of that, and [unclear: "we will run it if it happens"]. And there aren't very many people in the world that an insured can count on to do that.

The interesting thing is that the worst exposure still for super-cats are not borne by us, but they're borne implicitly by some very big direct writing companies that have lots of risk on Long Island or along the New Madrid Fault or other places. And they have got, well, millions of policies, and maybe hundreds of thousands of exposed policies, and they don't think of themselves as being in the super-cat business. Or they really do, but they don't think, you know, day by day about it, and they are very exposed. Our exposures are limited to a given dollar amount. That dollar amount may be large, but at least we know what it is. And we're willing to take risks when we think we're appropriately paid. There's a mentality to bring to the super-cat business that's somewhat akin to what you bring to the investment business, so we think we're well equipped for [it]. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, the billion dollars would be, what, two and a half percent or less of the liquid assets and securities around Berkshire. And so that's irritating, but it's not going to destroy the enterprise. Whereas if you have an unwitting super-cat exposure that you don't even recognize you have, it could destroy your company. 20th Century, a very well-run [unclear: "director of"; likely "direct writer of"] insurance, they all but went broke with the Northridge earthquake, and they didn't think it was possible either, and they had no idea they had a super-cat exposure in what they thought was a simple little direct writing insurance operation. Now, I don't think we've got the main super-cat risks at all at Berkshire.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* GEICO lost something like 150 million in Andrew, and their initial estimate of the loss was like 35 million dollars, and that's after they thought they'd heard about most of it. You can really get fooled in this business. In fact, 20th Century, which lost the billion dollars at Northridge, just at the end of 1996, I think, added 40 million dollars, as I remember, to the reserves for the Northridge quake, which I think was in January of '94. Now, you'd think on an earthquake you would, you know, you'd kind of know when it was over, but you can really get fooled.

And down at Andrew, I mean, the cost of construction go up dramatically in an area that's been wiped out. And then there were all kinds of things in the codes. I mean, I think they started requiring architects' drawings on everything, you know, over five thousand dollars in the way of repair, some number like that. [unclear: "I don't owe me the number"]. And of course the architects had a field day. And then it turned out that everybody had a homeowner's policy [...] in the Oakland fire, for example, they all had a three hundred thousand dollar book collection in their library, and, you know, who knows after the place is burned down. You get a lot of surprises in that field, and the surprises in insurance are never symmetrical. They're all bad.

## Part 3: Repeated mistakes, margin of safety, and mistakes of omission ([11:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_iR4gXyLsI&t=682))

**Warren Buffett:** I think we were told by some higher authority which one was more important there for a second. Ah, well, they're bound together. Obviously, if you understood a business perfectly, the future of a business, you would need very little in the way of a margin of safety. So the more volatile the [unclear: "businesses are possibility is"], but assuming you still want to invest in it, the larger the margin of safety. I think in that first edition of [...] if I remember, it was J.I. [unclear: "Casey used"], and said, you know, maybe it was worth somewhere from between 30 and 110, or some number. He said, well, you know, that sounds perfect: how much good does that do you, to know that it's worth between 30 and 110? Well, if you're driving a truck across a bridge that says it holds ten thousand pounds and you've got a 9,800 pound vehicle, you know, if the bridge is about six inches above the crevice that it covers, you may feel okay. But if it's, you know, over the Grand Canyon, you may feel you want a little larger margin of safety, in terms of only driving a four thousand pound truck or something across. **Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* Yeah, you've got to be willing to take a really big bite. And it's crazy if you don't, and it's crazy if you dabble around at the edges so you're not prepared to take a big bite when the time comes.
