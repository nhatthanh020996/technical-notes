# Buffett & Munger on Spotting Unprecedented Risks, Avoiding Leverage, and Making Fast Investment Decisions

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=knuRt9U2qjA

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: How To Avoid Black Swans". It is somewhat misleading: the term "black swan" is never used, and there is no method for avoiding them. What is actually said: Buffett reads the criteria for a future Berkshire investment manager from the 2006 annual report (someone "genetically programmed to recognize and avoid serious risks, including those never before experienced"), says the banks' risk models failed, and explains why Berkshire runs with little leverage; Munger mocks "chief risk officers" and model worship. The second half (about 40% of the clip) is a different question, about how fast Berkshire can decide on an investment, which the title does not cover. The questioner's second question (about investing in South Korea or China) is not answered in the clip. The clip is most likely from the **2009** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence; 2008 is possible). The clues: Buffett says his 2006 warning "proved to be somewhat prophetic of what happened last year", with the major banks and investment banks not understanding their risk, which fits the 2008 financial crisis best; he still talks about "the four people we're thinking about" as investment successors, as in the 2006–2009 period; and Berkshire's credit and liquidity are discussed in post-crisis terms.

## Part 1: Needing someone who can recognize risks never experienced before ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=knuRt9U2qjA&t=0))

*(The clip starts in the middle of an answer; the question is not included. From the context, it is about the criteria for Berkshire's future investment manager(s).)*

**Warren Buffett:** There was one other item in here, I believe, which I think achieved a little added relevance in the last year. I said, "We therefore need someone genetically programmed to recognize and avoid serious risks," and then I put in italics, "including those never before experienced." And I said, "Certain perils that lurk in investment strategies cannot be spotted by use of the models commonly employed today by financial institutions."

Well, I think that proved to be somewhat prophetic of what happened last year. All of these places had models. I mean the major banks, the major investment banks, and they would meet weekly at a risk committee, probably, and go over their models, and all of the statistics would be printed in nice columns and everything, and they didn't have the faintest idea what risk they were involved [in].

You really need in the investment world someone very solid, someone you trust, reasonable analytical skills, but you also need someone that actually can contemplate problems that haven't popped up yet but which are starting to become possibilities, in terms of new financial instruments or new behaviors in markets and that sort of thing. And that's a rare quality. I mean, that inability to envision something that doesn't show up in your past model can be fatal.

And Charlie and I spend a lot of time thinking about things that could hit us out of the blue that other people don't include in their thinking. We may miss some opportunities because of that, but we feel it's essential when managing other people's money, or for that matter managing our own money.

So I would say that you might go back and read the 2006 annual report again, but those are the criteria we're looking for, and [that] we have identified as being met by the four people we're thinking about. Charlie?
