# Buffett & Munger on Owning a Few Wonderful Businesses, Modern Portfolio Theory, and Avoiding Industries Driven by Change

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z85iiX2QWqU

Recorded: mid-to-late 1990s, most likely 1996-1998, Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting (low-to-moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger Explains Why You Only Need Three Stocks To Get Rich". It is misleading in two ways. Most of the "three wonderful businesses" argument comes from Buffett, not Munger, and neither of them says three stocks will make you rich. Buffett says that three wonderful businesses you understand beat a diversified group of 50. The clip is from a Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting in the mid-to-late 1990s. Gillette and Coca-Cola are core holdings, Bill Gates and Microsoft come up as a technology example, and the questioner asks about Pfizer and J&J. The exact year cannot be pinned down from the clip. 1996–1998 is most likely (low-to-moderate confidence). The clip seems to be edited together from more than one exchange, and it opens with a short teaser line repeated from later in the video.

## Part 1: Three wonderful businesses beat fifty average ones

*(The clip opens with a teaser: "What is taught in modern corporate finance courses is twaddle. Do you want to elaborate on that, Charlie?" It then starts mid-answer, so the original question is not in the video.)*

**Warren Buffett:** Three wonderful businesses is more than you need in this life to do very well. And the average person isn't going to run into that. If you look at how the fortunes were built in this country, they weren't built out of a portfolio of 50 companies. They were built by someone who identified a wonderful business. Coca-Cola is a great example. A lot of fortunes have been built on that. And there aren't 50 Coca-Colas. There aren't 20. If there were, it'd be fine. We could all go out and diversify like crazy among that group and get results equal to owning the really wonderful one. But you're not going to find it.

And the truth is you don't need it. If you have a really wonderful business, it is very well protected against the vicissitudes of the economy over time, and against competition. We're talking about businesses that are resistant to effective competition. And three of those will be better than a hundred average businesses. And they'll be safer, incidentally. There is less risk in owning three easy-to-identify wonderful businesses than there is in owning 50 well-known big businesses. It's amazing what has been taught over the years in finance classes about that. But I can assure you that if I had to bet the fortunes of my family for the next 30 years on the income from a given group of businesses, I would rather pick three businesses from those we own than own a diversified group of 50.

Charlie?

## Part 2: Modern portfolio theory as "twaddle"

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah. What he's saying is that what is taught in modern corporate finance courses is twaddle.

**Warren Buffett:** You cannot believe this stuff. It's modern portfolio theory, and it has no utility. It will tell you how to do average, but I think anybody can figure out how to do average in fifth grade. It's just not that difficult. And it's elaborate, and there are lots of little Greek letters and all kinds of things that make you feel that you're in the big leagues, but there is no value added.

**Charlie Munger:** I have great difficulty with it because I am something of a student of dementia. We hang around a lot together. I can ordinarily classify dementia on some theory structure of models, but modern portfolio theory involves a type of dementia I just can't even classify. Something very strange is going on.

**Warren Buffett:** If you find three wonderful businesses in your life, you'll get very rich. And if you understand them, bad things aren't going to happen to those three. That's the characteristic of it.

**Charlie Munger:** By the way, maybe that's the reason there's so much dementia. If you believe what Warren said, you could teach the whole course in about a week.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah.

**Charlie Munger:** And the high priests wouldn't have any edge over the lay people. And that never sells well.

## Part 3: Why not Microsoft, Pfizer, or Johnson & Johnson?

*(The question is cut in mid-sentence.)*

**Questioner:** ...But I'm thinking about not only Microsoft but, say, Pfizer and J&J. All three companies have already proven that they have a great product, proven management over 10 to 15 years, and great market share positions that are not easy to get into. And frankly, I don't see a big difference in the P/E ratios if you compare, say, Coca-Cola with Johnson & Johnson or Pfizer, which are very powerful companies. I wonder if either or both of you would address that question again.

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie, why don't you...

**Charlie Munger:** If you have something you think you understand that looks very attractive to you... We think it's smart to do what you understand. If we'd been unable to find companies suited to our slender talents, we might well have been into it. We don't sneer at it. Other people with more talent have found that a wonderful course of action.

**Warren Buffett:** We generally look for businesses where we feel change is not likely to work against us. We do not think we have great ability to predict where change is going to lead. We think we have some ability to find businesses where we don't think change is going to be very important.

Now, at Gillette, the product is going to be better 10 years from now than now, and better 20 years from now than 10 years from now. You saw those earlier ads going back to the Blue Blade and all that. The Blue Blade seemed great at the time, but the shaving technology keeps getting better and better. Gillette had that little experience with Wilkinson in the early '60s, but you know that Gillette is basically going to be spending many multiples of the money anyone else spends on developing better shaving systems than exist now. They've got the distribution system. They've got the believability. If they bring out a product for women in the shaving field and say, "This is something," they have credibility. They wouldn't have that same credibility someplace else, but in the shaving field, they have it. Those are assets that can't be built, and they're very hard to destroy.

So we think we know in a general way what the soft drink industry or the shaving industry or the candy business is going to look like 10 or 20 years from now. We think Microsoft is a sensational company run by the best of managers, but we don't have any idea what that world is going to look like in 10 or 20 years. If I were going to bet on somebody to go out and do what we can't do ourselves, I'd rather bet on Bill Gates than anybody else. But I don't want to bet on anybody else. In the end, we want to understand for ourselves where we think a business is going.

When somebody on Wall Street tells us a business is going to change a lot, they love to tell you that's a great opportunity. They don't think it's a great opportunity when Wall Street itself is going to change a lot, incidentally. But they say it's a great opportunity. We don't think it's an opportunity at all. It scares the hell out of us, because we don't know how things are going to change.

When people are chewing gum, we have a pretty good idea how they chewed it 20 years ago and how they'll chew it 20 years from now. We don't really see a lot of technology going into the art of the chew. As long as we don't have to make those other decisions, why in the world should we? There are all kinds of things we don't know, so why go around trying to bet on things we don't know when we can bet on the simple things?

## Part 4: Emerging markets through Coca-Cola and Gillette

*(The speaker attribution in this part is a best guess.)*

**Charlie Munger:** We are indirectly in all of these emerging markets through Coca-Cola and Gillette. So it isn't true that we're totally absent.

**Warren Buffett:** No. At Coca-Cola, the international markets are 80% of profits, actually a little more. At Gillette, I think they're about 70% or so. We love the international aspects of the Coca-Cola and Gillette businesses, and that's a very major attraction. And the management of those companies is focused on that. They have distribution systems, they have recognition, and they've got a lot going for them over there. The beauty of it is that they're maximizing what they do have going for them, which was not the case 20 years ago. Back then they just let it go more by default, and they started fooling around with a lot of diversification, and basically that has not worked that well. So we like focus. We love focus.

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah. And by doing it indirectly, as we've done, one can argue that we thereby do it a lot better.

**Warren Buffett:** We won't explore the implications of that.
