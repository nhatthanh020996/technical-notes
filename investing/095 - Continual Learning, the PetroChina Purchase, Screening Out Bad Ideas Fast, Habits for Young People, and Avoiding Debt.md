# Continual Learning, the PetroChina Purchase, Screening Out Bad Ideas Fast, Habits for Young People, and Avoiding Debt

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9wUWVtpKMjU

Recorded: most likely 2008 Berkshire annual meeting, possibly 2007 (moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: These Simple Habits Made Me Very Rich". It is misleading: Buffett never describes habits that made him rich. Asked which habits helped him keep learning, he says he hasn't learned any new fundamental principles since Graham (with Munger and Phil Fisher's influence); Munger pushes back that Buffett has kept learning. The "habits" part is a separate answer to a 14-year-old about life and financial concepts for young people: form good habits early, avoid debt, avoid dumb risks, and associate with people better than you. The clip is most likely from the **2008** Berkshire annual meeting, possibly **2007** (moderate confidence). The clues: Munger speaks of PetroChina as one of Buffett's "recent investment decisions" and the block of stock is discussed in the past tense (Berkshire bought it in 2002–2003 and sold it in 2007); Buffett says the New Yorker chess article from the time of the Fischer–Spassky matches (1972) was "30 years ago or so, a little more than that".

## Part 1: Buffett: no new fundamental principles since Graham ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9wUWVtpKMjU&t=0))

**Questioner:** What habit or habits have contributed most to your ability to continue learning and improving your investment decisions in a changing business and financial environment?

**Warren Buffett:** I would say that, at least in my case, I haven't been continually learning in terms of the basic principles. You always learn a little more about given techniques, or I learn more about some industries over time, and therefore maybe I've widened the universe in which I can operate, although more funds narrows it back down, unfortunately. But I know more about businesses than I knew 20 years ago or 40 years ago. I haven't really changed the principles. The basic principles are still Ben Graham. They were affected in a significant way by Charlie and Phil Fisher in terms of looking at the better businesses, but I didn't leave Graham behind on that. And I really haven't learned any new fundamental principles, but I may have learned a little bit more about how business operates over time.

And there's really nothing [...] I mean, you ought to get an investment framework that comes straight, in my view, from The Intelligent Investor and from Phil Fisher, more from The Intelligent Investor actually. And then I think you ought to learn everything you can about industries and businesses where you think you have the ability to get your mind around them if you work at them. And with that arsenal you'll do very well, if you've got the temperament for the business. Charlie?

## Part 4: Tuning out folly: hanging up fast, and how chess masters prune possibilities ([03:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9wUWVtpKMjU&t=238))

**Charlie Munger:** No, I think that takes a certain amount of what an old Omaha friend used to call uncommon sense. He used to say there is no common sense; when people say common sense, they mean uncommon sense.

Part of it, I think, is being able to tune out folly, as distinguished from recognizing wisdom. And if you've just got whole categories of things you just bat away, so your brain isn't cluttered with them, then you're better able to pick up a few sensible things to do.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, we don't consider many stupid things. I'm gonna get rid of them fast, and in fact people get irritated with us, because they'll call us, and when they're in the middle of the first sentence we'll just tell them "forget it." We can see it coming.

And that's the way, actually, the mind was. There was a great article in the New Yorker magazine 30 years ago or so, a little more than that, when the Fischer–Spassky chess matches were going on, and it got into the speculation of humans being able to take on computers in chess. And here with these computers doing hundreds of thousands of calculations a second, they said, how can the human mind, when all you're really looking at is the future, the results from various moves in the future, how can a human mind deal with a computer that's thinking at speeds that are unbelievable? And of course [unclear: "the exam on the subject some and a mind like"], well, in fact all minds, but some much better than others [...] but Fischer–Spassky essentially was eliminating about 99.99 of the possibilities without even thinking about it. So it wasn't that they could out-think the computer in terms of speed, but they had disability in what you might call grouping or exclusion, where essentially they just got right down to the few possibilities, out of these zillions of possibilities, that really had any chance of success.

And getting rid of the nonsense, I mean, just figuring that people start calling you and saying, "I've got this great, wonderful idea." Don't spend 10 minutes. Once you know in the first sentence that it isn't a great, wonderful idea, don't be polite and go through the whole process. And Charlie and I are pretty good at that. We can hang up very fast, right?

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* All you've got to do is go at it in the way that Vasily Smyslov did when he was the world champion of chess, and just do the same thing in investments.

## Part 6: Don't get into debt, and associate with people better than you ([08:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9wUWVtpKMjU&t=533))

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, we would say, even though we issue lots of credit cards, I think we'd say, probably, if I had one piece of advice to give to young people across the board, it would be just don't get in debt. The game plays a lot easier if you're a little bit ahead of the game than if you're behind the game. And Ben Franklin said that long ago in better terms, which Charlie can recite. But there's a real difference.

I get letters every day from people that are in all kinds of financial trouble, and often it's health-related, which is tragic, but very often it relates to debt. I mean, they get behind the game and they're never going to catch up. And often, it may surprise you, but often, yeah, I write these people. They're very decent people; they've just made mistakes. And I just tell them the best course is bankruptcy. I mean, they are not going to catch up, and they should start all over again, and they should never look at a credit card the rest of their life. But it would have been better if they'd gotten that advice a little earlier. But it's very tempting to spend more than you earn. I mean, it's very understandable, but it's not a good idea.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* And of course you particularly want to avoid evil or seriously irrational people, particularly if they are attractive members of the opposite sex. [unclear: "That can"] [...]

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* Charlie knows more about this. The expert. [unclear: "They"] [...] Yeah, it's better to hang out with people better than you. I find that very easy to do over the years. But yeah, if you're picking associates, pick out those whose behavior is somewhat better [unclear: "the years"], and you'll drift in that direction. And similarly, if you hang out with a bad bunch, you're very likely to find your own behavior worse over time.

But, like Charlie says, the [unclear: "Trident"] advice which Ben Franklin was handing out a few hundred years ago really works. We've said it, but look at the people you like to associate with.
