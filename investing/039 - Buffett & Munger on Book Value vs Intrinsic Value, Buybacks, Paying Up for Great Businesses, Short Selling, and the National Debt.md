# Buffett & Munger on Book Value vs Intrinsic Value, Buybacks, Paying Up for Great Businesses, Short Selling, and the National Debt

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LAZNpEyOCIw

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why Book Value Is Meaningless". It is misleading. Buffett says book value has virtually no correlation with intrinsic value across companies, but he also says it has "reasonable tracking utility" at Berkshire, and Berkshire's buyback threshold is set as a percentage of book value. Book value takes up only the first two and a half minutes. The rest of the clip covers buybacks, paying up for wonderful businesses (See's Candy), stock-market vs. negotiated purchases, lifelong learning, whether Munger would move to Omaha, short selling, the 2008 crisis response, and the national debt.
>
> The clip is from a Berkshire annual meeting ("as I said earlier on this weekend"), most likely **2013** (low–moderate confidence), and certainly between 2013 and 2018. The clues: the buyback threshold of 120% of book value was set in December 2012 and dropped in 2018. Buffett mentions his New York Times op-ed on the debt "in 2009 or 2010" (likely "The Greenback Effect," August 2009) as something from a few years back. The flash-crash reference points after 2010. The debt-to-GDP and "if you aren't confused, you don't understand it" discussion fits the 2013 fiscal-cliff and sequester period. The clip is a string of separate Q&A segments with the questions mostly cut. They may not all come from the same year.

> Notation: `[word]` = added by editor for readability; `[...]` = speaker trails off; `[unclear: "..."]` = garbled in the captions, kept verbatim; `[speaker uncertain]` = attribution inferred.

## Part 1: Book value vs. intrinsic value, and the 120%-of-book buyback ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LAZNpEyOCIw&t=0))

*(The clip starts mid-answer; the question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** [...] generally speaking, book value's got nothing to do with the price at which you should repurchase your shares. Intrinsic business value does. And the correlation between intrinsic business value and book value throughout the investment universe is [...] there's virtually no correlation. So book value is unimportant in most companies. It actually has reasonable tracking utility at Berkshire.

Our intrinsic value is very considerably above book value, and we have signaled that. We'll say it right here, we said it before, but in addition we've signaled that by saying that we would repurchase our shares, as long as we had a substantial cash balance [and] met all the needs of our operating companies, at 120% of book value. And if we got the opportunity to buy it there, we would probably buy a whole lot of it.

The calculus is very much what I put in the report. You take care of your business with money first. And if you can buy additional businesses, it's something where you add to the per-share value of the business; you do that. If you can repurchase your shares at a significant discount from intrinsic value, it's like buying dollar bills at 90 cents or 80 cents or whatever it may be, and it's a very sure way of improving per-share value.

It's been very difficult for us to do it, because every time we announce it, people say, "Well, if he thinks it's worth more than 120% of book, if those cheapskates are willing to pay that [...]" Yeah, right. "Well, if at least one cheapskate is willing to pay that," they [...] And you know, they're right.

And we've got mixed emotions on it. We don't really like the idea of running a company where it makes most of its money by buying its partners out at a discount. But if partners want to sell out at a discount, we also like the idea of buying and making sure money that way. We haven't done much of it. Most of the time our stock has sold in a reasonable range in relation to intrinsic business value, we would think.

## Part 2: When Berkshire buys back stock, and paying up for wonderful businesses ([02:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LAZNpEyOCIw&t=158))

**Warren Buffett:** [We would think] that probably a fairly significant percentage of the time in recent years [it] sold at least some discount. There were a few years when we thought it sold for more than intrinsic business value. **Warren Buffett:** We usually feel we're paying too much, isn't that right, Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* It [...]

**Warren Buffett:** But we find the business so compelling, the management or associates so compelling, that we gag and we get there on the price. But there is no perfect mathematical formula. Looking back, when we bought wonderful businesses that turned out to continue to be wonderful, we could have paid significantly more money, and they still would have been great business decisions. Generally speaking, if you get a chance to buy a wonderful business, and by that I mean one that has economic characteristics that lead you to believe with a high degree of certainty that they will be earning unusual returns on capital over time (unusually high), and better yet, if they get the chance to employ more capital at, again, high rates of return, that's the best of all businesses, and you probably should stretch a little.

Charlie and I have had several conversations while we were looking at a business which we liked, and we're sort of gagging at the price, and Charlie or I will say, "You know, let's do it," even though it kind of kills us to pay that last 5%. ## Part 3: Why the stock market offers opportunities that negotiated purchases don't ([05:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LAZNpEyOCIw&t=328))

**Charlie Munger:** Modern prices are not cheap.

**Warren Buffett:** No. Yeah. And great businesses, you know, you're not going to find lots of them, and you're not going to get the opportunity to buy them. And although you do in the market [...] The stock market will offer you opportunities for profit, percentage-wise, that you'll never see in terms of negotiated purchase of business. In negotiated purchase of a business, you're almost always dealing with someone that has the option of either selling or not selling, and can sort of pick the time when they decide to sell, and all that sort of thing.

In stock markets, it's an auction market. Crazy things can happen. You can have some technological blip that will cause a flash crash or something, and the world really hasn't changed at all, but all kinds of selling mechanisms are tripped off and that sort of thing. So you will see opportunities in the stock market that you'll never really get in the business market.

But what we really like [...] we really like buying businesses to hold and keep. We like buying cheap marketable securities too, but particularly when you've got lots of cash coming in, and you're going to continue to have lots of cash coming in, you really want to deploy it in great businesses that you can own forever. Charlie, anything?

So I'm not [...] I am disturbed by a national debt that grows in respect to GDP. In fact, I wrote an article in the New York Times, an op-ed piece, in 200[...] I think maybe 2009 or 2010, talking about this very problem. But you know, we came out of World War II with a debt higher, a gross [unclear: "oret"] debt higher in relation to GDP than we have now, and people were predicting terrible things at that time because of that situation, and the country has done sensationally.
