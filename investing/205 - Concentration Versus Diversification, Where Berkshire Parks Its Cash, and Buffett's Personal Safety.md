# Concentration Versus Diversification, Where Berkshire Parks Its Cash, and Buffett's Personal Safety

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gKH5sQXpVCk

Recorded: Parts 1–6 and 8 most likely 1996 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence); Part 7 Berkshire annual meeting, year uncertain, possibly the same meeting (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why Diversification Is Bullsh*t". It exaggerates: Buffett never uses that word. What he says is that diversification "as practice generally makes very little sense for anyone that knows what they're doing", that it "is a protection against ignorance", and that owning everything "is a perfectly sound approach for somebody who does not feel they know how to analyze businesses". Munger calls much of modern corporate finance "twaddle". The diversification answer also covers only about half of the clip; the rest is about where Berkshire keeps its short-term cash (the Penn Central commercial paper story), a shareholder's worry about Buffett's and Munger's personal safety, Munger on mental models, and Berkshire's assumption that it will be around forever. The clip is a compilation from a Berkshire annual meeting, most likely the **1996** meeting (moderate–high confidence). The clues: a first-time shareholder raises "the B shares" (Berkshire issued its Class B shares in May 1996) together with Buffett's growing visibility; Buffett says only positions "above 600 million" were listed "in the last report"; Coca-Cola and Gillette are mentioned as current holdings; and the numbered microphone zones fit 1990s meetings. The opening 37 seconds (Part 1) are a repeat of a passage from Part 3 used as a teaser. Part 7 starts in the middle of a Munger answer about mental models and cannot be dated independently.

## Part 5: Never reaching for an extra eighth: the Penn Central lesson ([09:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gKH5sQXpVCk&t=555))

**Warren Buffett:** Right now we have maybe a billion and something [unclear: "ensure"] in relatively short-term Treasuries, and we have a little extra in some commercial paper, maybe. But you will never see us reaching for an extra eighth of a percent on short-term [unclear: "meals"].

Some of you may remember the fiasco in Penn Central in the commercial paper market. Penn Central, around 1970 or thereabouts, was paying a quarter of a point, as I remember, more than other commercial paper issuers. And of course, one day, despite showing a positive net worth, I think, of a billion and a half or so, they said they had a lot of net worth but no cash. Turned out cash was more important, and so they defaulted.

Now, the interesting thing about doing that is, if you're getting a quarter of a point extra, and you came over on the Mayflower and you landed and you said, "Well, I'm going to apply myself to getting a quarter of a point extra on short-term money," and you didn't make any mistake until you got to Penn Central, you would [unclear: "sign"], from the compounding aspect, you would be behind at that point. And I don't like a business that you can do right for 300 years and then make one mistake and be behind.

So we are very picky about short-term paper. But it is all invested, and when it's large amounts, it probably will be in Treasuries. A couple firms' commercial paper we take.
