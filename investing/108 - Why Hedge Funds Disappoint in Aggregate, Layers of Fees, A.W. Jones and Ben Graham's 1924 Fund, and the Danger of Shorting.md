## Part 4: A.W. Jones and Ben Graham's 1924 fund ([04:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QOTep1wr7Yk&t=240))

**Warren Buffett:** Many people think of A.W. Jones, who was a Fortune writer at one time and who developed the best-known hedge fund, whenever it was, in the early '60s or thereabouts, maybe the late '50s even. For some of the audience: the idea originally with A.W. Jones is that they would go long and short more or less equal amounts and have a market-neutral fund, so that it didn't make any difference which way the market went.

They didn't really stick with that over time, and I'm not even sure whether A.W. Jones said that they would. Sometimes they'd be 140% long and 80 short, so they'd have a 60 net long or whatever it might be. They were not market neutral throughout the period, but they did operate on the theory of being long stocks that seemed underpriced and short stocks that were overpriced. Even the Federal Reserve, in a report they made on the Long-Term Capital Management situation a few years ago, credited A.W. Jones as being sort of the father of this theory of hedge funds.

As Mickey Newman, if he's still here, knows, I think it was in 1924 that Ben Graham set up the Benjamin Graham fund, which was designed exactly along those lines and which even used paired securities. In other words, he would look at General Motors and Chrysler and decide what [unclear: "she"; context suggests "he"] thought was undervalued relative to the other, and go long one and short the other. And he was paid a percentage of the profits, and it had all [unclear: "pays"] hedge funds, except it was started in 1924. And I don't know that Ben was the first on that, but I know that he was 30 years ahead of the one that the Federal Reserve credited with being the first, and that many people still talk about as being the first, A.W. Jones.

## Part 5: Why shorting is so hard: Graham's paired trades and Buffett's 1954 experience ([06:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QOTep1wr7Yk&t=383))

**Warren Buffett:** Ben did not find that particularly successful, and he even wrote about it some, in terms of the problems he encountered with that approach. My memory is that a quite high percentage of the paired investments worked out well. He was right: the undervalued one went up and the overvalued one, or the spread between the two, narrowed. But the one time out of four, or whatever it was, that he was wrong lost a lot more money than the average of the three that he was right on.

All I can say is that I [unclear: "I'm short of stocks"; context suggests "I've been short stocks"] in my life, and I had one particularly harrowing experience in 1954. I can hardly think of a situation where I was wrong if viewed from 10 years later, but I can think of some where I was certainly wrong from the view of 10 weeks later, which happened to be the relevant period, and during which my net worth was evaporating and my liquid assets were getting less liquid and so on. So all I can tell you is [it's] very difficult.
