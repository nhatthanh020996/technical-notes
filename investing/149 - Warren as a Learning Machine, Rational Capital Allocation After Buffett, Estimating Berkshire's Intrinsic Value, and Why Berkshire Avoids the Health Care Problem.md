# Warren as a Learning Machine, Rational Capital Allocation After Buffett, Estimating Berkshire's Intrinsic Value, and Why Berkshire Avoids the Health Care Problem

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCD8jo_nYHo

Recorded: compilation of Berkshire annual meeting clips. Part 1, intrinsic-value segment: most likely 2007 (low-moderate confidence); Part 1, culture and capital-allocation segment: probably 2006-2008 (low confidence); Part 1, Munger's learning-machine segment: after 1995, otherwise undated; Part 2: probably 2003-2007 (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger: Daily Habit That Can 10x Your Investing Returns". It is misleading: no daily habit is named and nothing is said about multiplying anyone's returns. Munger says Buffett's record comes from being "a learning machine" who kept improving even past retirement age, and that the record "would be a pale shadow of what it is" otherwise, and that Berkshire's long concentration of power in one learner "ought to be more copied". The rest of the clip is Buffett: Berkshire's culture of rationality and the kind of capital allocator it needs after him; how to estimate Berkshire's intrinsic value (marketable securities, operating businesses, and how well retained earnings will be used, with the 1965 textile example); and a surgeon's question on whether Berkshire could help fix health care (Buffett and Munger say they look for easy problems and cannot solve that one). The clip is a **compilation** of at least three, probably four, answers; the cuts fall inside the first uploader chapter. Clues: Munger says Warren "has improved since he passed the retirement age of man", so after 1995. Buffett says Berkshire will need someone in capital allocation who "doesn't do any dumb things" and "we're on that road already", which fits the search for an investment successor announced in the 2006 letter. The "roughly $80,000 in marketable securities" (per share, from the annual report) fits the end-2006 figure, so most likely the **2007** meeting. In Part 2, health costs are "close to 15% of GDP" and there is an "upcoming political campaign", which suggests a meeting in a year before a presidential election, probably 2003-2007. These dates are estimates only.

## Part 1: The learning machine, Berkshire's culture after Buffett, and how to estimate intrinsic value ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCD8jo_nYHo&t=0))

*(The clip starts with Munger's remarks; the question, if any, is not included.)*

**Charlie Munger:** What on earth has caused this extreme record to go on for such a very long time? I would argue that the young man who was reading everything he could read when he was 10 years [unclear: "of"] old became a learning machine, and he got a lot of power early, and then he got a very long run when he kept learning. If Warren had not been learning all the while, I'm telling you, having watched the process closely, the record would be a pale shadow of what it is. And Warren has improved since he passed the retirement age of man. In other words, in this field at least, you can improve when you're old.

Now, most people don't even try and create that kind of a record. They pass power from one 65-year-old to [unclear: "159y old"] and then do it over and over again. But you get an enormous advantage from practice in this field. And so what happened accidentally in the case of Warren has helped you shareholders greatly, because you had this long run with power extremely concentrated, and with the man holding the power being a ferocious learner. Our system ought to be more copied than it is. This idea of passing the power from one old codger to another in a settled way is not necessarily the right system at all.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* We have a very strong culture now of rationality, of being owner oriented, that will go on long after I'm not around. And we have the talent on the operating side in place to do a lot of wonderful things over time. We will need, in capital allocation, to keep doing intelligent things. We won't get to do brilliant things, because you don't get to do brilliant things with the kind of sums we're talking about. Maybe once in a blue moon something, you know, you get a chance. But we will need somebody that never does a [...] basically doesn't do any dumb things, and occasionally does something that's reasonably good. That can be done. And we're on that road already.

It is not [...] fitting into this organization as an investment officer or a capital allocator, you're getting in the right vehicle. It has the right standards. It will reject ideas that really are irrational. I've been on a lot of boards. Charlie's been on a lot of boards. You would be amazed at the number of things that are responding to animal spirits rather than the rationality that take place. And we have our animal spirits, but we devote them to other areas.

*(The clip appears to cut here to another answer.)*

**Warren Buffett:** The intrinsic value of a Berkshire, like any other business, is based on the future amount of cash that can be expected to be delivered by the business between now and judgment day, discounted back at the proper rate. Now, that's pretty nebulous. Another way of looking at it is to try and figure out the value of the businesses we own presently, and we try to give you the information that will enable you to make a reasonably close estimate [as to] that. We own lots of marketable securities. It's probably safe to say that they are worth more or less what they are carried for. And then we own a number of operating businesses, and we try to give you the figures on those businesses that are the figures that we use in making our own judgments about the value of those businesses.

Now, that tells you what we have today and more or less what it's worth. But since Berkshire retains all of its earnings, it becomes very important to evaluate what will be done with those earnings over time. I mean, it is not only a question of what the present businesses are worth. It's a judgment on the efficiency or the effectiveness with which retained earnings will be used.

If you had looked at the intrinsic value of Berkshire in 1965, we had a textile business that was probably worth about $12 a share. But that was not the only part of the equation, because we intended to use any cash generated to try and buy into better businesses than we had, and we were fortunate enough to be able to buy in the insurance business in 1967 and build on that. So it was not only [unclear: "a combination of with"] the business we had, but the skill with which retained earnings would be used, that determined what the present value actually should have been at Berkshire going back that far.

It's the same situation today. We will put to work billions and billions of dollars this year, next year, the year after. If we put that to work effectively, each dollar has a greater present value than a dollar has simply in cash or distributed. If we do it ineffectively, it has [a] value of something less.

The businesses today, you know, we have, whatever the figure is in the annual report, roughly $80,000 in marketable securities. If our insurance business breaks even, that $80,000 is free to us in terms of using it. And we have a group of operating businesses, and we show their earnings in the report, and we're going to try and add to those, and they'll try to add to their earnings. But if Charlie and I were each right now to write down on a piece of paper what we think the intrinsic value of Berkshire is, our figures would not be the same. They'd be reasonably close.

## Part 2: Could Berkshire help fix health care? We look for easy problems ([06:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCD8jo_nYHo&t=379))

**Questioner (Mike Klene, Salinas, California):** Morning. Good morning.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* Morning.

**Questioner (Mike Klene, Salinas, California):** My name is Mike Klene, and I'm a general surgeon from Salinas, California. Given your resources and experience in underwriting insurance, do you have any thoughts of venturing into or helping to solve our health care mess? Time is ripe for a new approach, with Berkshire's clarity brought to the formula. Let's acknowledge the stakes are huge, with implication for our economy and our future as a country.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Let me try that one. It's too tough.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* I would [...]

**Charlie Munger:** We can't. Warren and I can't solve that one.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, we can't solve that one. We try to look for easy problems, because those are the ones we find we have the answers for, and you can do that in investments. We don't really try tough things. Now, sometimes life hands you a problem, not in the financial area in our case usually, but it'll hand you a problem that is very tough and that you have to wrestle with. But we don't go around looking for tough problems.

I would say this: we do very, very little in health insurance. You know, if we were looking for a solution through the private sector, we would be looking for something with very, very low distribution costs. I mean, you do not want a lot of the revenue soaked up in frictional costs between the benefits paid and the premiums received. I don't know how to do that. And I haven't seen anybody else that's very good at doing it. And you can say, if you're paying close to 15% of GDP for health costs, you know, somebody ought to be able to figure out something. But I haven't heard it. Maybe we'll hear it in the upcoming political campaign. Charlie's view reflects mine at the present.
