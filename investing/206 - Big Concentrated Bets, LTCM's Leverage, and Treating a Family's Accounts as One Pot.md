# Big Concentrated Bets, LTCM's Leverage, and Treating a Family's Accounts as One Pot

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_3vTA8o1Vw

Recorded: most likely 2008 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why Smart Investors Should Never Diversify". It is misleading in two ways. Nobody says smart investors should "never" diversify: Buffett says that a few times in a lifetime there are "lead pipe cinches" worth 75% of your net worth (when working with smaller sums), and that the real danger is leverage, using LTCM's roughly 25-times-net-worth positions as the example; the sharpest anti-diversification lines ("the whole secret of investment is to find places where it's safe and wise to non-diversify"; "Diversification is for the know-nothing investor") come mostly from Munger, who adds that it is "exactly what they should practice" for know-nothing investors. And the last third of the clip is about something else: whether a married couple should manage their IRAs and brokerage account separately or as one pot. The clip is from a Berkshire annual meeting, most likely the **2008** meeting (moderate confidence). The clues: Buffett mentions things he saw "in 2002 in the junk bond field" and LTCM (1998) as past events; he says some shareholders have had close to 100% of their net worth in Berkshire "for 40 or more years" (Buffett took control in 1965, so this is 2005 or later); he refers to the Cologne portfolio "as mentioned earlier" in the meeting; and the questioner mentions a Roth IRA.

## Part 2: Diversification is for the know-nothing investor ([03:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_3vTA8o1Vw&t=236))

**Charlie Munger:** The students of America go to these elite business schools and law schools, and they learn corporate finance the way it's now taught, and investment management the way it's now taught. And some of these people write articles in the newspaper and other places, and they say, "Well, the whole secret of investment is diversification." That's the mantra. They've got it exactly [unclear: "back assert"; context suggests "backwards"]. The whole secret of investment is to find places where it's safe and wise to non-diversify. It's just that simple.

Diversification is for the know-nothing investor. It's not for the professional. And there's nothing wrong with the know-nothing investor practicing it. It's exactly what they should practice. It's exactly what a good professional investor should not practice.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* But that's [...] there's no contradiction in that. A know-nothing investor will get decent results as long as they know they're a know-nothing investor. Diversify as to [the] time they purchase their equities and as to the equities they purchase. That's crazy for somebody that really knows what they're doing.

And you will find opportunities that, if you put 20% of your net worth in it, you have wasted the opportunity of a lifetime, in terms of not really loading up. And we've had the chance to do that way, way in our past, when we were working with small sums of money. We'll never get a chance to do that working with the kinds of money that Berkshire does. We try to load up on things, and there will be markets when we get a chance to from time to time, but very seldom do we get to buy as much of any good idea as we would like to.

## Part 3: Separate accounts or one pot for a married couple ([05:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_3vTA8o1Vw&t=344))

**Questioner:** I'm 45 years old and have achieved financial independence, in that I'm able to manage the money of my spouse and myself full-time. And that goes to marrying well, part of that.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* I was [...]

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* That could be a big part of it.

*(Laughter.)*

**Questioner:** Marrying well in the sense that I received the encouragement and the confidence to pursue that.

**Warren Buffett:** That's terrific.

**Questioner:** I was going to ask you a question more along the lines of diversification, but I think I will put it this way. I'll skew it a little differently. Each of us has a traditional IRA, a Roth IRA, and together we have a brokerage account. Should the assets in those accounts be separated, or better managed as a whole pile? In other words, have overlapping securities in each account, or different types of securities relegated to a specific account?

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. Well, I would say your marriage sounds like it's going to last. So I think you should think of yourself and your husband as a unit. In my view, you should look at your overall financial condition and not worry about where the location of the assets will be. So if you have a net worth of X, and you have 20% of it in a 401k and 30% outright and so on like that, just look at the whole picture and decide what mix of assets, what type of assets you want, and don't treat them as being in separate pots.

I mean, at Berkshire, we own stocks in a whole bunch of different [...] our insurance companies own stocks in separate portfolios, and we even have a portfolio in Cologne, as mentioned earlier. I don't even think about what entity anything is in. It's all working for Berkshire. And I think the way to think about your situation is to think about it all working for your family.

Now, you strike me as having a very solid marriage, and I think your husband would be crazy if he split with you. But if you're just starting out, you may want to keep your money separate for a while, so you'll see how it plays out, because a significant percentage do end up in divorce. I don't get into marriage counseling very often, so I can feel the ground sort of disappearing [unclear: "between"] my feet here. But I will turn it over to our marital expert, Charlie Munger.

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, occasionally you'll find an investment that is going to produce a huge amount of taxable income. It's a junk bond paying a high yield that's taxable, or something. So some items are more suitable for those retirement accounts that get tax deferral benefits. But apart from that, it's all one pot.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* Sure.
