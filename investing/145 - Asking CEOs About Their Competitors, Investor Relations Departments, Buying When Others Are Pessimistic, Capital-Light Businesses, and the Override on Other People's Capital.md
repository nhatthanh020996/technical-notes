# Asking CEOs About Their Competitors, Investor Relations Departments, Buying When Others Are Pessimistic, Capital-Light Businesses, and the Override on Other People's Capital

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RG8pctqLIZI

Recorded: most likely 2024 or 2025 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence that it is one of these two; low confidence on which), possibly spliced from more than one answer

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: How To Find Great Stocks". It is vague and overstated: there is no method for finding great stocks. The only research tip is an anecdote: as a young man, Buffett would visit CEOs, promise to leave in 10 minutes, and ask which competitor's stock they would own (and which they would short) if stuck on a desert island for 10 years. He then says that today's investor relations departments make that impossible, declines a personal meeting, and moves on to separate topics: buying when people are most pessimistic, not getting emotional about stock prices, capital-light vs. capital-intensive businesses, and how money managers get rich from "an override on other people's capital".
>
> Dating. Munger is not on stage and is referred to in the past tense ("Charlie and I decided it wasn't too elegant a business"), so this is after his death in November 2023. Becky Quick is present ("Becky's many listeners and viewers"), Buffett mentions "the 40,000 people we may have here", and he talks about rising capital intensity at "the Magnificent Seven". That points to the **2024** or **2025** Berkshire annual meeting. His remark that 1% fees on Berkshire "last year" would have been $8 billion fits Berkshire's market value of roughly $800 billion during 2023 slightly better (suggesting 2024), but the AI-spending context of the "Magnificent Seven" remark could suggest 2025. ## Part 3: Born in 1930, and not being emotional about stock prices ([03:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RG8pctqLIZI&t=236))

**Warren Buffett:** We will make our best deals when people are the most pessimistic. You know, that's been true ever since I was 1930. I was born in 1930. When I was born, things got much more attractive over the next 2 years, and apparently I didn't do anything about it there.

*[Laughter]*

**Warren Buffett:** But, you know, that was the opportunity of a lifetime, and I blew it by, you know, worrying about the kid in the next crib or something. But over my lifetime, you know, I've had fabulous opportunities sometimes, and they happen because humans are human.

I'm fearful of all kinds of things. I don't want to try and, you know, be one of the wonders and walk on a tiny strip between a couple of twin towers or something, or whatever it may be. But I don't get fearful by things that other people are afraid of in a financial way. The idea that, if Berkshire went [...] let's say Berkshire went down 50% next week, I would regard that as a fantastic opportunity, and then it wouldn't bother me in the least. And most people aren't [...] they just react differently.

And so it's not that I don't have emotions, but I don't have emotions about the prices of stocks. I mean, I actually can [...] those decisions get all the way to my brain, whereas emotions can get bogged down some other place.

## Part 4: Retained earnings, and businesses that need little capital ([05:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RG8pctqLIZI&t=352))

**Warren Buffett:** So Berkshire will increase its earning power over time as we retain money. I mean, we are doing things, making decisions every day. People are working. We're retaining earnings. We will build the earning power, but it won't be coming in any even stream, and it certainly won't be matched dollar for dollar on either the upside or the downside in market prices. But that's what makes it a good business. And, you know, the investment business is that everything isn't properly appraised, and the sillier other people get, the better your opportunities get.

Better to make a lot of money without putting up anything than it is to make a lot of money by putting up a lot of money. And so a business that takes no capital to speak of [...] Coca-Cola: the finished product, which has gone through bottling companies and everything, that takes a lot of capital. But in terms of selling the syrup, or the concentrate that goes to it, it doesn't take a lot of capital. So one is a fabulous business, and one is a [...] you know, it depends where it is and everything like that. Coca-Cola's popular every place. But some places, I mean, if you're in the bottling business, it costs real money. You have real trucks out there. You have all kinds of machinery, and you have capital expenditures coming up.

And, you know, we've got businesses that take very little capital but make really high returns on capital. And the ones the politicians talk about as making high returns actually aren't making high returns, usually, in terms of capital.

Property-casualty insurance is kind of a rare business, because you need capital as a guarantee fund that you will keep your promises. But you can use it to buy other low-intensive capital businesses. I mean, you can buy Apple, and that would support that business. So that can be a pretty good business, and it's one of the reasons we've done well over time.
