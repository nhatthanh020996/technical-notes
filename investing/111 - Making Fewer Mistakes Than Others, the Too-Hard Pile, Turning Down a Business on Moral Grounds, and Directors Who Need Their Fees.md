# Making Fewer Mistakes Than Others, the Too-Hard Pile, Turning Down a Business on Moral Grounds, and Directors Who Need Their Fees

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cHJ3JeaB710

Recorded: compilation of Berkshire annual meeting answers, most likely from the mid-to-late 2000s (low confidence for every part; Part 1 is no earlier than 2005)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger: How To Build A Fortune With Your Stock Portfolio". It is misleading: there is no advice about building a stock portfolio. Only the first minute touches on getting rich (you just need slightly more wisdom than others and to make fewer psychological mistakes), and most of the talking is Buffett's. The rest is about the "too hard" pile and the lack of a degree-of-difficulty factor in investing, a business Buffett and Munger turned down on moral grounds after a trip to Memphis, and why directors who need their fees are not independent.
>
> Dating: the clip is a cut-together set of answers, and several cuts are abrupt (Munger's "I share Warren's agnosticism on the subject" answers something not shown, and the "most difficult decision" question runs straight into an unrelated answer on director independence). Part 1 is from **2005 or later**, because Buffett refers to *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (first published 2005). Part 4 fits the mid-to-late 2000s, when director independence rules after Sarbanes-Oxley were a frequent topic and Buffett spoke of having been on 19 boards (low confidence). Parts 2 and 3 cannot be dated from the captions; the venue looks like Berkshire annual meetings throughout (questions from the audience, "Charlie?", references to "past meetings"). The captions render "Elihu Root" (whom Munger often quoted) as "L.A. County Road"; it has been corrected in the text. The upload date (2026-07-05) is not the event date.
>

## Part 1: You only need slightly more wisdom than others ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cHJ3JeaB710&t=0))

*(The clip starts mid-answer; the question is not included.)*

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* You don't have to have perfect wisdom to get very rich. All you've got to do is have slightly more than other people, on average, over a long time.

**Warren Buffett:** It's the old story about the guy outrunning the bear. I mean, they don't have to outrun the bear; I just have to outrun that other fellow.

And Charlie, in Poor Charlie's Almanack, which I probably do take credit for the name of, he talks about the various psychological traps that people fall into, and simply reading that section, you will come away wiser than before you started on it.

Our personalities are such that Charlie and I probably are a little less prone to some of those mistakes than other people are. But as our record clearly indicates, we still are prone to them, and we make them, and we'll make them again. We're probably a little less inclined to make some of them than we were 30 or 40 years ago. But, you know, the nice thing about it, though, is that if you make fewer of those mistakes than others, you know, they will continue making their share, and you'll get very rich.

*(The clip cuts here; Munger's next line answers something not shown.)*

**Charlie Munger:** I share Warren's agnosticism on the subject.

## Part 2: The too-hard pile, and no degree-of-difficulty factor in investing ([01:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cHJ3JeaB710&t=80))

**Charlie Munger:** We just throw some decisions into the too-hard pile and go on to others.

**Warren Buffett:** Incidentally, there's a lot of wisdom in that remark. I mean, there are things in life that you don't have to make a decision on and that are too hard. And many years ago in one of the reports, I said one of the interesting things about investment is that there's no degree of difficulty factor. I mean, if you're going to go diving in the Olympics and try and win a gold medal, you get paid more, in effect, for certain kinds of dives than others, because they're more difficult, and they properly adjust for that factor.

But in terms of investing, there is no degree of difficulty. If something is staring you right in the face and [it's] the easiest decision in the world, the payoff can be huge. And we get paid not for jumping over 7-foot bars but for stepping over 1-foot bars. And the biggest thing we have to do is decide which ones are the 1-foot bars and which ones are the 7-foot bars, so when we go to step, we don't bump into the bar. And that is something that I think we're reasonably good at.

Now, maybe we cast out too many things as being too hard and thereby narrow our universe. But I'd rather have the [unclear: "narrow the universe be a little too interpreted as being a little too"] a little smaller than it really is than being interpreted as larger than it is. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Obviously.

## Part 3: Businesses they won't own: the trip to Memphis ([02:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cHJ3JeaB710&t=174))

**Questioner:** *[speaker uncertain; the question may be read out by someone else]* Are there some businesses we won't have as subsidiaries in Berkshire even though they're wonderful businesses? Are we rejecting some business opportunities on moral grounds?

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, well, I've referred in past meetings to one we did [unclear: "on a basis"]. We will own stocks of companies where we wouldn't want to own the whole business. I mean, I'm not sure that the logic is perfect on that, but we would not have trouble owning stock in a cigarette company. We wouldn't want to manufacture cigarettes, you know. We might own a retail company that sells cigarettes. I mean, there's all kinds of gradations, but there are things we don't want to own and be responsible for their businesses where we have no problem owning their stocks or bonds.

And some years back, Charlie and I went down to [unclear: "where"] Memphis.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Yeah. Yeah.

**Warren Buffett:** We looked at a [...] We were invited down, and we looked at a company that made a product that [was] perfectly legal. Probably one of the best businesses I've ever seen in terms of the economics of it.

**Charlie Munger:** Absolutely.

**Warren Buffett:** Still doing very well. And we met in the room with [...] We went to a hotel, we met in the room with the people that had the business, and [the] people were perfectly decent people. And they described the business to us, and we went down in the lobby, and as I remember, we sat in the lobby and we just decided we didn't want to be in that business.

And, you know, the lines are not perfect on this sort of thing. I mean, I'm sure that there may be ads in the Buffalo News that are selling some investment service or something that I would cringe at if I knew the people involved or what they were selling. And if you own a big retail establishment, a retailer [of] general merchandise, you know, you're probably going to be selling cigarettes when you don't think that you should smoke yourself or that your children should smoke. And they're not perfect, but we have turned down some, the most dramatic being that one, because [it] took us a trip of a thousand miles or so to finally face up to the fact that we didn't want to own it. Charlie, do you have anything to add on it?

**Charlie Munger:** No, but that was interesting, because we were young and poor then by modern standards, and, you know, we're very human, and we could see it was just like putting a hundred million dollars in a bushel basket and setting it on fire as we walked away. And [...]

**Warren Buffett:** You're making me feel bad.

## Part 4: The hardest decision (deflected), and directors who need their fees ([05:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cHJ3JeaB710&t=344))

**Questioner:** What is the single most difficult decision you've had to make in your lifetime, whether it be business or personal?

**Warren Buffett:** I think I want to let Charlie answer that one first.

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, I would argue that that may be one that you shouldn't ask. Or let's put it this way: I think you should answer it with several interesting examples before you ask us to answer.

*(The clip cuts here to a different answer, about director independence.)*

**Charlie Munger:** A director getting $150,000 a year from a company, who needs it, is not an independent director. That director automatically becomes an inside director. And so it's a typical government intervention. It says it's doing one thing, and it does another.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, I've been on 19 boards. I've never seen a director where the director's fees were important to them object to an acquisition proposal, [or] object to a compensation arrangement of the CEO. It's just never happened, you know, in my experience. And they frequently do not behave as they would if they owned the place. And basically we want people to behave as if they owned the place.

**Charlie Munger:** The correct system is the Elihu Root system. Elihu Root, who had three different cabinet appointments, if I remember right, said no man was fit to hold public office who wasn't perfectly willing to leave it at any time. And if Elihu Root didn't approve of something the government asked him to do, he could always go back and be the most sought-after lawyer in the world. He had an identity to go back to, and he didn't need the government's salary. And I think that ought to be more of the test in corporate directorships. Is a man really fit to make tough calls who isn't perfectly willing to leave the office at any time? My answer is no.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, we have one of our directors who's been removed twice from compensation committees of other corporations because he had the temerity to actually question whether the compensation arrangement being suggested was the appropriate one. I mean, [unclear: "It's not It's being put on the comp committee of American corporations"], as I've said, they're looking for Chihuahuas, and not Great Danes and Dobermans. But [...] Yeah. And I hope I'm not insulting any of my friends that are on comp committees.

*(Laughter.)*

**Charlie Munger:** You're insulting the dogs.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* No.
