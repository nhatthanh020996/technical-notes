# Avoiding Bad Business Associates, Filtering Ideas Fast, and Talking Up Disney Stock

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VRmGAzkBJPU

Recorded: 1997 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence; 1998 possible)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger: Avoid These Awful People Who Will Wreck Your Life And Career". It is misleading. Only the first minute and a half is about "awful people", and it is about choosing business associations (people who sell you a business or partner with you), not about your life and career in general. It is also not clear that Munger is the speaker in that part (see below). The rest of the clip is about filtering investment ideas (opportunity cost, understandability, a sustainable edge), walking away from auctions and renegotiators, and a question on whether Buffett's public praise of Disney makes it harder to buy more cheaply. The Disney question points to the **1997** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence; 1998 is possible). The clues: the questioner refers to Buffett's 1995 annual report and to his remarks "at the '97 meeting" of Disney (February 1997), and to concern about "the Ovitz compensation package" (Michael Ovitz left Disney in December 1996 with a large severance); the meeting talk of a "shrinking universe of stocks" and "less margin of safety than ever" fits the high market of 1997. The clip appears to be edited from several answers; the first two parts have no date clues of their own and may come from the same meeting or a different one.

## Part 4: Does praising Disney make it harder to buy more cheaply? ([04:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VRmGAzkBJPU&t=288))

**Questioner:** You have both talked today about the shrinking universe of stocks you could purchase, less margin of safety than ever and a higher opportunity cost. And you've also talked about looking to potentially purchase your great companies that you already have at reasonable prices. And so I wonder if, by talking so positively about some of your stocks, in particular Disney, such as in the '95 annual report, when you talked about actually telling everyone that you were buying more shares on the open market, and again at the '97 meeting, at their meeting, when you talked about maybe not selling the shares [...] Those were both opportunities, maybe, when Disney may have dropped because of such things as increased debt, or even people's concern about the Ovitz compensation package. And I just wonder if that may hurt your ability to buy these great companies at reasonable prices, by talking so positively about them, when in fact maybe you could buy them at lower prices when people get irrational.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. You're saying that, which I probably agree with, that if we would say the world is going to hell at Coke or Disney or Gillette, we might be better off in terms of being able to buy more stock. I got asked a question at Disney and I answered it, and that's my general approach. I think it's usually a bad mistake to sell your interest in wonderful businesses. I don't think people find them that often, and I think they get hung up: if they've sold them at X, they want to buy them back at 90% of X or 85% of X. So they'll never go back in at 105% of X.

I think, on balance, if you are in a business that you understand, and you think it's a really outstanding business, the presumption should be that you just hold it and don't worry. And if it goes down 25% in price or 30% in price, if you have more money available, buy more, and if you don't, so what? Just look at the business and judge how it's doing.

But there's no question [...] I mean, we try not to talk very much about the businesses, except maybe to use them as an illustration in a teaching mode or something of this sort. We're not touting anything, and I did try to stick those precautions in when I do talk about them as being wonderful businesses, so people don't take it as an unqualified buy recommendation or something of the sort. But we won't try and put any spin on [...] When we're talking about business generally, we may not talk about them at all. If we're buying something, particularly if no one knows that we've been in that stock at all, we might be somewhat quiet about the fact. But we don't want to talk down something in order to buy it.

**Charlie Munger:** [unclear: "trying I always"] Jerry Newman, as I understand it, didn't like Ben Graham giving all these courses explaining what Newman and Graham were doing. But Graham's attitude was that he was a professor first, and if he made just slightly less money by being very accurate in what he taught, well, so be it. And I think it's fair to say that Warren has assimilated a bit of that ethos, and I think it's all to the good. And if it costs us a tiny little bit of money from time to time, there're probably compensating benefits. And if there aren't, it's probably the right way to behave anyway.
