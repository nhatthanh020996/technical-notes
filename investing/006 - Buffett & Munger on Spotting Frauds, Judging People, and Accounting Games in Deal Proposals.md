## Part 1: How Berkshire filters out untrustworthy people

*(The clip starts mid-answer. From the context, the question was about how to recognize fraud or dishonest people in business and finance.)*

**Warren Buffett:** I get letters all the time, and I hear from people who have been taken advantage of in financial transactions. It really is sad. And a lot of it isn't even fraud. For one thing, there are just the charges involved, the frictional costs, and the baloney that is presented. It's tough.

Charlie and I have had very good luck in terms of buying businesses and putting our trust in people. It's been overwhelmingly good. But we filter out a lot of people. And then people ask, "How do you filter them out?" I would say, and I think Charlie will agree with this, that people give themselves away fairly often. Maybe it helps to have been around as long as we have and to have seen the various ways they give themselves away. When somebody comes to me with a business (and I probably shouldn't say this publicly, because they'll tailor their approach afterward), the very things they talk about, what they regard as important and not important, give a lot of clues about their subsequent behavior. Like I say, we've had a batting average I wouldn't have thought we would have had with the people we've joined with. It hasn't been 100%, but it's been well above 90. I get asked how we make those judgments. Charlie, can you articulate the way we do it?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, partly we're deeply suspicious when the proposition is too good to be true. Warren once introduced me to a gentleman promoter who wanted to inveigle us into an insurance program. He said, "We only write fire insurance on concrete bridges that are covered by water. It's like taking candy from babies." We are able to filter out propositions like that.

**Warren Buffett:** Yes. Anybody who implies in their comments, or in what they laugh about, or in all kinds of things, that it's so easy, when it ain't that easy, we get suspicious very quickly. The truth is we rule out 90% of the time, and we may be wrong about a fair number of the ones we rule out. The important thing is whether we're right about the ones we rule in. So we don't mind. We're looking for the obvious cases of people you can trust.

## Part 2: Munger's fidelity bond claim story

**Charlie Munger:** I think you really ought to talk about the experience with the fraud.

**Warren Buffett:** What, the fidelity claim with the guy? Yes. It was a very well-known insurance company. We don't have to name names. You basically just told them to raise the stakes to make the game fair. This was back in the 1960s. Do you remember that?

**Charlie Munger:** I don't remember it well.

**Warren Buffett:** I do. I remember it well.

**Charlie Munger:** Tell it, then.

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie had this tiny little operation where he ran his fund. It also had a seat on the Pacific Coast Stock Exchange. The firm was called Wheeler, Munger. It was called Wheeler, Munger at first, and later it changed to Munger, Wheeler, and Jack Wheeler said, "Pretty soon it'll be Munger and Company, but that's okay." Jack Wheeler was a very interesting guy, and he had the specialist position in General Motors and a few other things.

Some employee stole, I don't know, $12,000 or something like that.

**Charlie Munger:** I remember. He had the trading tickets.

**Warren Buffett:** He stole some money. Charlie's firm, Wheeler, Munger, was required to have a fidelity bond covering dishonest employees and all of that. This guy was clearly dishonest, and he'd clearly stolen the money. So Charlie puts in a claim for $12,000 or whatever the loss was and sends it to this very big and prestigious insurance company. And of course the insurance company denies the claim. They say the guy really wasn't employed, he doesn't exist, you don't have a dog, the whole thing. Charlie gets this letter back saying they're not going to pay the claim.

So Charlie writes a letter to the very well-known, big-name person who runs the insurance company. He says, "Look, we have this $12,000 claim. This guy stole the money, and we thought we had an insurance policy against people stealing money. We're in a very interesting position, because you've got a bunch of people on the payroll who are going to get their weekly or monthly check, so they just say 'we're not going to pay' and life goes on. Whereas I'm sitting here with my own time, and it isn't worth the $12,000 for me to fool around with a claim against the company while they appeal it and all these things." Then he said, "I know you would be offended by the thought that you might be using this inequality in bargaining position to avoid paying the claim. That never could be your intention. So, in order to really live up to your code of behavior, why don't we take the $12,000 claim and multiply it by 10, and call it $120,000 either way. If you lose, you pay me $120,000. If I lose, I'll pay you $120,000. Now it's worth my while."

He addresses the letter to the chairman, and he gets a $12,000 check by return mail.

**Charlie Munger:** It's not a bad lesson.

## Part 3: Assessing sellers and reading financial statements

**Warren Buffett:** We can't identify 100% of the frauds, or 90%, or 80%, but there are certain ones that jump out at you. People give themselves away a lot, too. In poker they talk about tells. Charlie and I have bought a lot of businesses, and when we buy them it's very important that we assess the individuals we're buying from with some degree of accuracy. They hand us the stock certificate, we hand them a lot of money, and then we count on them to run the business with as much enthusiasm after they have the money as they did before. So we are assessing people. We don't think we can assess everyone accurately. We just have to be right about the ones where we make an affirmative decision. Those decisions have not always been perfect, but they've been pretty good, and I would say they've probably gotten a little better as the years have passed.

Similarly with financial statements. In the insurance field, for example, we've seen some frauds, and you can see things being done with loss reserves. I won't name any names; unlike Charlie, I don't. We'll call them companies A and B instead. But you would see companies where, a year or two before they offered stock to the public, the reserves would go down very suspiciously. Or when they were buying in stock, they might be building the reserves. There are a million different ways, and I obviously don't claim to know all of them. But I have seen enough situations over the years, and I've seen how promoters act, and you can spot certain people who are, one way or another, playing games with the numbers.

## Part 4: Accounting games in proposals from investment bankers and private equity

**Charlie Munger:** Yes, the proposals we receive from the investment world. I've got to tell you about one, because it illustrates...

**Warren Buffett:** Go on.

*(From here the speaker appears to be Buffett, who often tells these stories about deals pitched to Berkshire. The transcript gives no clear hand-off, so the attribution is uncertain.)*

**Warren Buffett:** We received a proposition the other day, and I'll disguise the numbers a little so nobody can pick it out. It was a private company, and we'll say it was earning $100 million a year. But the seller of the business and the investment banker suggested we should look at the earnings as being $110 million a year. As a private company, they had to pay their top people in cash, which was expensed. But we could pay them in stock options and things like that, which weren't expensed, or were explained away as not really counting. So we could report $110 million if we gave away something we didn't want to give away. Essentially, by lying about our accounting, we could add $10 million in earnings. And they wanted us to pay them for that, because they couldn't do it and we could. At this point, of course, they've lost me totally. But it's just astounding, the accounting games that are played, and all the adjustments for why the place should really be earning more than before.

We also had one come in from a private equity firm. By mistake, we got the email the private equity firm had sent to the manager about making projections for the business. They told him to add 15%, because they said Buffett discounts projections by 15 or 20% anyway, so just add 15 to offset his conservatism. It's not an elegant business, as Charlie will tell you.
