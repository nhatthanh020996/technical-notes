# Buffett & Munger on Discounted Cash Flows, Graham's Three Ideas, Faked Rewards, Mental Models, and Reading

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FwFvOzIfRjY

Recorded: compilation of Berkshire annual meetings, 1990s to early 2000s. Part 1: probably 1996; Parts 7-8: probably around 1998; other parts: undated (low-moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger: Why Calculating Cash Flow Is A Waste Of Time". It is misleading: Munger never says calculating cash flow is a waste of time. In the first 45 seconds he jokes that he has never seen Buffett actually do a discounted cash flow calculation, and Buffett adds that if a deal needs pencil and paper, it is too close; the margin of safety should be obvious. The rest of the clip, mostly Buffett, has nothing to do with cash flow: Graham's three ideas, rewarding things that can be faked, the bottom 20% of the workforce, mental models, Berkshire's long horizon, and books.
>
> The clip is a **compilation** of short excerpts, apparently all from Berkshire annual meetings of the **1990s to early 2000s** (the "zone" microphones, Gillette still being a separate company, which ended in 2005). Dating each part (low–moderate confidence): the discounted cash flow exchange (Part 1) matches a well-known quote usually attributed to the **1996** meeting; the mole rats / Nathan Myhrvold story (Parts 7–8), with Buffett having taken Munger to Microsoft "in December", is most likely from the late 1990s (probably **1998**); the other parts give no firm date clues. The upload date (June 2025) is not the event date.

## Part 3: Munger: never reward anything that can be easily faked ([01:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FwFvOzIfRjY&t=85))

*(A separate excerpt.)*

**Charlie Munger:** I hate with a passion rewarding anything that can be easily faked, because I think then people lie, and lying works, and the lying spreads, and I think your whole civilization deteriorates. If I were running the world, the compensation for stress under workman's compensation would be zero. Not because there isn't real stress, [but] because there's no way to keep the fakery out if you reward stress at all.

## Part 4: Buffett on the bottom 20% and a changing job profile ([01:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FwFvOzIfRjY&t=118))

*(A separate excerpt; it starts mid-answer, referring to charts in a Forbes magazine article.)*

**Warren Buffett:** One problem you can see just by looking at that profile is that if you assume 20% of the [...] the bottom 20%, however you measure it in terms of employability, whether it's measured by IQ or interest in working or energy level or whatever you want to do, they fit very well most of the jobs that were available 100 years ago. In other words, you could do most of the jobs, of which there were many, with relatively unimpressive mental abilities. And as jobs have changed, the profile of people hasn't changed. So there are more people that end up on the short end.

Now, the good part of that is the society produces so much more that it can take care of those people one way or another. Now, the trick is to take care of them and make them not only feel but be productive and be part of the act. And we've got enough product to do that. We're not perfect at figuring out how to make the bottom 20 or 30% in terms of abilities fit a new changing job profile.

I really recommend you look at that Forbes magazine, because if you think through the implications of those charts, I think you'll see what social problems have to be attacked.

## Part 6: Wall Street's short memory, and Berkshire's assumption that it will be around forever ([04:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FwFvOzIfRjY&t=257))

**Warren Buffett:** I mean, Wall Street really doesn't seem to learn business lessons for very long. It may not be to their advantage to learn it. Charlie, would [...] that would probably plug right into [unclear: "Charlie's money"; context suggests "Charlie's model"].

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* You bet. Yeah, it's better to [...] if you're out selling the future, it may be better to forget the past, if you're getting paid on selling it and not on betting your life on it in some way.

**Warren Buffett:** One situation at Berkshire that really is somewhat different than many companies: we assume, and unfortunately it's an error, but we assume we'll be around forever. So in our insurance business, we assume we're going to be here to pay every claim, and we're not going to retire at 65 and hand over something to someone else. It wouldn't be any sense playing games on accounting, because it would catch up with us later on.

Whereas in many businesses, I don't think they have quite the same horizon on things. They do it at Coca-Cola or they do it at Gillette, but many companies are [...] I'm afraid that more than you'd like are thinking about what kind of little pictures they can paint for the next four quarters or so. And that's easy to do, but our problem is we're going to be around a lot longer, we think, than four quarters. So that's not an option available to us, and we really run it as if in the year 2050 or something somebody's going to look and say, "How'd it work out?"
