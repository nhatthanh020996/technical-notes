# Worst-Case Thinking, Avoiding Borrowed Money, Insuring Unusual Catastrophe Risks, and Nuclear, Chemical and Biological Exclusions

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G5xOd4Lzzi0

Recorded: most likely 2005 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence)

## Part 2: Never risking what you need for what you don't need ([01:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G5xOd4Lzzi0&t=60))

**Warren Buffett:** We've never used a lot of borrowed money. Back when I started out, I had $10,000, but I just didn't want to borrow any significant amount of money. There's no reason to. We're living fine. We were living fine when I had $10,000. And the idea that you risk what you need and is important to you for something that you don't need and is unimportant is just craziness. And we try to run Berkshire that way.

I had a 98-year-old aunt, my Aunt Katie, that died last year. She had everything she had in Berkshire, and the idea that I should be doing something to try and add a few dollars to my net worth or a few percentage points to the record, and be risking the fact that she would go back to Social Security, is, you know, I just think that's kind of crazy for a manager. But maybe if I had a 2-and-20% arrangement with my Aunt Katie I'd behave differently, but I hope not.

## Part 3: Planning for the worst: quakes and hurricanes ([01:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G5xOd4Lzzi0&t=115))

**Warren Buffett:** My job is to think absolutely in terms of worst case, and to know enough about what's going on in both investments and operations to make sure that no matter what happens, I don't lose sleep that night over whether there's a 9.0 quake someplace, or whether there's a force five hurricane that actually goes up the East Coast and enters at places that very seldom does it enter. Long Island, for example: a huge amount of exposure.

How often is Long Island going to really have a major hurricane? Not very often. I think there was one back in the '30s, but there were potato fields there in the '30s, and now there's all kinds of insured value. And everything that can happen will happen. In terms of what we know of history in this country, the last three or 400 years, the most severe quake by far was at New Madrid, Missouri. And who would have guessed that that quake would happen? And there were two subsequent quakes. They're far enough apart so they didn't exactly seem like aftershocks. All three of those were higher than eight on the Richter scale. Nobody thinks about that. But they've had them [in] Charleston, South Carolina [...]

You will see things, maybe not in your lifetime, but if you take the centuries, some extraordinary things can happen. And it's Berkshire's job to be prepared absolutely for the very worst. And by and large, I would think hurricanes and quakes are the biggest thing now. But a few years ago we did not have nuclear, chemical and biological risks excluded in policies, and we had huge exposure, subsequently gotten rid of.

## Part 4: Writing risks nobody else will write: an airport, the Final Four, the Grammys ([03:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G5xOd4Lzzi0&t=235))

**Warren Buffett:** But we take on large risks. I'll give you a couple of examples. Just the other day, and nobody else will write this stuff, basically because they would want to reinsure it with somebody else and they're not set up to do it [...] One large international airport came to us, and we wrote a policy for $500 million excess of 2.5 billion from any action that was not caused by nuclear, chemical or biological sources. So if that airport is taken out in some way, but not by nuclear, chemical or biological activities, the first 2.5 billion somebody else worries about, and we get the next half billion. There's a [unclear: "sublim counted"; probably "sublimit"] for business interruption. So that airport would have to be out for a couple of years to have a billion six of business interruption. So you need 900 million of physical damage on top of that. But somebody is willing to buy that policy, and there's a real risk.

We insured the NCAA Final Four down in St. Louis against being cancelled: not postponed, or having those Final Four games moved to another city. But if it was cancelled totally, and again not through nuclear, chemical or biological, we would have paid $75 million. Same thing at the Grammys.

We take on risks that very few people want to write. In the end, we're willing to lose a lot of money in one day. But we're not willing to do anything that causes us any discomfort in terms of writing checks the following morning.
