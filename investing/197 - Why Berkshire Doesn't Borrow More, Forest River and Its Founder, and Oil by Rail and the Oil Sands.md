# Why Berkshire Doesn't Borrow More, Forest River and Its Founder, and Oil by Rail and the Oil Sands

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6n7RftC-KAU

Recorded: most likely 2014 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: How Debt Can Rapidly Destroy Great Stocks". It is misleading: nobody talks about debt destroying stocks. In Part 1 Buffett answers a suggestion that Berkshire should borrow more; he says it would have been an easy yes 40 years ago, that "another 30 or 40 billion of debt at Berkshire would be nothing", and that the questioner is "probably right", but explains why Berkshire keeps parent-level debt low. The rest is about Forest River and its founder, and about BNSF carrying crude oil and the Canadian oil sands. The clip is most likely from the **2014** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate–high confidence). The clues: float of "77 billion" (Berkshire's float at the end of 2013); "25 billion or so of excess cash"; a specialty chemicals company "recently" bought from Phillips 66 in exchange for Berkshire's Phillips stock (agreed at the end of 2013, closed in early 2014); Forest River bought "about 10 years ago" (Berkshire bought it in 2005); and a "Matt" from the railroad sitting nearby (a guess: BNSF's then-CEO Matt Rose). The Forest River answer is addressed to "Johnny", apparently one of the analysts on the panel (a guess: Jonathan Brandt); the Part 3 question comes from "Station 10". None of the questions themselves are included.

## Part 1: Leverage: why not borrow tens of billions at the parent? ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6n7RftC-KAU&t=0))

*(The clip starts at the beginning of the answer; the question, which apparently urged Berkshire to take on more debt, is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** I think if you'd asked Charlie and me 40 years ago, that if we were looking at the present set of interest rates and we had some wonderful businesses that are making a lot of money, whether we'd have gone out and borrowed a whole lot of money for the long term, we would have said yes, right, Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Wouldn't have been a hard decision.

**Warren Buffett:** But we've got several reasons. We, A, we do have a good way of generating funds other than through equity, through float. And we've done that to the tune of 77 billion.

And we don't like the idea of operating a very conservatively leveraged company and then changing courses so that the people who bought bonds that were rated double A sort of find themselves with much lower rated bonds or of the sort. We don't have any problem leveraging up the utility or the railroad. They deserve to have even a lot more debt than they do, but we keep it sort of in line with what the rating agencies think should be conventional ratios. But if you analytically look at them, both of them could withstand a fair amount more debt.

At the parent level, you know, looking back on the BNSF deal, we borrowed some money that time and we used some equity. I think using equity helped us make the deal, but it was, you know, it was not a smart thing to do, basically. I should have [...] And I could have always gone in the market and repurchased a bunch of stocks subsequently, and that's probably what I should have done on that. So, I understand your point. I completely [...] you know, another 30 or 40 billion of debt at Berkshire would be nothing, and it would cost very little.

We don't actually have great places to put it now, as evidenced by the fact that we've got 25 billion or so of excess cash. We are reluctant to leverage it up a lot at the parent now, since we have these other sources of money that are really pretty attractive. We're selling what we call structured settlements, for example, that have a very long duration, and they actually have an interest cost to us of less than if we were to sell bonds. So, we are doing certain things that are along the lines you urged, but not nearly as aggressively as you urged, and you're probably right. And you're certainly right if we saw a $50 billion deal and we passed on it for some reason because we were unwilling to take on some debt. If we see a really good $50 billion deal, we'll figure out a way to do it.

Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** I think we'd welcome it. But even though what you suggest is intelligent, we're probably not going to do it in advance.

## Part 2: Forest River: Pete Liegl's company ([03:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6n7RftC-KAU&t=197))

**Warren Buffett:** We bought a company called Forest River, run by a fellow named Pete Liegl, I'd say about 10 years ago or so, and it's interesting. Pete, who was not an MBA type at all. He's a terrific guy. He built up a [...] That was not a statement. That was an observation.

Pete built up a very successful, but much smaller RV business, and he sold it to a private equity firm in the mid-1990s, and they promptly started telling him how to run it, and he very shortly thereafter told them to go to hell, and not very long after that it went broke, which is not an unusual [...] I would have predicted that. So Pete then bought it out of bankruptcy and rebuilt it, and then came to see me about 10 years ago. And then one afternoon [...] we went to dinner that night, he brought his wife and his daughter, and we bought the business. And he made me a couple of promises, that he's very limited his promises, I told him what I'd do, and we've lived happily ever after.

I've never been to Forest River. It's based in Elkhart, Indiana. I hope it's there. I mean, maybe they're just making up these figures, you know. I can see some guy saying, "What figure shall we send Warren this month?" You know, haha.

But Pete does a terrific job of running the company. We made a deal at the time he came on, incentive comp and base comp. He's never suggested a change, I've never suggested a change. He's built the company to where it'll do over [...] I think it'll do over four billion dollars of business this year. I probably had three or four phone calls with him in the whole time. It's his company, and he does a sensational job.

I don't know about the Thor–Forest River situation in terms of how tough it is to go in to compete with them. I think it'd be tough to compete with Pete under any circumstances. It's a tough business, and you do work on narrow margins, to get to your point on that, Johnny. It's a business that runs with maybe 11 or 12% gross margins, and probably 5 to 6% of SG&A. So, you know, your margins are in that 6% to 12% range.

We have a very good [...] both from his standpoint and from our standpoint, we worked out an incentive comp. Like I said, we worked it out that afternoon, and [unclear: "all my money came by"]. And it's worked for him. It's worked for us. You know, it couldn't be a better arrangement. I wish we had 20 like it, and probably most of our shareholders don't even know we own Forest River.

But that is a company that will do 4 billion of business this year, and I'll bet we'll do more business over time. It's a leader in its industry. The industry is not going to go away, and you know, maybe we can even sell a little insurance on RVs. So, that's the story on Forest River.

## Part 3: Crude oil by rail, a pipeline additive, and the oil sands ([07:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6n7RftC-KAU&t=427))

*(The question from Station 10, apparently about the oil sands, is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** Station 10.

I mean, you know, oil sands are huge, and we own some ExxonMobil, and they've got an operation in the oil sands, obviously. One thing you might find kind of interesting: you know, we are moving 700,000 barrels a day of crude oil on our railroad. We've probably got maybe nine unit trains now. Matt can correct me on that. You know, they carry 100 cars or so, and each one has 650 barrels or so [of] oil. So that oil, you may find interesting. Not only is there a significant advantage in terms of the flexibility of where you take it, so that spreads are different in different places, and you can move it to refineries that you might otherwise have trouble moving to. Rail's flexible that way. But rail actually, you know, mentally you think of oil gushing through pipelines, but rail is probably, I would say, close to twice as fast in moving crude as are pipelines.

But we recently bought a company from Phillips 66. We got it in an exchange for our Phillips stock. We bought a specialty chemicals company, and its main product is a chemical additive that causes oil to move through pipelines about 10% faster than it would otherwise. So it may take a day off of the trip. So in some way we're actually in the pipeline business, in a small way, the crude pipeline business in a small way, through that.

I don't think [...] I think the oil sands are an important asset for mankind. Obviously, there's a huge amount of oil there. They're [an] important asset for mankind, you know, over the centuries to come. But I don't think it will dramatically change anything at Berkshire. Matt might have a different view on that. I'll ask Charlie to talk to that. If Matt would like to say anything, I'd be glad to hear from him, too.

**Charlie Munger:** Well, but a lot of the oil sand production uses natural gas to produce the heavy oil. So it's a very peculiar thing. It's economic only if oil stays at a very high price, and it's delightfully economic only if natural gas is too cheap. So, it's a very peculiar business. And it is good for mankind. But whether it's a great investment or not, I haven't the faintest idea.

**Warren Buffett:** Matt, do you have anything to add on the crude situation there? Okay.
