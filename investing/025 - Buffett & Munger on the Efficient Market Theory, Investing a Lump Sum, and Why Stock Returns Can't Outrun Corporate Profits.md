# Buffett & Munger on the Efficient Market Theory, Investing a Lump Sum, and Why Stock Returns Can't Outrun Corporate Profits

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Pvg8bscRFA

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Efficient Market Theory Is Bullsh*t". It is misleading: Buffett never says that, and his actual view is more careful. He says the market is "generally fairly efficient" and that it is hard to find mispriced securities, but that being fairly efficient does not support the efficient market theory approach to investing that the academic world built on it. Munger is the harsher one, calling the "hard-form" efficient market theorists "an embarrassment". Only the first half of the clip is about the efficient market theory; the second half answers an elderly shareholder's question about investing new money and turns into a warning that equity returns cannot keep outgrowing GDP and corporate profits. The clip is most likely from the **1999** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate–high confidence), and it appears to be one continuous stretch from that meeting. The clues: Buffett says the Fortune 500 earned $334 billion and had a market cap of $9.9 trillion "at the end of the year" (these are the 1998 figures from the 1999 Fortune 500 list, which Buffett also used in his 1999 Sun Valley talk); he says it is "difficult" right now to find mispriced securities and that equities are not a great deal, consistent with the 1999 bull market peak; Munger refers to the U.S. being 53% of world stock market value "mentioned earlier"; and the questioner asks about Buffett's "designated successors", which was a recurring topic in the late 1990s.

## Part 2: Buffett: the market is fairly efficient, but that doesn't support efficient market theory ([00:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Pvg8bscRFA&t=48))

**Warren Buffett:** Well, we like to think they are, and they may be more outliers than we are.

The market is generally, you know [...] to me, it's almost self-evident, if you've been around markets for any length of time, that the market is generally fairly efficient. It's fairly efficient as [to] pricing between asset classes. It's fairly efficient in terms of evaluating specific businesses. But being fairly efficient does not suffice to support an efficient market theory approach to investing, or [to] all [the] offshoots that have come off of that in the academic world.

So if you'd believed in efficient market theory, and been taught that and adopted it for your own 20 or 30 years ago, or 10 years ago (I think it probably hit its peak about 20 years ago), you know, it would have been a terrible, terrible mistake. It would have been like learning the Earth is flat. You would have had the wrong start in life.

Now, it became terribly popular in the academic world. It almost became required belief in order to hold a position. It was what was taught in all the advanced courses, and a mathematical theory that involved other investment questions was built around it, so that if you went to the center of it and destroyed that part of it, it really meant that people who'd spent years and years and years getting PhDs found their whole world crashing around them.

I would say that it's been discredited in a fairly significant way over the last decade or two. I mean, you don't hear people talking the same way about it as you did 15 or 20 years ago. But the market generally is fairly efficient in most ways. I mean, it's hard to find securities that are inefficiently priced. There are times when it's relatively easy, but right now, for example, it's difficult.

I don't know exactly how much it's [unclear: "wholly written"; probably "holy writ"] still in business schools. I certainly get the impression, as I go around talking to business schools, that it is far less regarded as, you know, sort of unquestioned dogma than it was 15 or 20 years ago. The University of Florida now has some courses in valuing businesses; the University of Missouri's putting in one. And I think the high priests of efficient market theory are probably not in the same demand for speaking engagements and seminars and all of that as they were a decade or two ago.

It's very interesting, though: it's hard to dislodge a belief that becomes sort of the dogma of a finance department. It's so challenging to them. And, you know, they have to, at age 30 or 40, go back and say, "What I've learned up to this point, and what I've been teaching students and all of that, is silly." That doesn't come easy to people.
