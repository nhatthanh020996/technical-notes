# No Opinion on Commodities, Gutenberg and the Newspaper Business, Dexter Shoe and Deals Pushed Through Boards, and Berkshire's Owner Board

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoxUNiJyQbY

Recorded: most likely 2007 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why You Should Never Own Gold or Oil". It is misleading. Gold is never mentioned, and Buffett does not say you should never own oil. He says Berkshire has "no opinion on commodities": if it owns an oil stock, it is because the stock is cheap, not because Berkshire expects the oil price to rise; it once bought oil futures. He then explains that Berkshire prefers businesses that need little capital (See's Candy versus steel or oil businesses). The rest of the clip is about something else: the newspaper business (the Gutenberg thought experiment and the falling LA Times circulation), his Dexter Shoe mistake and how failed acquisitions never show up in the accounts (ten Gillette deals that missed their projections), Munger on self-serving delusion in deals, the bank owner who asked for a promise of no more deals that dumb, and what makes a good board (Data Documents and Berkshire's own). The upload date (2025) is not the event date. The setting is a Berkshire annual meeting (See's Candy on stage, "you all" as shareholders, a questioner thanking them). The year is most likely **2007** (moderate confidence). The clues: Berkshire owns POSCO, bought "at four or five times earnings", and has made 20% on the Korean won (POSCO was first disclosed in the 2006 annual letter, and the won was strong in early 2007 but fell sharply in 2008); the LA Times circulation is "800 and some thousand" and falling every week; Bill [Gates] is on the Berkshire board (he joined in 2004). Munger is present.

**Warren Buffett:** So we may occasionally be in those kind of businesses. But you can find some businesses that really require relatively minimal capital investment.

Here's the case. This is a small one, but See's Candy is not going to require a huge capital investment. [It] requires some capital investment, but it's a wonderful business. ## Part 5: Self-serving delusion in deals, the small-bank seller, and boards of real owners ([05:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoxUNiJyQbY&t=338))

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah. The self-serving delusional nature of even some very good minds, in terms of IQ points, is amazing. I had a friend who sold a business to a government-controlled business in a socialized Scandinavian country. And my friend had a very nice business. And the people on the other side, after they had bought it for stock in their government corporations, said, "This was such a marvelous deal. We got your whole business and we didn't have to give anything."

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* Well, we owned stock in the Third National Bank one time, down in Asheville, and they're wonderful people, really wonderful people, but they got the ability to acquire other banks, where they formerly had been limited in that ability. And they went out to some very small bank, and the guy at the very small bank said, "I want stock." And he says, "My stock is worth private market value, and your stock is worth market price." Well, market price happen to be half of [...] But he says, "You know, all I'm getting is whatever the market is. So I want you to value your stock at the market and value mine at this huge premium." And he said, "And then there's one other condition." He said, since I'm going to be putting my whole net worth in your stock, he said, "I want your promise you'll never do a deal this dumb in the future." [Laughter] You remember that one?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, I remember.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. And that fellow was just getting a little more out in the open than is typically the case.

I've been on some terrific boards. There was a local one here called Data Documents that really functioned, with everybody on the board thinking about the business, understanding the business, making decisions as owners. Every one of them had a significant percentage of their net worth in the business, and [it was] probably the best board I've ever been on. I mean, every decision there was made for business reasons.

The worst decisions [...] at least they have the potential for being the worst. I mean, but it's standard procedure now, when an acquisition comes up, to trot in, you know, investment bankers and the lawyers, and the momentum is just totally to get the deal done. And like I say, there will be a lot of slides presented, and believe [me], I don't need to look at the slides. I know what the answer is going to be at the end. They're going to say it's a great deal, you know. But there will be nobody arguing the other side there. It is not like something where you would make a decision and you'd have somebody get pro and con. It just doesn't work that way. I don't know how to improve that a lot.

I think we've got a sensational group at Berkshire. You have a group with almost everybody on the board having a significant percentage of their net worth. Bill's is so big we can't get him to a significant percentage, but he's got hundreds of millions of dollars in it. We've got a board that is in exactly the same position as the shareholders. They don't have directors and officers insurance. They've got the downside as well as the upside. They bought their stock in the open market, so it hasn't been given to them. It is a real owner's board. And I like it that way. I think it's a terrific group, and I'm glad I can get them to work cheap.
