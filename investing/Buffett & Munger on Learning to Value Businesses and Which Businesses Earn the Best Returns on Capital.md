# Buffett & Munger on Learning to Value Businesses and Which Businesses Earn the Best Returns on Capital

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5woO0OmKaJ8

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: The Easiest Way To Value Stocks". It is misleading. Buffett does not offer an easy valuation method. He says there is no shortcut: know the edge of your circle of competence, keep asking questions about businesses, and insist on a margin of safety. The clip is from a Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting, most likely 2010 or 2011 (moderate confidence). Clues: Buffett says he bought Korean stocks "six or seven years ago" (he bought them around 2004). A question is read by Becky Quick, and journalists only started reading questions in 2006. Business Wire, acquired in 2006, is mentioned as a Berkshire company. A questioner jokes that he "would have come by rail", which points to after Berkshire bought BNSF in early 2010.

---

## Part 1: How do you get better at valuing businesses?

**Questioner (Jeff Colette, Olathe, Kansas):** Hello, my name is Jeff Colette and I'm from Olathe, Kansas. I got started in investing in 1999, right before the big tech bubble. Unfortunately, I learned "buy and hold" and "don't fret about market price fluctuations" before I learned the importance of valuing a business and applying a margin of safety. So, as Charlie said, I got my feet wet with huge failure right away.

**Charlie Munger:** Join the club.

**Questioner:** Thank you, I don't feel so bad now. That leads to my question. I've read all the Berkshire reports and everything I can about you two, and I thank you for these wonderful meetings. But it seems like it boils down to some simple things: valuing a business and applying a margin of safety. So my question is, what approach do you recommend for getting better and better at valuing companies?

**Warren Buffett:** That was a very, very good question. In my own case, I started out without knowing anything about valuing companies. Ben Graham taught me a way to value a certain type of company that would prove successful, except the universe of those companies dried up. Nevertheless, it was almost a guarantee against failure. But it was not a guarantee that these things would continue to be available. Charlie taught me a lot about the value of a durable competitive advantage in a really first-class business. Over time I've learned more about various types of businesses, but you'd be amazed how many businesses I don't feel I understand well.

The biggest thing is not how big your circle of competence is, but knowing where the perimeter is. You don't have to be an expert on 90% of the businesses, or 80%, or 70, or 50. But you do have to know something about the ones you actually put your money into. If that's a very small part of the universe, that still is not a killer.

Think about what you would pay for a McDonald's stand. Think about the businesses in your own hometown of Olathe. Which would you like to buy? Which do you think you could understand the economics of? Which do you think will be around 10 or 20 years from now? Which do you think would be very tough to compete with? Just keep asking yourself questions about businesses, and talk with other people about them. You will extend your knowledge over time. And always remember that margin of safety.

I think you basically have the right attitude, because you recognize your limitations, and that's enormously important in this business. You will find things to do. Six or seven years ago, when I was looking at Korean stocks, for example, I never had any idea that Korean stocks would be something I'd be buying. But I looked over there and I could see that there were a number of businesses that met the margin of safety test. There I diversified, because I didn't know that much about any specific one. But I knew that a package of 20 was going to work out very well, even if a crook might run one of them and a couple might run into competition I didn't anticipate, because they were so cheap. That was the old Graham approach. You will find opportunities from time to time, and the beauty of it is you don't have to find very many of them. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, obviously, if you want to get good at something which is competitive, you have to think about it a lot, learn a lot, and practice doing it a lot. And the way the world is constructed in this field, you have to keep learning, because the world keeps changing and your competitors keep learning. So you just have to get up each morning and try to go to bed that night a little wiser than you were when you got up. If you keep doing that for a long time and accumulate some experience, good and bad, as you try to master what you're trying to do, people who do that almost never fail utterly. They may have a bad period when luck goes against them, but very few people ever failed with that. If you have the right temperament, you may rise slowly, but you're sure to rise.

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie, did you take any business courses in school?

**Charlie Munger:** None. I took accounting.

**Warren Buffett:** When did you start valuing businesses, and how did you go about it?

**Charlie Munger:** When I was a little boy, I can remember coming down to the Omaha Club, and there was an old gentleman who hit the Omaha Club about 10:30 every morning. He obviously did almost no work, and yet was quite prosperous.

**Warren Buffett:** He became your ideal.

**Charlie Munger:** Yes. Well, he made me very curious as a little boy. I said to my father, "How in the hell does he do that?" And he said, "Charlie, he's in a business where he enjoys practically no competition. He gathers up and renders dead horses." That was an example of avoiding competition by one strategy. If you keep asking questions like that of reality, starting at a young age, you gradually learn.

**Warren Buffett:** And you were doing the same thing.

**Charlie Munger:** Yes. Thankfully, I extrapolated beyond my original insight there. But I noticed something rather interesting. If you take the rulers of the businesses in Omaha when I was a little boy, an awful lot of those businesses went broke, and a lot of them sold out at modest prices under distress. Some of the people who rose, like Kiewit, came from small beginnings. Nobody thought of them as the great glories of that early time. I think that's kind of the way life is. It's hard to get anywhere near the top, and it's hard to hold any position once you've attained it. But I think you could have predicted that Kiewit was likely to win. They cared more about doing it right, they cared more about avoiding trouble, and they put more discipline on themselves.

**Warren Buffett:** If you knew the individual well, you would have bet right.

**Charlie Munger:** If you knew Pete Kiewit himself. I would not have bet on any of the people I knew who were already wealthy, but I would have bet on Pete Kiewit. His sister taught me math. Half Dutch, half German. This is a tough culture.

**Warren Buffett:** There you've just heard it, folks: half Dutch, half German. Go out looking for them.

**Charlie Munger:** Well, the man who's recommending this is named Munger. Anyway, no, I don't think it's that. I was just automatically asking: what was working, what was failing, why was it working, why was it failing? If you have that temperament, you are gradually going to learn. And if you don't have that temperament, I can't help you.

**Warren Buffett:** If you'd followed Pete around for 10 years, you never would have seen him do anything dumb.

**Charlie Munger:** Oh, yes.

**Warren Buffett:** So it's avoiding the dumb thing. You really don't have to be brilliant, but you have to avoid what almost seem like the obvious mistakes. I would say you're on the right track back there, in terms of having the basic fundamentals and knowing your limitations, but still seeking to learn more about various kinds of businesses.

When Charlie practiced law, with any client that came in, Charlie was thinking about that business as if he owned the place. And he probably generally felt he knew more about the place than the guy who actually owned it, who was his client. I remember talking to him 50 years ago, and he would start talking about Caterpillar dealerships in Bakersfield or something of the sort. He was incapable of looking at a business without thinking about its fundamental economics. How did that guy do with the Caterpillar dealership?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, he sold it for a perfectly ridiculous price to a dumb oil company. It wasn't worth half what he got for it.

**Warren Buffett:** But they had a concept and a strategy.

**Charlie Munger:** They had a concept and a strategy, and no doubt they had consultants.

## Part 2: Which businesses earn the best return on capital?

**Becky Quick:** This question comes from Carson Mitchell in Aberdeen, South Dakota, who asks both of you: what business has had the best return on capital for Berkshire, and what business of any on Earth has had the best return on capital? And he adds, "P.S. I would have come by rail, but there are no seats in the grain rail cars."

**Warren Buffett:** There are two ways of looking at it: the capital necessary to run the business, as opposed to what we might have paid for the business. If you take a wonderful business, you could run the Coca-Cola Company, assuming you had the bottling system separate, with no capital. Now, if you buy it for $100 billion, you can look at that as your capital, or you can look at the basic capital. When we look at what's a good business, we're defining it in terms of the capital actually needed in the business. Whether it's a good investment for us depends on how much we pay for it in the end.

There are a number of businesses that operate on negative capital. Carol Loomis is with Fortune magazine. Any of the great magazines operate with negative capital. The subscribers pay in advance, there are no fixed assets to speak of, the receivables are not that much, and the inventory is nothing. So in the magazine business, my guess is that People magazine and Sports Illustrated operate with negative capital, and People in particular makes a lot of money. We had a company called Blue Chip Stamps where we got the float ahead of time, and it operated with really substantial negative capital.

But there are a lot of great businesses that need very, very little capital. Apple doesn't need that much capital. The best ones, of course, are the ones that get very large while needing no capital. See's is a wonderful business and needs very little capital, but we can't get people eating 10 pounds of boxed chocolates every day, except here. Generally the great consumer businesses need relatively little capital. Then there are the businesses where people pay you in advance, magazine subscriptions being one case and insurance being another. You're using your customers' capital. We like those kinds of businesses, but of course so does the rest of the world, so it can become very competitive to buy them.

We have a business, for example, that's run wonderfully by Cathy Baron Tamraz, called Business Wire. Business Wire does not require a lot of capital. It has receivables and everything, but it is a service-type business. Many of the service-type and consumer-type businesses require little capital, and when they get to be successful, they can really be something. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** I've got nothing to add.

**Warren Buffett:** At any rate, the formula never changes.

## Part 3: If you could own one business in the world, what would it be?

**Warren Buffett:** If you could own one business in the world, what would it be, Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** I hope I already own it myself. You and I got in trouble by addressing such a subject many decades ago, and I don't think I'll come back to it.

**Warren Buffett:** Okay.

**Charlie Munger:** If you name some business that has incredible pricing power, you're talking about a business that's a monopoly or a near monopoly.

**Warren Buffett:** Sure.

**Charlie Munger:** And I don't think it's very smart for us to sit up here naming as our most admired business something that other people regard as a monopoly.

**Warren Buffett:** Okay, we'll move right along. Number 13.
