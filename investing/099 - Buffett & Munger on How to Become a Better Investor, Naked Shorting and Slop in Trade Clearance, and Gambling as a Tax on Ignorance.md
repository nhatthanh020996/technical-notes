# Buffett & Munger on How to Become a Better Investor, Naked Shorting and Slop in Trade Clearance, and Gambling as a Tax on Ignorance

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OuOzAJUYhHY

Recorded: 2007 Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: The Easy Way To Boost Investment Returns". It is misleading: no easy way to boost returns is offered. The first answer is about how to become a better investor (read everything, then invest small amounts of real money; Munger on Sandy Gottesman's "what do you own and why do you own it"; Buffett's yellow-pad essay on buying the whole company). The rest is unrelated: naked shorting and why Buffett doesn't mind short sellers (the USG stock-lending story), Munger on "slop" in the clearance system, and why Buffett and Munger find government-promoted gambling socially revolting. The clip is from the **2007** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence). The clues: Buffett says "what I am doing today at 76" (he was 76 in May 2007); Sandy Gottesman is a Berkshire director; naked shorting was a live topic in 2007; the microphone numbering ("number 11", "number nine", "number 10") fits the meeting format.

**Warren Buffett:** Well, I think you should read everything you can. And if you haven't been interested enough in the subject to have that involvement already, anyway, he'd rather you go somewhere else.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. That whole idea that you own a business is vital to the investment process. It'd be a quantitative decision, and it would be based on pretty solid stuff. It would not be based on what you saw on television that day, it would not be based on what your neighbor said to you, or anything of the sort.

It's the same thing with stocks. I used to always recommend [to] my students that they take a yellow pad like this, and if they're buying 100 shares of General Motors at 30, and General Motors has, whatever it has out, 600 million shares or a little less, that they say, "I'm going to buy the General Motors Company for 18 billion dollars, and here's why." And if they can't write a good essay on that subject, they've got no business buying 100 shares, or 10 shares, or one share, at thirty dollars per share, because they are not subjecting it to business tests. And to get in the habit of thinking that way [...]

Sandy would have followed it up with the questions, based on how you answered the first two questions, that made you defend exactly why you thought that business was cheap at the price at which you're buying it. And any other answer, you'd flunk them. Number 11.
