# Greece and the Euro's Fatal Flaw, Social Media and Multitasking, Investing in Africa, and Sharing Your Will With Your Children

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vh1yQmwRe1A

Recorded: compilation of Berkshire annual meeting answers; Part 3 2011–2013 Berkshire meeting (moderate confidence); Parts 1–2 probably the early 2010s (low confidence); Parts 4–6 a later meeting, year uncertain (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger: Never Buy European Stocks". It is misleading, and in places it says the opposite of the clip. Nobody advises against European stocks. Munger does call letting Greece into the European Union "rat poison as whipping cream" and says Europe made terrible mistakes, but he also expects Europe to "muddle through", and Buffett says Berkshire "would be delighted tomorrow to buy a big business in Europe that we liked and we'd pay cash for it". Most of the clip is about other things: the euro's design flaw (Buffett), Munger on social media and multitasking, whether Berkshire would invest in sub-Saharan Africa, and Buffett on having grown children read your will before you sign it. The clip appears to be a compilation, so each part is dated separately. Part 3 (the euro) refers to "17 political bodies" in the monetary union, which matches the euro area's 17 members from 2011 to 2013, so it is from a **2011–2013** Berkshire meeting (moderate confidence). Parts 1–2 (accounting tricks, Greece lying about its debt to get into the union) fit the European debt crisis of the early 2010s and may be from the same meeting (low confidence). Parts 4–6 come from a meeting where questioners speak from numbered stations ("Station five") and Buffett mentions "social media at Berkshire Hathaway", so they suggest a later meeting. The exact year cannot be determined (low confidence). The questioner in Part 6 gives his name as Marvin Blum, an estate-planning lawyer from Fort Worth, and he says "as we wrap up today", which suggests it is one of the last questions of that meeting.

## Part 6: How much to leave your children, and letting them read your will ([06:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vh1yQmwRe1A&t=405))

**Questioner (Marvin Blum, Fort Worth, Texas):** Hello, I'm Marvin Blum from Fort Worth, Texas, the home to four of your companies.

**Warren Buffett:** Absolutely. We love Fort Worth.

**Questioner (Marvin Blum, Fort Worth, Texas):** Thank you. We love you too, and your presence in our community. I am an estate planning lawyer, and it's interesting, as we wrap up today, to ponder that the baby boomer generation is about to pass along the greatest transfer of wealth in history. I can design plans that eliminate estate tax and pass down great amounts of wealth to the next generation. But many of my clients come to me and say they want a plan like Warren Buffett's, leaving their kids enough so they can do anything, but not so much that they can do nothing. Now, they ask me, and I'm asking you, how much is that? And how do you keep from ruining your kids?

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. [applause] Well, I think more kids are ruined by the behavior of their parents than by the amount of the inheritance. Your children are learning about the world through you, and more through your actions than through your words. You know, from the moment they're born, you're their natural teacher, and you know, it's a very important and serious job.

I don't actually think that the amount of money that a rich person leaves to their children is the determining factor at all in terms of how those children turn out. But I think that the atmosphere and what they see about them and how their parents behave is enormously important.

I would say this: I've loosened up a little bit as I go along. Every time I rewrite my will, my kids are happy, because they know I'm not reducing the amount anyway. And I do something else that I find that [...] which I think is an obvious thing, but it's amazing to me how many people don't do it. I think that your children are going to read the will someday. That's assuming you're a wealthy person. Your children are going to read the will someday. It's crazy to have them read it after [unclear: "your dad"; context suggests "you're dead"] for the first time. I mean, you're not in a position to answer questions then, unless the Ouija board really works or something of the sort. And so if they're going to have questions about how to carry out your wishes, or why you did this or that, you know, why leave them endlessly wondering after you die?

So in my own case, I always have my children [...] only I rewrite a will every five or six years or something like that, and I have them read it. They're the [unclear: "executives"; context suggests "executors"] under it. They should understand how to carry out their obligations that are embodied in the will. And they should also, if they feel there's anything unfair about it, they should express themselves before I sign that will. We should talk it over, and we should figure out whether they're right or I'm right, or someplace in between.

So I think it's very important in wealthy families, once the kids are of a certain age. I mean, I don't advise doing this with your 14-year-old or something, but when they get, you know, certainly by the time they're in the mid-30s or thereabouts, I think they should be participants in the will. And I do think that if you get to be very wealthy, that the idea of trying to pass on, create a dynasty of sorts, it just sort of runs against the grain as far as I'm concerned. And the money has far more utility. You know, the last hundreds of millions or billions have far more utility to society than they would have to [...] create a situation where your kids don't have to do anything in life except call a trust officer once a year and tell them how much money they want. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** I don't think I want to go into this one.

**Warren Buffett:** Okay.

**Charlie Munger:** And I'm absolutely sure you don't want to discuss your will with your children if you're going to treat them unequally.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* No, that is poison. But one of the problems you have, I mean, and what you want to discuss just for that very situation, is there may be circumstances where one child will have much more of an interest in one type of asset than another, or something of the sort. And you want to make sure that your definition of equality in terms of handling different kinds of assets meshes, or at least is understood by the children, so that they don't think the fact that you gave one a farm and another [a] house, or something of the sort, resulted in inequality when you thought it was equality. Charlie? No, he's staying away from this one.
