# Buffett & Munger on Opportunities From Others' Folly, the Japanese Trading Houses, and Raider Risk to Berkshire

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rtFiPohBYg0

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett Explains When To Buy Stocks". It is misleading. Buffett gives no rule for when to buy stocks. The clip covers where investment opportunities come from today, the story of Berkshire's investment in five Japanese trading companies, and whether a corporate raider could take over Berkshire after Buffett. The clip is from the 2023 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting. Confidence is high: Buffett says "the 58 years we've been running Berkshire" (1965 + 58 = 2023), Greg Abel is on stage, and Buffett mentions that he recently went to Japan with Greg to visit the companies (April 2023) and that Berkshire now owns 7.4% of them. The clip starts in the middle of an answer, so the first question is not in the video.

---

## Part 1: Opportunities come from other people doing dumb things

*(The clip starts mid-answer. From the context, the question was about whether investment opportunities today are as good as when Berkshire started.)*

**Warren Buffett:** What gives you opportunities is other people doing dumb things. In the 58 years we've been running Berkshire, I would say there's been a great increase in the number of people doing dumb things, and they do big dumb things. The reason they do it, to some extent, is that they can get money from other people so much easier than when we started. You could start 10 or 15 dumb insurance companies in the last 10 years and become rich if you were the promoter, whether the business succeeded or not. The underwriters got paid and the lawyers got paid. That couldn't be done on a large scale 58 years ago. We couldn't get the money to do some of the dumb things we wanted to do, fortunately.

So I don't think investing has disappeared from this huge capitalistic market that anybody can play in. But the big money is in selling other people ideas, not in outperforming. I think if you're running small amounts of money, which we don't, the opportunities will be greater. But Charlie and I have always differed on this subject. He likes to tell me how gloomy the world is, and I like to tell him we'll find something, and so far we've both been kind of right. Charlie, we wouldn't budge an inch on that, would we?

**Charlie Munger:** There is so much money now in the hands of so many smart people, all trying to outsmart one another, out-promote one another, and get more money out of other people. It's a radically different world from the world we started in. I suppose it will have its opportunities, but it's also going to have some unpleasant episodes.

**Warren Buffett:** But they're trying to outsmart each other in arenas that you don't have to play in. Look at the Government bond market, the Treasury bill market. You've got one bill that's out of line with the others, and we bought over $3 billion of it the other day.

The world is overwhelmingly short-term focused. If you go to an investor relations call, they're all trying to figure out how to fill out a sheet showing the earnings for the year, and management is interested in feeding them expectations that will slightly be beaten. That is a world made to order for anybody who is trying to think about what will work over 5 or 10 or 20 years. I would love to be born today and go out with not too much money and hopefully turn it into a lot of money. And Charlie would too, actually. He would find something to do, I guarantee you. It wouldn't be exactly the same as before, but he would end up with a big, big, big pile.

**Charlie Munger:** I would not like the thrill of turning my big pile into a small pile. I like my big pile just the way it is.

**Warren Buffett:** Well, we agree on that, incidentally.

**Charlie Munger:** Okay, we do.

**Warren Buffett:** You're one of the most extreme lovers of the big pile. Charlie?

## Part 2: The Japanese trading companies investment

**Charlie Munger:** Well, tell them the story of the Japanese investment. That should be told again. That's a nice story.

**Warren Buffett:** Well, it was pretty simple. Back when I started, other people were going through Playboy and I was going through Moody's. There's a movie out called *Turn Every Page*, which I saw for the second time a couple of days ago. It's by Lizzie Gottlieb, and I recommend everybody in this world watch it. I turned every page in the past. I did it for thousands and thousands of pages in Moody's. I did it at the Department of Public Utilities in Boston. I did it at the insurance department. You just kept turning pages. That went on for a while, but now we need big ideas in order to find things. And what was your question, Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Tell them about the Japanese.

**Warren Buffett:** Well, the Japanese thing was simple. I like looking at figures about companies. Here were five very, very substantial, understandable companies. We've done business with most of them, maybe all of them, in a dozen different ways. If you go a couple of miles from where this place is, our last coal generating plant was built by one of the companies.

So here they were, sitting as a group, earning, we'll say, 14% on what we were going to pay to buy them. They were paying decent dividends. They were going to repurchase shares in some cases. They owned a whole bunch of businesses that we could understand as a group. That doesn't mean we had a deep understanding of any one of them, but we'd seen them operate. There wasn't anything to it.

At the same time, we could take out the currency risk by financing in yen, and that was going to cost us half of 1%. Well, if you get 14% on one side and half a percent on the other side, and you've got money that you know you'll have forever, and they're doing intelligent things, and they're sizable, we just started buying them. I probably didn't even tell Greg until maybe six months after we'd gotten going. When we hit 5% in all of them, we announced it on my birthday, my 90th at that, that we owned over 5%. I recently went over for the first time to visit with them, and we were more than pleasantly surprised. We were delighted with what we found there. Now we own 7.4%. We won't go over 9.9% without their agreeing. And we just sold another 164 billion or so of yen.

**Charlie Munger:** *[Partly unintelligible in the transcript.]* If we only had $5 billion or something, and it made $10 billion simply in that way, we would look like heroes.

**Warren Buffett:** Now $10 billion just sort of disappears as a little dot in Berkshire's reports. But it's fun.

**Charlie Munger:** It is fun, and it is $10 billion.

**Warren Buffett:** And Charlie says it keeps me out of bars. I probably talked to Charlie about this the year after I started, I don't know. But I knew he'd like it, obviously.

**Charlie Munger:** We tried to do more, but we could only do about $10 billion.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, well, not even quite that much. But we are four or five billion ahead, plus dividends, and we've got a carry that's terrific. They welcome us, and they should welcome us. We love the way they're operating. We're not there to tell them what to do in the least. But we did say we'd never go over 9.9%, and we mean it, and they know that we'll be true to our word.

I went over there partly to introduce Greg to those people, because we're going to be with them 10, 20, 30, 40 years from now, and they may occasionally find something we can do jointly. They look forward to doing that and we look forward to it. In addition, we have some other operating businesses in Japan. Greg, do you have anything?

**Greg Abel:** The only thing I would add is that, as Warren went over there, it was to build trust with these Japanese companies, because we do hope there are long-term opportunities. But fundamentally, as you highlighted, they've been a very good investment. I'd also highlight that the five meetings we had were really quite remarkable: these companies, the culture and the history around them, and how proud they are. There were moments of learning from them. It was a great experience to spend two days with the five companies.

**Warren Buffett:** And the yen issues we've been selling have turned out, over the years, to be worth to Berkshire a very wide spread, probably $400 or $500 million a year. We'll keep looking for more opportunities in Japan. Berkshire is the largest corporate borrower in yen from outside of Japan that exists. We didn't set out to be that, but it turned out that way. And we're not done in terms of what may come along there. We have some direct operations there, as I mentioned, and we've got some really wonderful partners working for us, and I don't have to do anything.

## Part 3: Could a corporate raider take over Berkshire after Buffett?

**Questioner:** I worry that a corporate raider like Carl Icahn, or a group, will buy up enough shares to take control of Berkshire and completely disregard Warren's philosophy of holding a lot of cash in U.S. Treasury bills, and instead be greedy, reckless, and highly speculative, and ruin Berkshire's position as a rock-solid financial fortress.

**Warren Buffett:** I think it's fair to say we think about it plenty, but I don't worry enormously about it. It is true that Greg and the directors will have a honeymoon period for a long time, simply because of the votes that will still remain. But it's true that eventually they will get judged on how well our operation fares versus others.

If we don't pay any dividends, in 12 or 15 years you're talking about a trillion and a half dollars that it would take to take over Berkshire. That limits the group. They like to think about how much they can borrow against it, and that doesn't work at that size. There's nobody who can come close to doing it themselves.

I think the important thing is that Berkshire be regarded as a national asset rather than a national liability. We've got to be a plus to the country with our form of operation. We will have a record that will then be 12 or 15 years longer, done with much more capital and more companies. More things will have happened, where our hundreds of billions work their way into the economy in terms of lots of jobs, lots of products, lots of behavior, and it can be compared with other things. So I think we win out if we deserve to win out, and I think the odds of that happening are very, very, very, very high. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** I don't spend much time worrying about something that's going to happen 50 years after I'm dead. I think if you take care of each day's responsibilities pretty well and think ahead as well as you can, then you just take the results as they fall. So I'm philosophical about it. I think he's fretting unnecessarily.

**Warren Buffett:** Okay. Neither one of us is worried, basically. But we do plan. I've got a model in my mind of what Berkshire has been. That model has been modified plenty of times over 58 years. The one thing I knew initially, or very quickly, was that it shouldn't be a textile company. That was an important decision. We've just played the hand as it came along, and we've made a few really good decisions.

We'll never make a decision that kills us. For the only things that are a threat to the planet, we don't have any answer. But we keep ourselves in better shape than anybody else. We just aren't going to have big maturities of debt coming along. We aren't going to have insurance policies that can be cashed in en masse. We will sit with what looks like a huge amount of capital, and it is a huge amount of capital, but there's also a huge amount of earning power and a huge amount of diversity. So our business model will be graded, and it'll be graded against a whole lot of people that we'd like to be graded against. I think we're handing something very secure over to the future.

And I think we've got a shareholder base like nobody has. There isn't anybody in the country that I know of, unless it was an employee-owned company prior to going public or something of the sort. This is the product of 58 years of regarding the shareholder as the owner of the company.

But what does that mean? It means having happy customers. It means being welcomed by your community rather than having them turn you away. It means the government feels better with you around if there's a financial crisis, because you can provide something that, under some circumstances, the country can't, and you'll be there. At the same time, it'll be good for the business. We will have crises of one sort or another, but if they aren't challenging the planet, which worries you in terms of some of the threats that we have, we'll be a plus to the United States. And if we're a plus to the United States, we'll survive.
