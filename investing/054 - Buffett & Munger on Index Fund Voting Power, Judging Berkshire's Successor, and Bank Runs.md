# Buffett & Munger on Index Fund Voting Power, Judging Berkshire's Successor, and Bank Runs

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_C1g1TgZ_zA

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett Explains Why Index Funds Are Dangerous". It is misleading. Buffett never says index funds are dangerous. He says the big index fund managers have piled up voting power, that it is in their own interest to back off from using it, and that they use cheap index funds to pull in money and then sell higher-fee funds. Index funds are only the first three minutes. The rest is about how shareholders should judge Greg Abel once he runs Berkshire, and about bank runs, the FDIC and poor messaging after Silicon Valley Bank. The clip is from the **2023** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence). The clues: Buffett says "here we are in, you know, 2023", talks about "the weekend of Silicon Valley Bank" (March 2023) and his recent trip to Tokyo (April 2023), and Munger is present.

## Part 2: What should shareholders watch for once Greg Abel runs Berkshire? ([03:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_C1g1TgZ_zA&t=188))

**Questioner:** "The only real way to see how someone is going to do when running a company is to let them run it." **Warren Buffett:** Well, I think I would have some comfort in the fact that 99% of my net worth is in that company, so I've probably got a stronger interest in it, and perhaps 100 billion or more of philanthropy will be affected by [it]. And if they put somebody in, Berkshire on automatic pilot can work extremely well for a long time. I mean, it doesn't [unclear: "like the businesses go away"] or anything [unclear: "the thing sort"]. It's hard to judge successor management in a really good business, because if they don't show up at the office, it'll keep working for a long time. And maybe that lack of useful input may show itself in five years, [or] it may go a long, long, long time.

And how are the shareholders [to be] advised by a bunch of people that are concerned about whether you're meeting earnings projections or something, telling them whether the management's any good? It is very, very hard. I've been on the board of 20 companies. If you ask me to rank the management of each one, it's very difficult to do, because some are just better businesses than others. Some would be better off not managed hardly at all. Others really need help, but they've got a lousy business.

Tom Murphy told me a long, long, long time ago, he said, "The secret of business is to buy a good business," and it's okay to inherit one too. If they put somebody in (there's a surprise, we both go down on a plane), they've got a real job in assessing that person, because it'll depend on how good he or she is as a talker. ## Part 3: Bank runs, the FDIC, and poor messaging after Silicon Valley Bank ([06:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_C1g1TgZ_zA&t=404))

**Warren Buffett:** Anticipating a few questions on banks, I decided we should start using bank language here to describe [unclear: "and Charlie"]. *(Applause)*

The situation in banking is very similar to what it's always been in banking: fear is contagious, always. And historically, sometimes the fear was justified and sometimes it wasn't. My dad lost his job in 1931 because of a bank run. They had a bank run on state banks, and the head of the Omaha National Bank said, "Well, we're a national bank," and they didn't have a run on the national banks. And of course they both faced the same problem.

So it used to be that if you saw people lining up at a bank, the proper response was to get into the line, and [unclear: "you'll always leave it"]. And the story is that Sidney Weinberg of Goldman Sachs, during one of the great bank runs, back in 1907 or thereabouts, had a job as a runner [at] Goldman Sachs, and asked his boss if he could take the week off. And [the] boss said, "Sure, not much is going on anyway." So he got in line, whether it was at Knickerbocker Trust or wherever, and as he got toward the front of the line, he sold his place in line to somebody. He didn't have an account at the bank, but that was an asset.

And the banking system has changed so much over the years, and we did something enormously sensible, in my view, when we set up the FDIC. As many [as] 2,000 banks failed in one year, back after World War I. I mean, bank runs were just part of the picture. And if you have people that are worried about whether their money is safe in the bank, and all trying to withdraw, you can't run an economy very well. So the FDIC was very logical. It's [been] changed over the years some, but here we are in, you know, 2023, and we actually see the FDIC pay off at 100 cents on the dollar to everybody, or make it available on all demand deposits. And yet you still have people very worried about their [...] periodically, geographically, all kinds of crazy ways. And that just shouldn't happen.

So the messaging has been very poor. It's been poor by the politicians, who sometimes have an interest in having it poor. It's been poor by the agencies, and I'd say it's been poor by the press. I mean, you shouldn't have so many people that misunderstand the fact that, although there may be a debt ceiling, it's going to get changed; although there's a $250,000 limit on FDIC, the FDIC and the U.S. government and [the] American public have no interest in having a bank fail and [unclear: "they have"] deposits actually lost by people. We had a demonstration project the weekend of Silicon Valley Bank, and the public is still confused.

So it's really something to have a law that was passed in 19[...], or a law that became effective in 1934, [unclear: "all modified in some ways"], not understood, about something as important as a bank system. I don't think the American public is that dumb. And I made that offer over in Tokyo, incidentally. I haven't heard from anybody that wants to take up my million-dollar bet on whether the public will lose money if they have a demand deposit at a bank, no matter what the size.

So that's the world we live in. It means that a lighted match can turn into a conflagration, or it can be blown out, and who knows what will happen. And we don't have any worry. We keep our money [in] cash and Treasury bills at Berkshire, because we keep [unclear: "100 28 billion"] or whatever it was at the end of the quarter, and we want to be there if the banking system temporarily even gets stalled in some way. It shouldn't. I don't think it will, but I think it could.

And I think that the incentives in bank regulation are so messed up, and so many people have an interest in having them messed up, that it's totally crazy. I mean, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were doing 40 or so percent of the mortgage business in the United States. That is huge. Those two companies were regulated by some group (I forget what they called it), but they had 150 people that were in charge of just figuring out whether Freddie and [unclear: "Patty"] were doing the right thing. But I could have done it, you know, Charlie could have done it, and I'm not sure they needed an assistant even to do it. But the incentives were all wrong. And Freddie and Fannie, which were doing fine in August, apparently doing fine in July and August of 2008, were put into conservatorship, you know, early in September, and the things that followed from that were just incredible.

So there are second-order and third-order and fourth-order effects that are somewhat unpredictable as to what they will be, and sequence and all that. But things change. And if people think deposits are sticky anymore, they're just living in a different era. You press a button. You don't have to get in the line and wait for days and have the teller counting out the money slowly in gold so they hope the line goes away. You can have a run in a few seconds. So the way it hasn't been addressed properly is a problem, and who knows where it leads.
