# Holding Through 50% Drops, Index Funds, Buybacks, and Berkshire vs the S&P 500

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LvsQG28L3Yc

Recorded: 2020 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting (high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Buy Stocks And Never Sell". It is misleading. Buffett says you should only buy stocks if you're prepared to hold them for a very long time and sit through big declines. He never says to never sell, and he spends much of the clip on index funds, buybacks and Berkshire's performance. The clip is almost certainly from the 2020 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting (high confidence). Greg Abel sits with Buffett instead of Charlie Munger, and Buffett compares fear to "the virus". He also mentions that buyback restrictions are being written into "the loan program" (the 2020 CARES Act loans), Berkshire trailing the S&P 500 by 8% year to date, and book net worth of "370-some billion" at quarter end. The questions are read by Becky Quick. The clip starts in the middle of an answer.

---

## Part 1: Only buy stocks if you can hold them through a 50% drop

*(The clip starts mid-answer. From the context, the question was whether this is a good time to buy stocks.)*

**Warren Buffett:** I'm not recommending that people buy stocks today or tomorrow or next week or next month. I think it all depends on your circumstances. But in my view, you shouldn't buy stocks unless you expect to hold them for a very extended period, and you are prepared financially and psychologically to hold them the same way you would hold a farm, and never look at46651996 a quote. You don't need to pay attention to them.

You're not going to pick the bottom, and nobody else can pick it for you or anything of the sort. You've got to be prepared, when you buy a stock, to have it go down 50% or more and be comfortable with it, as long as you're comfortable with the holding.

I pointed out, I think a year or maybe two years ago in the annual report, well, the one just before this most recent one, that there have been three times in Berkshire's history when the price of our stock went down 50%. Three different times. Now, if you owned it on borrowed money, you could have been cleaned out. There wasn't anything wrong with Berkshire when those three times occurred.

But if you're going to look at the price of the stock and think that you have to act because it's doing this or that, or because somebody else tells you, "How can you stay with that when something else is going up?", you've got to be in the right psychological position. Frankly, some people are more subject to fear than others. It's like the virus. It strikes some people with much greater ferocity than others. Fear is something I really never felt financially, and I don't think Charlie's felt it either. But if you can't handle it psychologically, then you really shouldn't own stocks, because you're going to buy and sell them at the wrong time.

And you should not count on somebody else telling you this. You should do something you understand yourself. If you don't understand it yourself, you're going to be affected by the next person you talk to. So you should be in a position to hold.

I don't know whether today is a great day to buy stocks. I know it will work out over 20 or 30 years. I don't know whether it'll work out over two years at all. I have no idea whether you'll be ahead or behind on a stock you buy on Monday morning, or the market.

## Part 2: Is the day of index funds over?

**Becky Quick:** Let me ask this question that came in from Kevin. He says: "The last few weeks we've been hearing from active money managers that the day of passive investing is over, and that the historical safety of investing in an index fund long term is gone. Would you please provide your thoughts on this topic, particularly in regards to an investment time span of 10 years?"

**Warren Buffett:** Well, I can tell you I haven't changed my will. It directs that my widow would have 90% of the funds in index funds. I think it's better advice than people are generally getting from people who are getting paid a lot to give other advice. You don't make a lot of money advising an S&P 500 index fund.

How can you say the day of index funds is over? If you say the day of investing in America is over, I would disagree quite violently. And then, is there something special about index funds being a terrible way to invest? It's very hard to have evidence of that. If the index funds reflect the market, and one side has high fees and thinks it can pick out stocks, and the other side has low fees, I know which side's going to win over time.

You have to recognize that it's in a great many people's interest to convince you that they can do something. They may well even believe they can. A certain percentage of them will do it from luck, and a few people will do it from skill. That's what makes it so enticing: you can find the Jim Simons, or somebody, who is going to produce extraordinary returns. Jim and his group have done it by brain power, but it's very unusual. And incidentally, they're going to charge you a lot of money, and they may actually close up their fund if they do it, because they can't do it with really huge amounts of money compared to the amounts with which the record was established in the past.

You just have to recognize you're dealing with an industry where it pays to be a great salesperson, and it pays even better if you're a great salesperson and you can actually produce something. But the money is in selling. There's a lot more money in selling than in managing, actually, if you look at the essence of investment management.

## Part 3: The backlash against buybacks

**Becky Quick:** Berkshire has invested in many companies with stock buyback programs. Recently there's been a backlash against buybacks. What are your views on this subject?

**Warren Buffett:** Well, it's very politically correct to be against buybacks now, and they're going to incorporate it in the loan program. There are a lot of crazy things said about buybacks.

Buybacks are so simple. It's a way of distributing cash to shareholders. Let's say that you and I and Greg, the three of us, decide to buy an auto dealership or a McDonald's franchise or something, and we each put a million dollars in, or whatever the number may be. We get along with each other and the business grows and all of that. One of us really wants to spend our share of the earnings, and the other two want to leave the money in the business to grow.

Now, if the three of us were the only shareholders, we would not establish a 100% dividend payout for everybody, and we wouldn't freeze out the one who wanted to get out either. The logical thing to do is to buy a portion of that person's stock, whatever that person wants to spend annually from the earnings. The other two find their interest in the company goes up, and the third person still has a little more of an interest from what they leave in, but they can also take some money out of the business. You're taking money out of the business in either case. In one case you call it dividends and you send it to everybody, whether they want it or not. With buybacks, you give it to the ones who want the money.

I have been following a policy of giving away stock since 2006, and I'll give away a lot of stock. But the philanthropies that receive the gifts have to spend the money very promptly, on a current basis more or less. So they're getting $3 billion worth of stock, or whatever it may be, and I'm in effect reducing my interest in Berkshire. But Berkshire is still retaining more capital than I'm giving away, so I have more dollars invested, but my interest goes down. The people who need the cash to carry out the philanthropic efforts cash out the stock. And I don't force my sister, or whoever it may be, to take a bunch of money she doesn't want. She wants all of it reinvested in the business. The people who want the cash sell some of their stock, and the company ends up in the same position. We've distributed some of the capital that we don't need for growth.

Now, whether the company should buy it back depends on a couple of things. One is that they ought to retain the money they need for intelligent growth prospects. That's fine. And secondly, and this is a point that's never mentioned, they should be buying it back below what they think it's worth. They'll make mistakes in that, but you make mistakes in a lot of business decisions. That should be the guiding principle.

To my knowledge, Jamie Dimon at JPMorgan said it once, and we've said it various times: we will repurchase shares when it's to the advantage of the continuing shareholders to have us do so. But you read about all these buyback programs where they say, "We're going to spend $5 billion buying it back," or $10 billion. Well, that's like saying, "I'm going to go out and buy some business this year for $5 billion," without knowing what you're going to get for the money. It should be price-sensitive, obviously. It should be needs-sensitive, obviously. But when the conditions are right, it should also be obvious to repurchase shares, and there shouldn't be the slightest taint to it, any more than there is to dividends.

And the people who have now taken up the cry about how terrible it was that companies bought back stock: well, you could say it was terrible for them to pay dividends too, because then they'd have more money now. But they were doing what was intelligent at the time, and I hope they continue to do what's intelligent as they go forward. Greg?

**Greg Abel:** The only thing, and I know you've commented on it in the past, Warren: obviously we're supportive of buybacks, but there are companies whose financial engineering was probably just a little extreme and too cute. Effectively, they were using every ounce of their balance sheet to buy back stock, and at the same time creating no cushion for their business for any type of event or bump in the road. We're going to see that, and I think that's a very unfortunate outcome, and hence you get some of the backlash. But there are still many companies, as you highlighted, that do it right.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. Now, if they're buying it back because it's fashionable, because they really do like the idea... There's nothing wrong with taking an action that increases the value of the remaining shares. But I've been witness to some programs where it really is stupid. I don't think it's immoral. I think it's stupid, basically.

On the other hand, we favor companies that take care of all their requirements for growth and, as Greg says, maintain sound balance sheets and all of that, and leave a margin of error for things you can get surprised by. And if they find their stock selling below what the business is intrinsically worth, I think they're making a big mistake if they don't buy in their stock.

It's going to be a political football. Like I say, when it becomes politically correct to do something in this country, if you're a politician, the best thing to do is get on board. But Berkshire is going to do what it thinks makes sense for its shareholders, and we like investing in companies that think that way too. Not all companies obviously do.

## Part 4: Why has Berkshire underperformed the S&P 500?

**Becky Quick:** I got a number of variations on this next question, some more polite than others. This one's right about down the middle. This is from Mark Blakeley, who writes in from Tulsa, Oklahoma. He says: "Like many, I'm a proud Berkshire Hathaway shareholder. However, in comparing the performance of Berkshire with the S&P 500 over the last 5, 10, or 15 years, I've been disappointed in Berkshire's underperformance. Even year to date, Berkshire is trailing the S&P 500 by 8%. To what would you attribute Berkshire's underperformance? While I can't imagine ever selling my Berkshire stock, at some point money is money."

**Warren Buffett:** Well, I agree with everything that, I forget his name, said. The truth is that I recommend the S&P 500 to people. And I happen to believe that Berkshire is about as sound as any single investment can be in terms of earning reasonable returns over time. But I would not want to bet my life on whether we beat the S&P 500 over the next 10 years. I obviously think there's a reasonable chance of doing it, and we've had periods... I don't know, out of the 55 years we've been doing it, how many we've beaten it or not.

I mentioned earlier that 1954 was my best year, but I was working with absolute peanuts, unfortunately. I think if you work with small sums of money, there is some chance for a few people who really do bring something to the game. But I think it's very, very hard for anybody to identify them. And when they work with large funds, it gets tougher. It's certainly gotten tougher for us with larger funds.

I would make no promise to anybody that we will do better than the S&P 500. But what I will promise them is that I've got 99% of my money in Berkshire, and most members of my family, who may not be quite that extreme, are close to it. And I care about what happens to Berkshire over the long period about as much as anybody could care about it. But caring doesn't guarantee results. It does guarantee attention. Greg?

**Greg Abel:** Well, I would agree, one, that there are never guarantees. But when I look at the assets we have in place and the teams that are in place, you're committed to Berkshire, and we have dedicated teams that are equally dedicated to Berkshire, and they're sure going to give it their best effort every day. When I look at the assets and the people, as you said, you can't guarantee it, but we have a great chance of giving a good effort to outperform it.

**Warren Buffett:** It's hard to imagine getting a terrible result with Berkshire, but anything can happen. What I do know is that it would be easier to be running $5 million than our book net worth. At Berkshire, at the quarter end, I think it was 370-some billion, which is down, but it's still greater than the book net worth of any corporation in the United States. Maybe there's some federal corporation that has more, and it may be the greatest in the world, I'm not sure. That makes life difficult in some ways too.

**Greg Abel:** Right. And the potential of our operating businesses is substantial. We've talked about energy, and you touched on it: that infrastructure is continuing to change. We're ready for $100 billion of investment opportunities there if we just look at the business over the next 10 years, the infrastructure that's required and how it's changing. Substantial investments there tell me we have very good prospects, and we're well positioned to pursue them. When you look at our core businesses, which you touched on, Burlington, the insurance, and energy, our downside is very nicely protected. We have three really great core businesses.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. And we're better positioned than anybody in the energy business, just because we don't have dividend requirements. We've retained $28 billion of earnings over 20 years. You can't do that if you run a normal public company. We've got a huge appetite, the country needs it, and the world needs it. We are a very logical, well-structured, well-managed company, I would say, because it doesn't involve me, to participate in huge requirements around the world. Now, they're slow, and they involve governments, state governments, and there's a lot of it. It's not anything that happens dramatically. But it will happen, and Berkshire should participate in a huge way.

We can do things in insurance nobody else can do. That doesn't mean much at many times, but occasionally it may be important. So there are some advantages to size and strength, but there are disadvantages to size too. If we find some great opportunity to double our money for a billion dollars, that's a billion pre-tax, and that's $790 million after tax. On a market value of $450 billion, or whatever it may be, it doesn't amount to much, unfortunately. We'll still try to do it if we can.
