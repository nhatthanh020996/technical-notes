## Part 1: Wells Fargo's incentive system ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KzFxAY30oWQ&t=0))

*(The clip starts at the beginning of the answer; the question itself is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** At Wells Fargo, there were three very significant mistakes, but there was one that dwarfs all of the others.

You're going to have incentive systems at almost any business. There's nothing wrong with incentive systems, but you've got to be very careful what you incentivize. You can incentivize bad behavior, and if so, you'd better have a system for recognizing it.

Clearly, at Wells Fargo, there was an incentive system built around the idea of cross-selling and the number of services per customer. The company highlighted how many services per customer in every quarterly [unclear: "industrial"] presentation. So it was a major focus of the organization, and undoubtedly people got paid, graded and promoted based, at least partly, on that number. Well, it turned out that it was incentivizing the wrong kind of behavior.

We've made similar mistakes. Any company is going to make some mistakes in designing a system. But it's a mistake, and you're going to find out about it at some point. I'll get to how we find out about it.

## Part 2: The biggest mistake: not acting when the CEO finds out (the Salomon example) ([01:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KzFxAY30oWQ&t=79))

**Warren Buffett:** But the biggest mistake was that [...] Obviously I don't know the facts as to how the information got passed up the line at Wells Fargo. But at some point, if there's a major problem, the CEO will get wind of it. That moment is the key to everything, because the CEO has to act.

The Salomon situation happened because on April 28th, I think, the CEO of Salomon, the president of Salomon and the general counsel of Salomon sat in a room and had described to them, by a fellow named John Meriwether, some bad practice, terrible practice, that was being conducted by a fellow named Paul Mozer, who worked for him. Paul Mozer was flimflamming the United States Treasury, which is a very dumb thing to do. He was doing it partly out of spite, because he didn't like the Treasury and they didn't like him. So he put in phony bids for U.S. Treasuries and all of that.

So on April 28th, roughly, the CEO and all these people knew that something had gone very wrong and that they had to report it to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. The CEO, John Gutfreund, said he would do it. And then he didn't do it. He undoubtedly put it off just because it was an unpleasant thing to do. Then on May 15th, another Treasury auction was held, and Paul Mozer put in a bunch of phony bids again.

At that point, it's all over, because top management had known [unclear: "or had a time"], and now a guy who was a pyromaniac had gone out and lit another fire. And he lit it after they'd been warned that he was a pyromaniac, essentially. It all went downhill from there. It had to stop when the CEO learned about it.

## Part 3: The third mistake: judging seriousness by the size of the fine ([03:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KzFxAY30oWQ&t=203))

**Warren Buffett:** Then they made a third mistake, actually, but again, it pales in comparison to the second mistake. They totally underestimated the impact of what they had done once it was uncovered. There was a penalty of $185 million, and in the banking business people get fined billions and billions of dollars for mortgage practices and all kinds of things. I don't know whether the total fines against the big banks come to 30 or 40 [billion] or whatever the number may be. So they measured the seriousness of the problem by the dimensions of the fine. They thought a $185 million fine signaled a less offensive practice than something involving $2 billion, and they were totally wrong on that.

But the main problem was that they didn't act when they learned about it. It was bad enough having a bad system, but they didn't act.

## Part 4: How Berkshire finds out: the hotline ([04:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KzFxAY30oWQ&t=256))

**Warren Buffett:** At Berkshire, my main source of information about anything being done wrong at a subsidiary is the hotline. We get communications on the hotline perhaps 4,000 times a year. Most of them are frivolous, like "the guy next to me has bad breath" or something like that. But there are a few serious ones.

The head of our internal audit, Becky Amick, looks at all of them. A lot of them come in anonymously, probably most of them. She refers some of them back to the companies, probably most of them. But anything that looks very serious, I will hear about, and that has led to action more than once. We've spent real money investigating some of those; sometimes we put special investigators on them. And like I say, it has uncovered certain practices that we would not condone at all at the parent company.

I think it's a good system. I don't think it's perfect. I'm sure they've got an internal audit at Wells Fargo, and I'm sure they've got a hotline. I don't know the facts. But I would just have to bet that a lot of communications came in on that. I don't know what their system was for getting it to the right person, and I don't know who did what at any given time, but it was a huge, huge, huge error, if they were getting communications (and I'm sure they were) and they ignored them, or just sent them back down to somebody below.

Charlie, you followed [it]. What are your thoughts on it?

## Part 5: Munger on compliance departments vs. a culture of trust ([06:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KzFxAY30oWQ&t=390))

**Charlie Munger:** Well, put me down as skeptical when some law firm thinks they know how to fix something like this.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah.

**Charlie Munger:** If you're in a business where you have a whole lot of people under incentives that are very likely to cause a lot of misbehavior, of course you need a big compliance department. Every big wire house stock brokerage firm has a huge compliance department. And if we had one, we would have a big compliance department too, wouldn't we, Warren?

**Warren Buffett:** Absolutely. Absolutely.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* But that doesn't mean everybody should try to solve their problems with more and more compliance. I think we've had less trouble over the years by being more careful about whom we pick to have power, and by having a culture of trust. I think we have less trouble, not more.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* But we will have trouble from time to time.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Yes, of course. We'll be blindsided someday.

## Part 6: Acting promptly, and anonymous letters ([07:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KzFxAY30oWQ&t=442))

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie quotes what Ben Franklin, whom he worships, said: "An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure." He understated it. An ounce of prevention is worth more than a pound of cure, and I would say a pound of cure properly applied is worth a ton of cure that's delayed. Problems don't go away.

John Gutfreund originally called that problem a traffic ticket. He told the troops at Salomon it was a traffic ticket, and it almost brought down the business. Some other CEO described the problem he had encountered as a foot fault, and it resulted in incredible damage to the institution. [unclear: "And so I did it"] You've got to act promptly.

Frankly, I don't know any better system than hotlines and anonymous letters to me. I get anonymous letters. I've gotten three or four of them in probably the last six or seven years that have resulted in major changes. Almost always they're anonymous, but it wouldn't make any difference, because obviously there will be no retribution against anybody who calls our attention to something that's going wrong.

But I will tell you, as we sit here, quite a few people are probably doing something wrong at Berkshire. Usually it's very limited, maybe stealing small amounts of money or something like that. But when it gets to a sales practice like what was taking place at Wells Fargo, you can see the kind of damage it would do.
