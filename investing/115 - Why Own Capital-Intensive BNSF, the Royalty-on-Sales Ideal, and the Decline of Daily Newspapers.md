# Why Own Capital-Intensive BNSF, the Royalty-on-Sales Ideal, and the Decline of Daily Newspapers

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qI8majAJngU

Recorded: most likely 2019 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett Explains How To Get Rich From Railroad Stocks". It is misleading: nobody explains how to get rich from railroad stocks. A young questioner asks why Berkshire owns capital-intensive BNSF instead of capital-light businesses; Buffett says BNSF is a "second best choice" that is still good, because Berkshire cannot deploy enough money in capital-light businesses, and Munger says the railroad and utility returns are satisfactory. The second half is a separate answer about the decline of daily newspapers, which Buffett says matters little economically to Berkshire but enormously to society. The clip is most likely from the **2019** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence). The clues: Buffett jokes about bumping one of "the six panelists" (the journalist-and-analyst panel used at the meetings up to 2019); Apple is mentioned as a capital-light business Berkshire owns (bought from 2016); McClatchy's results "came out the other day" with advertising down about 17 to 18%; Buffett cites about 1,300 U.S. daily newspapers and says the decline has not moderated in the last five years. The newspaper part may come from a different question at the same meeting; the clip starts it mid-answer.

## Part 3: Daily newspapers keep shrinking, and few have a digital answer ([04:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qI8majAJngU&t=243))

*(This section starts mid-answer, probably to a different question.)*

**Warren Buffett:** The problem is [...] [there] has been about 1,300 daily newspapers in the United States. There were 1,700 not that long ago. [The problem] is that no one except the Wall Street Journal, the New York Times, and now probably the Washington Post has come up with a digital product that really, in any really significant way, will replace the revenue that is being lost as print newspapers lose both circulation and advertising.

And if you look at the communities in which we operate, or the communities in which, you name it, other newspapers operate, the community could be prospering. We're in a prosperous economy presently. And all are losing daily circulation. They're losing Sunday circulation. And they're [losing] what [they] call street sales. They're losing home-delivered. And I've been surprised that the rate of decline has not moderated in the last five years.

We bought all the papers at reasonable prices, so it is not a great economic consequence to Berkshire. But I would like to see daily newspapers actually, you know, be economically viable, because of the importance to society. But I would say that the trends [...] I put those circulation figures in there because I think shareholders [are] entitled to look year-to-year at what is happening. And it's happening to 1,300 newspapers throughout the United States, and it happens in small towns where you would think that the alternative sources of information would not be that good. It happens every place.

The Journal, the Times and probably the Post have a viable economic model in the digital world, and probably will continue to shrink [...] I'm almost certain we'll continue to shrink in the print world, but the digital world will be big enough, and they'll be successful enough, so that they have, in my view, a sustainable business model. But it is very difficult to see, with the lack of success in terms of important dollars arising from digital, it's difficult to see how the print product survives over time. And I'm afraid that's true of 1,300 papers in this country.

And we'll keep looking to see if there is a way to do it, but you'd have to look at our experience, and look at the experience of everyone else. McClatchy newspapers came out the other day, you know, and I think the [unclear: "newspap"], which is very good, you know, fine cities that they operate in, and advertising revenues down something like 17 or 18%, [and] in circulation. I would say that the economic significance to Berkshire is almost negligible. But the significance to the society, I think, actually is enormous. And, you know, I hope that we find something. I hope others find something, because we'll copy it. But so far we have not succeeded in that. Charlie?
