# Short Selling and Ethics, Munger's Case for BYD, and How Berkshire Pays Its Managers

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JpHIejkDX3k

Recorded: Part 1 a Berkshire annual meeting, year unknown, probably 2000s–2010s (low confidence); Part 2 2009 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence); Parts 3–4 a 2010s Berkshire annual meeting, after the BNSF acquisition (low-moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Efficient Market Hypothesis Is Nonsense". It is misleading: Buffett mentions the efficient market theory only in passing, as an example of how institutions attack anyone who challenges the conventional wisdom (in a question about short sellers who speak out). He never calls it nonsense in this clip. Most of the clip is about something else: Munger explaining that Berkshire's BYD investment shows "the old men are continuing to learn", and Buffett explaining how Berkshire sets pay for subsidiary CEOs (no compensation consultants, no HR department, no headquarters charges, and paying for "widening the moat").
>
> Dating: this is a compilation. Part 1 (short selling, ethics, accountants) has no firm clues; Buffett says the efficient market theory was dominant "30 years ago", which suggests the 2000s or 2010s. Part 2 is most likely from the **2009** meeting (moderate–high confidence): the questioner says Berkshire "recently" invested in BYD (the investment was made in 2008), and Munger credits Dave Sokol, who was still at Berkshire. Parts 3–4 are from a later meeting, after Berkshire bought BNSF (2010), since BNSF is named as a subsidiary; Buffett refers to "70 plus" businesses and calls on Becky [Quick] to read an emailed question, which fits the mid-2010s (low–moderate confidence). The name of the shareholder whose question Becky reads is captioned as "Mark Wears"; the spelling may be wrong.
>
> Notation: `[word]` = added by editor for readability; `[...]` = speaker trails off; `[unclear: "..."]` = garbled in the captions, kept verbatim; `[speaker uncertain]` = attribution inferred.

## Part 1: Short selling, attacks on the conventional wisdom, and the accountants ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JpHIejkDX3k&t=0))

*(The clip starts mid-answer; the question, apparently about short sellers who speak out publicly, is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** Well, take the extreme example. If there were two banks in town and I own one of them, and I was of kind of a devious type of mind, I might go out and hire 50 people to stand in line in front of the other bank, and I would probably not have a competitor before long. So you can do things on either side, the long side or the short side, that I would regard as certainly unethical and in many cases should be illegal.

But anytime you attack the conventional wisdom, you're going to meet with a lot of opposition, because you're threatening people's positions. When we would talk about the efficient market theory 30 years ago, when it was absolutely [unclear: "derogative"; context suggests "de rigueur"] in virtually every finance department in the country, major schools, you either had to swear allegiance to it or you were not going to be promoted. You know, people don't like that. And any institution, when they get a threat from the outside, they will attack both the threat and the threatener. But that exists on both sides.

I have no problem with short selling, and I have no problem with speaking out responsibly about your reasons for doing so, any more than I have on the long side. There have been some very bad practices on the short side, and there have been some very bad practices on the long side, in terms of people trying to, well, [unclear: "re-spread"] things that are untrue. But there's probably been more on the long side over the years [unclear: "and on the short side"; context suggests "than on the short side"] by some margin. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, I think to some extent you're criticizing the wrong people. In many cases, the accountants that allowed the lousy accounting are the ones that ought to be held in the dock. And they get very little criticism in America, and that's a mistake.

## Part 2: BYD, and the old men continuing to learn ([02:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JpHIejkDX3k&t=122))

**Questioner:** Recently you make investment in BYD, a company in China that makes batteries and also electric cars, which are arguably technology companies. So can you sort of go through that example and see how you sort of, like, analyze the case, and sort of ask questions that helped you make the decision to invest in such a company? Thank you.

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie deserves 100% of the credit for BYD, so I'm going to let him answer that.

**Charlie Munger:** Well, it's an interesting example, because Berkshire would not have made an investment in BYD if the opportunity had come along five or 10 years earlier. And it shows that the old men are continuing to learn. And that's absolutely essential. Berkshire would have a lower potential than it does if we had stayed the way we were. And so you were absolutely right in calling attention to this episode.

Again, Dave Sokol helped. I wasn't at all sure I could get Warren to do this all by myself, so I inveigled Dave into going over to China, and the two of us were able to help the learning process.

**Warren Buffett:** Well put. Well put. Becky.

## Part 3: How Berkshire sets pay for subsidiary CEOs ([03:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JpHIejkDX3k&t=201))

*(This appears to be from a different meeting from Part 2, even though the clip runs on from "Becky.")*

**Becky Quick:** This question comes from Mark Wears, and it has to do with Berkshire's compensation. He says, "How does Berkshire structure the performance-based compensation of the CEOs of its subsidiaries? Please be as specific as you can regarding the metrics on which you focus the most, and how the degree to which those metrics are attained translates into compensation."

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, well, the first thing we do is we never engage a compensation consultant. And we have, whatever it may be, 70 plus or whatever number of businesses we have; they have very different economic characteristics. To try to set some Berkshire standard to apply to businesses such as insurance, which has capital as a bulwark, but which we get to invest in other things we'd invest in anyway, so there is a minus capital involved, to a BNSF or our utility business, where there's tons of capital involved, or in between, See's, where there's very little capital involved [...]

We have other businesses that are basically just so damn good that, you know, a chimpanzee could run them, and we have other businesses that are so tough at times, you know, that if we had Alfred P. Sloan back, you know, we wouldn't be able to do very well with them. So there's enormous differences in the economic characteristics of our business.

I try to figure out, if I own the whole business, what is a sensible way to employ somebody and compensate them, considering the economic characteristics of the business. So we have all kinds of different plans. It doesn't take a couple of hours of my time a year to do it. We have managers who stay with us, so they must be reasonably happy with the plans. And, you know, it is not rocket science, but it does require the ability to differentiate.

If we had a human relations department, it would be a disaster. You know, they would be attending conferences, and people would [be] telling them all these different things to put in equations and so on. And it just requires a certain amount of common sense. Then it requires, incidentally, an interaction with the managers where, you know, I listen to them, they listen to me, and we sort of agree on what really is the measure of what they're actually adding to the company. And I would [...] What do you say to that, Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, I think the U.S. Army and General Electric have centralized personnel policies that probably work best for them. And we have just the opposite system, and I think it clearly works best for us. And practically nobody else is entirely like us, which makes us very peculiar. And do you [...]

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, we really like it that way. We get worried when people agree with us.

We pay some very big money out. I mean, we have managers that have made and will make in the tens of millions annually. And we have managers that, you know, when we suffer, they suffer. But you got to treat people fairly, even though they don't need the money. Everybody wants to be treated fairly. And so the rationale for how you're doing it should be understood, but there is no cross-Berkshire rationale at all.

I mean, if you run See's Candy, to put a cost of capital factor in or something like that with the consultant, and say, "It's nonsense." You know, it isn't going to make any difference whether there's 40 million or 43 million or 37 million of capital in the business, and the main thing to do is in terms of market position and all of that sort of thing.

The real thing I really want to pay managers for is widening the moat that separates our business from our competitors' businesses over time. Now, that gets very subjective. So I don't have any perfect way of doing that, but that is always going through my mind in trying to design compensation systems. So far, like I say, I don't think [...] I can't [...] Can you recall any manager that's ever left us over compensation, Charlie?

## Part 4: No imperial headquarters, no headquarters charges ([08:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JpHIejkDX3k&t=487))

**Charlie Munger:** I think it's amazing how simple it's been, and how little time it has taken, and how well it has worked. There's this idea that headquarters can do these wonderful things. Headquarters in a conglomerate kind of a company is frequently hated in the field. We don't want to be hated in the field. We don't want an imperial headquarters with big costs that's imposed everywhere. And averaged out, it's worked wonderfully well for us.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, we make no headquarter charges. We charge for our credit with a couple of companies, but most companies are, you know, allocating a couple percent of sales maybe, or whatever it might be, to all the different operations, and usually it's resented out in the field, and [...]

**Charlie Munger:** Is it ever.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, so we don't do it.
