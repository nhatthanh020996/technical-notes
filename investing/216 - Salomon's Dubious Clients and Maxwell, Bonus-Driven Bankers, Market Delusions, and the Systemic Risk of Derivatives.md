# Salomon's Dubious Clients and Maxwell, Bonus-Driven Bankers, Market Delusions, and the Systemic Risk of Derivatives

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rzZiGQOCG3o

Recorded: probably 2003 Berkshire annual meeting, possibly a compilation of parts of one or more meetings in the early 2000s (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Dodge The Obvious Frauds To Achieve High Returns". It is misleading: there is no advice about dodging frauds to get high returns. Buffett and Munger tell stories from their Salomon board days: a client with "a neon sign that said crook on him" whose deal Salomon's underwriting committee had approved anyway, and Maxwell, whose nickname was "the bouncing check" but whose investment banking business Salomon was courting. Buffett explains that bankers' bonuses make it hard to stop such business. The second half is about investors' "crazy notions" after the bubble, and about the danger of margin calls, collateral-posting clauses and derivatives linkages (the October 1987 wire transfer, and what a Salomon bankruptcy in 1991 would have meant). The clip is probably from the **2003** Berkshire annual meeting (low confidence). The clues: Buffett says their expectations were "more modest than most people's a few years ago" when people thought they could day trade or put money in a 401(k) and earn double-digit returns (post-2000 bubble); the long discussion of derivatives and collateral posting matches the period right after Buffett's 2002 letter on derivatives. The Salomon stories (1991) are told as reminiscences. The clip starts mid-conversation, the chapters are short and some are fragments, so it may be stitched from more than one place. The upload date (2025) is not the event date.

## Part 4: Investors' crazy notions, not American business, are the problem ([03:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rzZiGQOCG3o&t=232))

*(The chapter starts with the end of an earlier exchange; the speakers of the first lines are not clear.)*

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* That was a wonderful experience.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* There are certain stretches [...]

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* [...] are easier than other stretches.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. Our expectations were more modest than most people's a few years ago. We didn't say the world was coming to an end or anything. We just said that people have gone crazy in certain sectors, and that anybody that thought that you could sit at home and day trade and make double-digit returns over time, or do anything that you were entitled to that by just sticking a little money in your 401(k) or something, was really living in a fool's paradise. Now, to some extent they get sold that by other people. But American business really has never let investors down as a group, but investors have done themselves in quite frequently.

## Part 5: Margin calls, the 1987 wire transfer, Salomon in 1991, and a "demented" collateral system ([05:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rzZiGQOCG3o&t=304))

*(The uploader's chapter boundary falls mid-sentence: "I think many of the" is said just before 05:04. What "that" refers to is not clear from the clip.)*

**Warren Buffett:** I think many of the CEOs, some of them anyway, I should say, don't really fully comprehend that. When you get margin calls for huge amounts of money, it only has to be one day when you can't meet it. That almost happened. If you go back to October of 1987, there was a large wire transfer that didn't make it to the [...] for a while didn't make it to the clearing house in Chicago. And that came close to [unclear: "hauling"] the whole system at the time. And we were very close to closing the exchange. And a lot of things would have unraveled. The money finally showed up, but it's dangerous to have a system where people are depending on billions of dollars coming in from other people.

Well, we had that on Salomon on that Sunday in 1991. If Salomon had gone bankrupt the next day, you would have had people on the other side of 1.2 trillion of notional amount, or something like that, of derivatives who would have had a contract with a party where they would have been dealing with a bankruptcy court. You would have all kinds of security settlements that wouldn't necessarily have settled. You would have all kinds of confusion. And believe me, it would have been huge at that time, between what was going on in Japan, what was going on in the UK, and what was going on in the United States, because the accounts were all intermingled.

As a matter of fact, Salomon was banking, or was running a bank, in Germany, which took on large amounts of deposits from individuals and just loaned it all to Salomon. So it would have had a receivable from a bankrupt company and owed money to I don't know how many German depositors. There were all kinds of things that would have come out at that time, and who knows what the effect would be on the system. You don't need to put more and more of those kind of linkages and strains on an economic system that already is pretty damn leveraged.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* That's just like a margin account. And when you sign pieces of paper like that, you can go absolutely broke and into default and catastrophe, and having other people liquidating your positions under distress conditions, et cetera, et cetera. So there's a lot of irresponsible mechanics. In attempting to protect themselves, they've introduced this enormous instability into the system through all these clauses about collateral posting. And nobody seems to recognize what a disaster of a system they've created in an attempt to make each party feel safer. It's a demented system.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* And you don't get properly paid in most cases for playing the game, and therefore we're not in it [...]
