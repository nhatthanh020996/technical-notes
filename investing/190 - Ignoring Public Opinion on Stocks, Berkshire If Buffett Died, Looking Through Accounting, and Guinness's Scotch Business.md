# Ignoring Public Opinion on Stocks, Berkshire If Buffett Died, Looking Through Accounting, and Guinness's Scotch Business

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ja7JuyfrjSY

Recorded: most likely 1993 Berkshire annual meeting (low-moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why Stock Market Analysts Are Always Wrong". It is misleading. Buffett does not say analysts are always wrong; he says only that he and Munger pay no attention to what analysts, or anyone else, say about a stock, the economy or the market, because "a public opinion poll" will not make you rich and "you cannot get rich with a weather vane". That takes about one minute. The rest of the clip covers what would happen to Berkshire if Buffett died, why managements preoccupied with accounting are a negative and how Buffett and Munger recast accounting in their heads, and Guinness's long-term growth (mainly Scotch, with weak volume trends). The upload date (2025) is not the event date. The setting is a Berkshire annual meeting (microphone "zones", Borsheims mentioned, Munger beside Buffett). The year is most likely **1993** (low-moderate confidence). The clues: Berkshire still owns Guinness (bought in 1991; it merged into Diageo in 1997), and Buffett says the pound is at about $1.46 or $1.47 against an average cost of $1.80-something, which fits early-to-mid 1993 best; the pooling-versus-purchase accounting discussion is a 1990s topic; Munger says "movie theaters like this one", which suggests the meeting was held in a theater (Berkshire met in Omaha's Orpheum Theater in the early 1990s). Coca-Cola and Gillette are discussed as current holdings. The "Charlie Skurus" Munger did legal work for is kept as in the captions; he may be the movie-theater executive Charles Skouras, but this is a guess.

*(The clip starts mid-answer; the question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** It really doesn't make any difference. I mean, we don't pay any attention to what people say about Coca-Cola stock or Gillette stock or any of those things. If you talk to one person, you'd hear one thing, and talk to another [...] You really should not make decisions in securities based on what other people think. If you're doing that, you should think about doing something else, because a public opinion poll will just [...] it will not get you rich in Wall Street. So you really want to stick with businesses that you feel you can somehow evaluate yourself.

And I don't think [...] I mean, Charlie and I, we don't read anything about what business is going [...] the economy is going to do, or the market's going to do, or what anybody [...] Anytime I see some article [that] says, you know, these analysts say this or that about some business, it just doesn't mean anything to us. You cannot get rich with a weather vane.

## Part 3: Managements preoccupied with accounting, and recasting the accounts ([03:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ja7JuyfrjSY&t=229))

*(The question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** We're right now at our peak of activity. This is it. Zone four.

We regard it as a negative when we find a management that's preoccupied with accounting considerations, but we find it so frequent that we can't afford to use it as a total exclusionary factor. It really surprises me how many managements focus on accounting, and the time they spend on it. It's really unproductive. If you find a management that doesn't care about the accounting but does explain to you in clear terms what's going on, I think you should regard that as a plus in owning a security.

We think our shareholders are smart enough, particularly if we make it clear to them the accounting consequences. We think they're smart enough to look through to the economic reality of what Berkshire's businesses are all about. And I think some managements sell short their own ownership group by doing various kinds of financial acrobatics in order to have the charges come in a certain way, rather than, as you point out, often they might be better off buying for cash rather than [using] their own stock as currency. But they may prefer to use their own stock because they avoid goodwill charges. We've written a few things on goodwill in past annual reports that might get to that subject.

We don't care what accounting [...] We sort of rewrite the accounting for any business that we're looking at, because in our heads, we want to have, in effect, a standardized way of looking at businesses. And if one company goes through pooling transactions and another goes through purchase transactions, we're going to recast them in our own mind so that there's comparability. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, the published accounting results are in accordance with standard convention, and they're a place to start economic analysis. The figures are frequently quite silly on a functional basis. I'm not criticizing accounting conventions, except for some.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* Yeah.

**Charlie Munger:** But I think it's just a place to start thinking about economic reality. By their nature, they can't tie perfectly. They can't even tie very well to economic reality.
