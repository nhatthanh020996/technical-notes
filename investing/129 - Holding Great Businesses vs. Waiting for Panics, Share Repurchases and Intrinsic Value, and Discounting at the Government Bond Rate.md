# Holding Great Businesses vs. Waiting for Panics, Share Repurchases and Intrinsic Value, and Discounting at the Government Bond Rate

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZCaOKNIRV1c

Recorded: 1996 Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Buy Great Stocks And Never Sell". It is misleading: Buffett never says "never sell". He says that unless the price of a great company is "really offensive", it is a lot to count on waiting to buy it in a panic, and holds up Phil Carret as someone who did well by finding businesses he likes and sticking with them. The rest of the clip is about share repurchases (good only below intrinsic value, with Coca-Cola and GEICO as examples) and what discount rate to use when valuing a business (the long-term government rate, with no added risk premium). This is the **1996** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence). The clues: Phil Carret is introduced in the audience as 99 years old and as having written a book on investing in 1924 (he was born in 1896); Berkshire owns "just over eight percent" of Coca-Cola; Berkshire's interest in GEICO is described as having gone from about 33% to 50% through repurchases (Berkshire bought the rest of GEICO at the start of 1996); long rates are "about seven percent". The upload date (2023) is not the event date.

## Part 2: Repurchases above intrinsic value harm shareholders: Coca-Cola and GEICO ([04:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZCaOKNIRV1c&t=240))

**Questioner (Tim Medley, Jackson, Mississippi):** Mr. Buffett, Mr. Munger, I'm Tim Medley from Jackson, Mississippi. My question is on allocation of capital. You've indicated that one thing you like in companies is a willingness on the part of management to repurchase its own shares.

**Warren Buffett:** Right.

**Questioner (Tim Medley, Jackson, Mississippi):** I wonder if you would talk for a minute about your own frame of reference on repurchases when it appears that the current price of the stock is rich in relation to its intrinsic value. And some have said that, with the right company, ongoing repurchases of stock should be made irrespective of the price. So would you speak for a moment as to how you think it pencils out when the current price of the stock is rich in relation to its intrinsic value?

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. If you're repurchasing shares above a rationally calculated intrinsic value, you are harming your shareholders, just as if you issue shares beneath that figure, you are harming your shareholders. That's a truism.

Now, the tough part of that, of course, is coming up with the intrinsic value. For example, a good example might be Coca-Cola. I think a number of people might have thought Coca-Cola was repurchasing shares at a very high price, because they'll look at book value or P/E ratios. But there's a lot more to intrinsic value than book value and P/E ratios, and anytime anybody gives you some simplified formula for figuring it out, forget it. You have to understand the business.

The people who understood that business well, the management, have understood, and been very forthright about saying so over the years, that by repurchasing their shares they are adding to the value per share for remaining shareholders. And like I say, people who didn't understand Coca-Cola, or who thought mechanistic methods of valuation should take precedence, really misjudged the value to the Coca-Cola Company of those repurchases.

So when you have a wonderful business, we favor using funds that are generated at [the] business to make the business even more wonderful, and we favor repurchasing shares if those shares are below intrinsic value. And I would say that if it's a really wonderful business, we probably come up with higher intrinsic values than most people do. We have great respect, Charlie and I (I think it's developed over the years), we have enormous respect for the power of a really outstanding business, and we recognize how scarce they are. And if a management wishes to further intensify our ownership by repurchasing shares, we applaud.

We own, we just [unclear: "won"] over eight percent of the Coca-Cola Company. Probably in the last three or so months [unclear: "by a very tiny fraction"], but we had a second purchase one time. But our percentage interest in the Coca-Cola Company has gone up significantly through their repurchases, and we are better off because they have bought those shares at what looked like, to some people, perhaps high prices. And we thought they were wrong at the time, and I think now it's been indicated, or proven.

So I urge you, if you're trying to decide on the wisdom of repurchases or of share issuances, that you don't think in terms of book value, you don't think in terms of specific P/Es, you don't think in terms of any little model. But [unclear: "do you think in terms of what would you really a"] pick businesses you can understand, and then think what you really would pay to be in those businesses. And that's what counts over time: whether the repurchases are made at a discount from that figure.

And I would say, with the companies that we own shares [in], our interest in GEICO went from 33 or so percent to 50 percent over a [unclear: "oh 15 years old"] period, simply through repurchases, and we benefited significantly. So did every other shareholder, I might add, that stayed with the company, and we benefited in no way disproportionate to them. But that was a very wise action on their part. And [unclear: "there are two they were all usually"] buying that stock at at least double book value, and you could compare it to other insurance stocks and say, "Well, that's too much to pay." But GEICO wasn't an insurance company [unclear: "it was comparable to other insurance companies"; context suggests "it wasn't comparable to other insurance companies"].
