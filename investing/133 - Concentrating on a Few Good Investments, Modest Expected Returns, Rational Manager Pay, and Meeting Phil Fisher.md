# Concentrating on a Few Good Investments, Modest Expected Returns, Rational Manager Pay, and Meeting Phil Fisher

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nxHRvwB4ebc

Recorded: most likely 2004 Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence for Part 6; moderate confidence for Parts 1–5)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Charlie Munger: Why You Should Buy Stocks And Never Sell". It is misleading: nobody says you should never sell. The closest remark is Buffett's point that someone who owned stocks "right straight through" the 20th century without leverage would have gotten "a perfectly decent return". Munger talks about concentrating on a few good investments, modest future returns, Les Schwab's compensation systems and Phil Fisher; most of the clip is about manager compensation and Phil Fisher. The clip opens with a short teaser (Munger's line about concentrating on a few investments), which is repeated at the very end of the video. The Phil Fisher answer is from the **2004** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence): Buffett says Fisher "died maybe a month ago", and Fisher died in March 2004. The other answers are most likely from the same meeting (moderate confidence): Munger refers to "very dramatically high returns" that lasted "up to about three years ago" (the 2000 peak), and Buffett mentions a big incentive for the managers at MidAmerican. The questions for Parts 1–5 are not included.

## Part 2: Modest returns ahead, and people living in a fool's paradise ([01:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nxHRvwB4ebc&t=68))

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, I think [...] but it's also true that both of us have said at various times over the last three years that we wouldn't be at all surprised if professionally invested money in America had a pretty modest result over a fairly extended period of the future, compared to the very dramatically high returns that it had achieved up to about three years ago. And so far that's been proved out to be pretty much right.

**Warren Buffett:** Certain stretches are easier than other stretches. Yeah. Our expectations were more modest than most people a few years ago. We didn't say the world was coming to an end or anything. We just said that people have gone crazy in certain sectors, and that anybody that thought that you could sit at home and day trade and make double-digit returns over time, or do anything that you were entitled to by just sticking a little money in your 401(k) or something, was really living in a fool's paradise. But that was never accompanied by any predictions of disaster for the American economy as a whole or for American business as a whole.

People get crazy notions from time to time in financial markets, and [unclear: "know I comment on this earlier"], but they just believe things that [...] it's hard to understand how they can believe. Now, to some extent they get sold by other people. But American business really has never let investors down as a group, but investors have done themselves in quite frequently.

## Part 3: Paying for performance in an easy business: the TV station example ([02:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nxHRvwB4ebc&t=145))

*(The clip jumps into a different answer, about compensation; the question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** If you had a group of television stations, just to pick an example, let's say they were network television stations, all of a reasonable size, you would probably figure that a chimpanzee could run the place and have 35% pre-tax margins, and you might want to pay for performance above some number like that. But it's silly to have something that starts at 10% or 15% when you do that. And a lousy manager will always suggest an arrangement like that.

## Part 4: Paying great managers well; Les Schwab, Walmart and Costco ([03:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nxHRvwB4ebc&t=200))

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie and I have seen all kinds of compensation arrangements where basically you get paid for showing up, but they try to make it look, by constructing some mathematics around it, like you really had to achieve something. But in the end, if you get a great manager, you want to pay him very well. We've got great managers, for example, at a place like MidAmerican, and somebody mentioned that there's a big carrot out there for them if they achieve the results that we've set out. And that will be a check I'll be very happy to write. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah. If you want to read one book that will demonstrate really shrewd compensation systems in a whole chain of small businesses, read the autobiography of Les Schwab, who had a bunch of tire shops, has a bunch of tire shops all over the Northwest. And he made a huge fortune in one of the world's really difficult businesses by having shrewd systems, and he can tell you a lot better than we can.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. And he worked that out himself. I mean, it's an interesting book, and selling tires, how do you make any money doing that? And [...]

**Charlie Munger:** Hundreds of millions selling tires.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. Yep. And people like Sam Walton. I mean, the compensation system, I will guarantee you, at Walmart, or Charlie's involved in Costco, they're going to be rational, because you had very rational people running them, and they wanted to attract good managers and they wanted to get the best out of them, and they had no use in paying for mediocrity. But that does require a knowledge of the business. I mean, if you don't understand a business, you're going to have a problem with both the manager and the consultant in terms of getting [unclear: "flims"] on how you pay people.

## Part 6: Meeting Phil Fisher, and getting it from the books ([06:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nxHRvwB4ebc&t=372))

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, Phil Fisher was a great man. He died maybe a month ago or thereabouts, well into his 90s. His first book, I believe it was *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*, was written in 1958, and the second book was written a few years later. Those two books were terrific books. And as with Ben Graham, you could really get it all by reading the books.

I met Phil Fisher just once. And it was great. I enjoyed it. I loved it. He was nice to me. But similarly, actually, to my experiences with Ben Graham, where I worked for him, I took his class and everything else, it was in the books. I mean, they were such good writers and their thoughts were so clear that you didn't need to meet them personally. I enjoyed meeting them personally, obviously, but they got it across in words.

And the only time I met Phil was sometime after that 1962 book, or whatever it was, '61 or '62, and I was in San Francisco. I think it was in the [unclear: "Rust building"]. I may be wrong on that. And I just went there. I used to do that all the time when I was younger. I'd go to New York and I'd just drop in on all kinds of people, and I guess they thought because I was from Omaha that, you know, one time and they'd be rid of me. And I would usually get in to see him, and I did that with Phil, and [he] was extraordinarily nice to me. But it wasn't that I gained new ideas, however, by meeting him, because I'd already read it in his books.

And Charlie [...] actually, I met Charlie in 1959, and Charlie was sort of preaching the Fisher doctrine also to me, a little different form, but his ideas paralleled those of Phil. So I was sort of getting it from both sides, and [it] made a lot of sense to me. I don't know what Charlie's experiences were with Phil, but [...]

**Charlie Munger:** Well, I always like it when somebody who's attractive to me agrees with me. And therefore I've got very fond memories of Phil Fisher. The basic idea that it was hard to find good stocks and it was hard to find good investments and that you wanted to be in good investments, and therefore you just find a few of them that you knew a lot about and concentrate on those, seemed to me such an obviously good idea. And indeed it's proved to be an obviously good idea, yet 98% of the investing world doesn't follow it. That's been good for us. It's been good for you.
