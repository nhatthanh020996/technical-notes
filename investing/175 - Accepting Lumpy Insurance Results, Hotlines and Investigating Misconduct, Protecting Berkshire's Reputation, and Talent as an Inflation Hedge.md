# Accepting Lumpy Insurance Results, Hotlines and Investigating Misconduct, Protecting Berkshire's Reputation, and Talent as an Inflation Hedge

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-gWgZhH3Tk

Recorded: Parts 3-5 most likely 2011 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence); Parts 1-2 and 6 likely Berkshire meetings from roughly 2008-2012, possibly the same meeting (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why You Should Own Stocks With Volatile Earnings". It is misleading: nobody talks about which stocks you should own. Munger and Buffett explain that Berkshire's insurance business deliberately accepts occasional big annual losses (a Katrina-size loss) in exchange for larger, lumpier earnings, which is a competitive advantage, not stock advice. The rest of the clip is about the hotline and investigating misconduct at subsidiaries, Buffett's biennial letter to managers about reputation, Munger on celebrating wealth "only when it's been fairly won and wisely used", and inflation, where Buffett says talent cannot be inflated away. The clip appears to be a compilation of segments. Parts 3-5 are most likely from the **2011** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence). The clues: Buffett says Berkshire has 260,000 people (Berkshire reported about 260,000 employees at the end of 2010); he mentions a new line he added to the letter to managers about doing something only because "the other guy is doing it" (in the 2010 memo); the question about unethical conduct at a subsidiary and his remark that people have sometimes covered things up fit the David Sokol affair of spring 2011 (a guess); Sarbanes-Oxley hotlines are in place. Parts 1-2 and 6 have no firm date: the Katrina loss (2005) is mentioned as past, and Buffett hands a question to "Carol", which points to the journalist panel years (2008 onward), with Carol Loomis.

## Part 1: Willingness to endure lumpy annual results as a competitive advantage ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-gWgZhH3Tk&t=0))

*(The clip starts mid-answer; the question is not included.)*

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* I would say that the main difference between our practice and that of most other people is that we are deliberately seeking a method of operation which will give us occasional big losses in a single year. Big overall losses. And everybody else is trying to avoid that. And we just want to be rich enough so a big loss in a single year is a blip. And that's a competitive advantage, that willingness to endure fluctuating annual results.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* No one else is going to pick up on [it]. I mean, they know what we do. They just don't want to do it, or they're unable to do it in terms of financial resources. So I would say that comes very close to a permanent and substantial advantage at Berkshire.

I don't forget about [it], and you shouldn't forget about it, but forget about the human suffering and all that. Just the financial consequences of a Katrina: when we lose 3 billion in that, I don't feel any different the next day than I felt the day before, financially. I mean, it just doesn't make any difference, because we are in that particular game. And as long as we make the right decisions over time in terms of the premiums we get, and as long as we never expose ourselves to a loss that would really shake up our capital structure or anything, that is a game in which we have a huge competitive edge. And it gets wider every year.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* So, you know, risk. We are in the business, in insurance, of taking the other guy's desire to smooth their earnings, and in exchange get what we think are larger, lumpier earnings over time. We like the business. Carol?

**Unidentified speaker:** Well, go ahead, Charlie.

**Charlie Munger:** Well, I'd say Warren has a different position than a lot of other people in the insurance business. After a year in which Berkshire has a big loss, he can look into his shaving mirror and say, "Your shareholder still loves you."

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, Charlie.

**Charlie Munger:** Other people are not in that position.

**Warren Buffett:** Charlie and I knew a guy from Omaha who, 40 or 50 years ago, was one of the richest guys in the United States, named Howard Ahmanson. And Howard had a fetish about owning 100% of everything that he came in contact with. And so, when asked why, he said, "I like to look in the mirror and say all my shareholders love me." And I'm not quite that extreme, but I like to look in the mirror and say enough of my shareholders love me.

## Part 4: The letter to managers: "we won't trade reputation for money" ([04:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-gWgZhH3Tk&t=260))

**Warren Buffett:** We have a letter that goes out every roughly 2 years. It's the only communication. I probably ought to put a copy of it in the annual report sometimes, so that the shareholders see it, but it's a page and a half long. It asks the manager to tell me who, if something happened to them that night, who I should consider putting in charge of the place the next day. Doesn't mean I'll follow their advice, but I want to know their reasons and the pluses and minuses.

But it starts off basically, and it says, "Look, we've got all the money we need. We'd like to make more money. We love making money, but we don't have a shred more of reputation than we need, and we won't trade reputation for money." And we want that message to get out. It's the reason we stick that Salomon thing in the movie every year. I mean, some of you can probably recite it by now, but I don't think it can hurt to keep repeating that story.

And we tell them that message, and then I've added a new line in this, and I say, "If the reason you're doing something, the only [...] the best reason you can come up with, is that the other guy is doing it, it's not good enough. There's got to be some other reason besides the other fellow's doing it, or you're in trouble."

And I tell them, "Call me if anything's questionable. If you think it's close to the line, give me a call." By saying that, nobody gives me a call, because they know that [the fact] that they think it's that close to the line probably tells them it's over the line. But I want to hear about stuff. We can cure any problem if we hear about it soon enough and take action soon enough. But if it's allowed to fester out someplace, and people cover it up, and sometimes they have, then we got a problem. And we will have more of that in the future. There's no getting around it. If you have 260,000 people, there're going to be some things going on.

I just hope we hear about them fast, and I hope their managers hear about them even faster and do something before it even gets to us. But we want very much to protect the reputation of Berkshire. It's the right thing to do. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, it is absolutely essential. And Berkshire, [unclear: "average out"], has a very good reputation, as you can tell by the ratings from major media and surveys, and that is absolutely precious to us.

## Part 5: Celebrating wealth only when fairly won and wisely used ([06:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-gWgZhH3Tk&t=405))

**Charlie Munger:** In a sense, you people are part of the culture, too. The ideal is not to just make all the money that can legally be made without causing too much legal trouble. The idea is bigger than that. It's that we celebrate wealth only when it's been fairly won and wisely used. And if that idea pervades the culture of the place, including the shareholders, we think that's very helpful to us.

## Part 6: Inflation, and why talent can't be inflated away ([07:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-gWgZhH3Tk&t=448))

*(This segment starts mid-answer; the question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** I would say if inflation ever really gets in the saddle, that it gets very unpredictable as to how fast it can accelerate and how faith in institutions can break down. A lot of bad things could happen with it. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, the best defense, of course, is to contribute as much as you can to the civilization, and expect to counter inflation's effects by your [...] That's the safest antidote. The idea that just by outsmarting other people you can somehow profit from the inflation is a much more dangerous course of action.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. Your money can be inflated away, but your talent can't be inflated away. If you're the best brain surgeon in Omaha, or the best painter, or whatever it may be, you will always command your share of the resources around you. And whether the currency is seashells or 10 million dollar notes or whatever it may be, talent is a terrific asset to deal with any kind of a monetary situation.

But Charlie and I have to fall back on money.
