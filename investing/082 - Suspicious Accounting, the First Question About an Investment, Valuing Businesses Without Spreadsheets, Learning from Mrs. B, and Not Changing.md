# Suspicious Accounting, the First Question About an Investment, Valuing Businesses Without Spreadsheets, Learning from Mrs. B, and Not Changing

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IMdVvy6Jznk

Recorded: most likely a 1997 or 1998 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Get Rich With Simple Stocks". It is misleading: nobody talks about getting rich, and "simple stocks" is only loosely related to the actual content. The year is not stated. **Questioner (Peter Bevin, Sweden):** Hello, my name is Peter Bevin from Sweden. What is the absolutely first question you ask yourself when you look at a potential investment? And do you and Mr. Munger ask yourselves the same first question?

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, well, I don't ask myself whether Charlie's going to like it, because that will be a tough one. Now, the first question is: can I understand [it]? There's no kidding myself into thinking that I'm going to understand some software company or some biotech company or something of the sort. What the hell am I going to know about it? I mean, I can [...] So that's the first threshold question.

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, we tend to judge by the past record, by and large. If the thing has a lousy past record and a bright future, we're going to miss the opportunity.

## Part 3: How far out to project cash flows, and "organized common sense" ([04:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IMdVvy6Jznk&t=269))

**Questioner (Keith Prior, San Francisco):** Hi, I'm Keith Prior from San Francisco. I have a question. When you're valuing the companies and you discount back the future earnings, you talk about, how many years out do you generally go? And if you don't go out a general number of years, how do you arrive at that time period?

**Warren Buffett:** Well, that's a very good question. I mean, it's the heart of investing, or buying businesses, which we regard as the same thing. And it is the framework in which we operate. I mean, we are trying to look at businesses in terms of what kind of cash can they produce if we're buying all of them, or will they produce if we're buying part of them, and there's a difference. And then at what discount rate do we bring it back.

And I think your question was how far out do we look and all that. Despite the fact that we can define that in a very kind of simple and direct equation, we've never actually sat down and written out a set of numbers to relate that equation. We do it in our heads, in a way. Obviously, I mean, that's what it's all about. But there's no piece of paper, and there never was a piece of paper that shows what our calculation on [unclear: "hburg"] or See's Candy or the Buffalo News was in that respect.

So it would be attaching a little more scientific quality to our analysis than there really is if I gave you some [unclear: "gobble go about"]: well, we do it for 18 years and stick a terminal value on and do all of this. We are sitting in the office thinking about that question with each business or each investment, and we have discount rates in a general way in mind. But we really like the decision to be obvious enough to us that it doesn't require making a detailed calculation. It's the framework, but it's not applied in the sense that we actually fill in all the variables. Is that a fair way [of] saying [it]?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah. Berkshire is being run the way Thomas Hunt Morgan, the great Nobel laureate, ran the biology department at Caltech. He banned the Friden calculator, which was the computer of that era. And people said, "How can you do this? Every place else in Caltech we have Friden calculators going everywhere." And he said, "Well, we're picking up these great nuggets of gold just by organized common sense, and resources are short, and we're not going to resort to any damn placer mining as long as we can pick up these major aggregations of gold." That's the way Berkshire works, and I hope the placer mining era will never come.

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* Somebody once subpoenaed our staffing papers on some acquisition, and of course, not only did we not have any staffing papers, we didn't have any staff. Zone two.
