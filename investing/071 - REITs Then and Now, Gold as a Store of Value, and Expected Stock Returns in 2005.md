# REITs Then and Now, Gold as a Store of Value, and Expected Stock Returns in 2005

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ylxXOYWYqBI

Recorded: 2005 Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why REITs Are A Scam (Real Estate Investment Trusts)". It is clickbait. Nobody calls REITs a scam. Munger makes a one-line quip that REITs "have phony accounting", and Buffett's actual point is about price: REITs were cheap and unpopular around 2000, when he put the small part of his net worth held outside Berkshire into small REITs, and by now they look unattractive because property prices are high and REITs often sell at a premium. Only the first two and a half minutes are about real estate; the rest is about gold (Buffett would much prefer farmland, an apartment house or an index fund) and expected stock returns (about 6–7%, neither a bubble nor bargain valuations). The clip is from the **2005** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence): Munger says "Berkshire Hathaway sitting here in 2005". The other clues fit: the long Treasury bond yields "a little over 4.5%", and Buffett refers to his Sun Valley talk of summer 1999 and to REITs being cheap "five or six years ago" when the NASDAQ was near its high.

**Questioner (Al Henderson, Minnesota):** My name is Al Henderson from Minnesota. I'd like to thank both of you for being yourselves and doing so much for all of us, and to help enrich ourselves and everyone else. My question I have is, actually you already referred to it, that you devote very little time to looking at the total market and look for individual opportunities most. **Warren Buffett:** Yeah, every now and then, and really very infrequently, you probably can say something intelligent about markets as a whole. I mean, you do see circumstances that are extreme enough that you can make a statement that is likely to look reasonably intelligent five or 10 years later. And I've seen a few of those times in my lifetime, and I've spoken out a couple of times. I did in '69 and '74 and a few times. Most of the time you're in some in-between zone.

Obviously you get more for your money in equities now than you got, say, in the summer of 1999, which is when I delivered a talk out at Sun Valley that later got turned into an article for Fortune. But I spoke out then because it was extreme. I mean, I knew in a general way that I was going to be right, particularly in certain aspects of the market, but I didn't know when, and then I didn't know how right, or anything of the sort. I think that if you had to make a choice between owning long-term bonds, which are now yielding the Treasury only a little over 4.5%, or owning equities for the next 20 years, and you couldn't make a change in that decision, I would certainly prefer equities. So I don't think we're in bubble-type, at all, valuations in equities, and I don't think we're anywhere close, remotely close, to bargain valuations. And I don't think it's an extreme enough period that you can speak out in some very definitive way about the outlook. But if you had told me I had to go away for 20 years and choose between what's obtainable in an index fund of equities or be committed to long-term bonds, I would rather take equities. But I think you will get a chance to do something that is more screamingly intelligent in not too many years, and maybe a lot shorter, than the alternatives that you're offered now. Charlie?
