# Why Berkshire Retains Earnings, See's Candy's Surplus Cash, and Coca-Cola's Dividends and Buybacks

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6QGtmnwQZu0

Recorded: compilation. Parts 1-2: Berkshire annual meeting(s), mid-1990s, Part 2 possibly around 1995 (low confidence); Part 3: interview, undated (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Dividends Are Sometimes A Big Mistake". It is only partly accurate. Buffett does say that Coca-Cola's shareholders "probably would have been even better off" with no dividends and more buybacks, but he does not call dividends a "big mistake". His actual point is a test: a company should keep a dollar only if it can turn it into more than a dollar of market value. He explains why See's Candy and the Buffalo News would pay big dividends as stand-alone companies, and he praises Coca-Cola for doing "exactly the right thing" by reinvesting what it can, paying a dividend and buying back shares. The clip is a compilation; the upload date is not given and the parts are not clearly dated. Clues: Parts 1-2 sound like Berkshire annual meeting Q&A (a shareholder asks the question in Part 2, and Buffett says "we" and "your money"). Part 1 names Coca-Cola, Gillette and Disney together as companies with great opportunities, and See's generating about $30 million a year, which points to the mid-1990s. In Part 2, Buffett says Bill Gates has "four and a half billion of cash" in Microsoft; Microsoft's cash was around that level in about 1995 (a guess from memory, not stated in the clip). Part 3 is a different format: an interviewer asks short direct questions ("Why won't you pay a dividend to your shareholders?"), and Buffett says Berkshire is "much closer to that point... than we were 20 years ago"; its date cannot be determined.

## Part 1: The test for retaining earnings, See's Candy, and the Buffalo News ([00:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6QGtmnwQZu0&t=2))

*(The clip starts mid-answer; the question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** We don't pay dividends because we think we can turn every dollar we retain into more than a dollar of market value. I mean, the only reason for us to keep your money is if it becomes worth more by us keeping it than it would be worth if we gave it to you. And if we can create more than a dollar of market value for every dollar we keep, you're better off, whether you want to take that dollar out by selling a little piece of your stock or whether you continue to leave it in. That's the test. If we come to the conclusion that we can't do that, and we could come to the conclusion sometime, then we should distribute it to you.

The interesting thing is, we're in certain businesses, for example See's Candy being one, [where] we don't have a way to intelligently use all of the money that See's generates within the See's Candy company. So [if] See's were a stand-alone company, it would pay very large dividends, not because it just had some dividend-paying policy. It would be simply because we wouldn't have a way of using, in this case, 30 million dollars a year intelligently in expanding that business. The Buffalo News is the same way. We don't have a way of using money within that specific business intelligently, to use the money that it generates.

We hope that in the overall Berkshire Hathaway scheme of things, we can intelligently use the money that the companies in aggregate generate for us. And we think so far we have, and we think the prospects are reasonably good that we can continue to do that.

But dividend policy should really be determined by that criteria, also bearing in mind the possibilities of repurchasing stock too. But they should be determined by whether a dollar left in the business is worth more to the shareholder than a dollar paid out.

Some place like Coca-Cola, if Coca-Cola had paid no dividends and simply repurchased shares and developed the bottling system and done the things that they have, the shareholders probably would have been even better off. They've been sensationally well off as it is, but they probably would have been even better off than they have been with the dividend policy they have had. And [unclear: "that's from"] Gillette and Disney and the companies of that sort that have got these terrific opportunities to use capital within the business, or to repurchase shares of a company that simply can't be replaced, if that usually is the best use of capital.

## Part 2: Should Coca-Cola and Gillette stop paying dividends? ([02:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6QGtmnwQZu0&t=146))

*(The question is cut off at the start.)*

**Questioner:** [...] It's probably better than [unclear: "debit"]. And one of the major reasons he stated was that Berkshire has never paid a dividend, as we all know, and consequently you had superior utilization of the extra cash. Now, if you extend that reasoning, could it also be a beneficial policy if Coca-Cola and Gillette stop paying dividends and utilize the cash in other ways?

**Warren Buffett:** Well, it depends what they could use [...] how they would utilize the cash, what they could use it for. Those are more focused enterprises than Berkshire, at least in terms of products. And I think I commend managements that have a wonderful business for utilizing cash in those wonderful businesses, or in businesses that they understand and [that] will also have wonderful economics, and for getting the rest of the money back to the shareholders.

So Coca-Cola, in my book, is doing exactly the right thing with its cash. When it both [...] Well, A, it uses all the cash that it can effectively in the business, to expand in new markets and all of that sort of thing. But then beyond that, it pays the dividend, which distributes cash to shareholders, and then it repurchases shares in a big way, which returns cash on a selective basis to shareholders, but in a way that benefits all of them.

So you will benefit from us not paying dividends just as long as we can use every dollar we retain to produce more than a dollar of value, and of market value, over time. Whether we can continue doing that, how long we can continue doing that, I can't promise you. But that is the yardstick by which the decision is made, and that is the [unclear: "Archduke"] I think by which Coca-Cola is making the decision too. And I think that they deserve great credit for exercising the discipline to quit [...] when they [...] using cash when they run out of the opportunities to use it well, and then to further deploy it advantageously by repurchasing shares.

I think one of the things I admire about my friend Bill Gates: he's got four and a half billion of cash in Microsoft, and very few managements could stand having four and a half billion of cash and not doing something unintelligent with it.

## Part 3: "Why won't you pay a dividend?" ([04:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6QGtmnwQZu0&t=288))

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* So far it's made sense for us to retain everything we earn, and I think it'll make sense for a while longer, but it may not make sense [unclear: "in depth"].

**Interviewer:** Why won't you pay a dividend to your shareholders?

**Warren Buffett:** Well, we think our shareholders five years from now will be wealthier, counting what they would get from the reinvestment of the dividend. We think they'll be wealthier if we hold on to the money. Now, we may be wrong in that, but historically we've been right, and if we'd paid a dividend over the years, our shareholders would be considerably poorer than they are. And as long as we [...] I think we will have the opportunity to invest dollars to create more than a dollar of present value. We'll retain the money, and when we don't think we can do that, we'll either repurchase stock or pay dividends.

**Interviewer:** Is there no way you will ever pay a dividend?

**Warren Buffett:** Someday Berkshire will pay a dividend, because it'll get so large that they can't use the money, or all of the money that it earns, effectively. But we don't think we're at that point, although we're much closer to that point, obviously, than we were 20 years ago.
