# Downsizing and Bloat, Where Berkshire Parks Its Cash, Y2K, and Predicting Berkshire's Businesses

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=76xlexyhHjk

Recorded: most likely 1996 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Why Job Losses Make Investors Rich". It is misleading: nobody says job losses make investors rich. Buffett says getting more output per worker is in society's interest but hard on the individuals, that he quarrels with how layoffs are sometimes done, and that Berkshire avoids taking on people it doesn't need so it doesn't have to lay them off; Munger says bloat has ruined far more businesses than "over-downsizing". The rest of the clip is about short-term cash (Penn Central's commercial paper), the year-2000 computer problem, and how predictable Berkshire's businesses are. The clip is most likely from the **1996** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence). The clues: Buffett calls the attention to downsizing recent and "a media fad based on some particularly dramatic examples at a couple of companies" (the downsizing debate peaked in early 1996); World Book has fewer people than "a year or two ago"; Dexter Shoe is still praised; a shareholder asks about the year-2000 computer problem as a future issue; Peter Bevelin from Sweden asks a question. The "Zone one / Zone three / on four" calls suggest all four parts come from the same meeting, but a nearby year (1997) can't be ruled out. The upload date (2023) is not the event date.

## Part 1: Downsizing, efficiency and the people affected ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=76xlexyhHjk&t=0))

*(The clip starts at the beginning of the answer; the question itself is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** Historically, every industry at all times is interested in downsizing or becoming more efficient. Now, if the industry is growing, you can achieve efficiency by doing more work or turning out more output with the same people. But if you go back 150 years and look at the percentage of people in farming, for example, farming has downsized from being a very appreciable percentage of the American workforce to a very small percentage. And essentially, that's released people to do other things. So it's in the interests of society to get as much output in anything as it can per unit of labor input. It's very difficult on the individual involved. And it's no fun. I guess it's no fun being a horse when the tractor comes along, or a blacksmith when the car comes along.

But the [...] So I don't quarrel with the activities. I quarrel sometimes with how it's done. And I do think there's been a certain lack of, in certain cases, some empathy or sensitivity in terms of the way it's being done. You should try to make [unclear: "in cases some empathy or sensitivity in terms of the way it's being done. You should try to make that will require us of people over time"] because we hope that physical output grows and that we become more productive and can keep the same number of people to get greater output.

Dexter Shoe has done a great job of that over time. They've become more and more productive, but they've sold more shoes instead of selling the same number of shoes and letting people go. But sometimes industry trends [...] I mean, at World Book, we have fewer people than we had a year or two ago, and we don't have any answer to that. Over time we got out of the textile business. I wish we didn't have to, but we did not know how to run a textile company in New England and compete effectively.

Like I say, I love avoiding those businesses, and to the extent we can, we will. I mean, GEICO is going to add people over time, and I think Berkshire Hathaway's going to add people over time. But I can't [...] But it is in the interest of society to do jobs more effectively. It's also in the interest of society, it seems to me, to take care in some way of the people that are affected by that activity. In some cases it may be retraining, but in other cases it doesn't work so well if you're 55 years old and you've been working in a textile mill all your life and all of a sudden the guy that runs the place can't make any money out of selling your output. I mean, that's not the fellow's fault that [has] been working in the textile mill for over 30 years.

So there's a balance in that. I think the attention that's come about lately, I think to some degree it was a media fad based on some particularly dramatic examples at a couple of companies. I don't think there is more displacement going on now as a percentage of the labor force annually than there was 10 years ago, in terms of reconstituting what people do, but it's gotten a lot of attention lately. There could be a backlash on that in terms of corporate tax rates or a number of things, and we might feel it in that direction.

We wanted Berkshire to do everything as efficiently as we can. Part of that, in a big way, is not taking on a lot of people we don't need. A lot of the mistakes that are being corrected now are because people got very fat, and their businesses got very fat in the past and took on all kinds of people they don't need. We see that in a lot of businesses that we're exposed to, and as long as everybody prospers, really no one does very much about it, and then when the time comes they all of a sudden find out they can get way more output. The oil companies are a classic example.

The people actually needed to produce, refine and market oil probably hasn't changed that much, but if you look at the employment relative to the barrels produced, refined and marketed, it's gone down dramatically [unclear: "over 20 years ago"]. To me, it just means that they weren't being run that well 20 years ago, and it never should have occurred in the first place. We don't want to take on more people than [unclear: "never should have occurred in the first place. We don't want to take on more people than businesses"] because we don't want to lay people off either. Charlie?

But if you ask me to name businesses that were half ruined or ruined by bloat, I could just rattle off name after name after name. It's gotten fashionable to assume that downsizing is wrong. Well, it may have been wrong to let the business get so fat that it eventually had to downsize. ## Part 2: Where Berkshire's uninvested cash sits, and the Penn Central lesson ([06:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=76xlexyhHjk&t=376))

*(This is the same exchange as Parts 4–5 of [205 – Concentration Versus Diversification, Where Berkshire Parks Its Cash, and Buffett's Personal Safety](<205 - Concentration Versus Diversification, Where Berkshire Parks Its Cash, and Buffett's Personal Safety.md>), cut from a different upload.)*

**Questioner (Nelson Coburn, Silver Spring, Maryland):** Good afternoon, Mr. Buffett. I'm Nelson Coburn from Silver Spring, Maryland. I have one question I want to ask that hasn't come up here yet. Where does the money sit that comes in, say, from dividends and whatever other income comes into Berkshire, that you're waiting to invest someplace else? Is it get[ting] someplace where it's taking in a profit, or is it just sitting gathering dust?

**Warren Buffett:** We only have about four or five commercial paper names we accept. We're very picky about where we put [it]; the money all gets invested. We do not have anything sitting around in the safe or any place else. So it's all invested. But we do not get venturesome in the least in terms of where our short-term money goes. So we only have, like I say, maybe four or five approved names on commercial paper, all of which I approve. I mean, if anything ever goes haywire on this, it's my fault.

Right now, we have maybe a billion and something in relatively short-term Treasuries, and we have a little extra in some commercial paper, maybe. But you will never see us reaching for an extra eighth of a percent on short-term yields.

Some of you may remember the fiasco of Penn Central in the commercial paper market. Penn Central, around 1970 or thereabouts, was paying a quarter of a point, as I remember, more than other commercial paper issuers. And of course they [unclear: "won they"], despite showing a positive net worth, I think, of a billion and a half or so. They said they had a lot of net worth but no cash. Turned out cash was more important, and so they defaulted.

Now the interesting thing about doing that is, if you're getting a quarter of a point extra, and you came over on the Mayflower and you landed and you said, "Well, I'm going to apply myself to getting a quarter of a point extra on short-term money," and you didn't make any mistake until you got to Penn Central, [unclear: "you would sign for the"] compounding aspect, you would be behind at that point. And I don't like a business where you can do right for 300 years and then make one mistake and be behind.

So we are very picky about short-term paper. But it is all invested, and when it's large amounts it probably will be in Treasuries. [There are] a couple of firms whose commercial paper we take. David Coles, Appleton, Wisconsin.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, I think they're far easier to predict than most businesses.
