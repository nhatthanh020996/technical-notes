# Why Investment Management Adds No Value in Aggregate, 2-and-20 Fees, Picking the Rare Good Manager, State Pension Funds, and Nuclear Terrorism

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xQwJP5Tw_v8

Recorded: most likely 2006 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Never Listen to Financial Advisors". It is misleading: Buffett never says that. He says the investment profession *in aggregate* adds nothing while charging an estimated $140 billion a year, that 2-and-20 fees cannot work out for investors as a group, and that salespeople will all claim to be the exception. But he also says that "occasionally you can make a very intelligent choice in picking an investment manager" if you know the person well, citing his 1969 referrals to Bill Ruane and Sandy Gottesman. The last third of the clip is about something else: a question on the economic impact of a nuclear terrorist attack. The clip is most likely from the **2006** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate-high confidence). The clues: the $140 billion estimate of annual investment costs is from Buffett's 2005 shareholder letter (the "Gotrocks" section), which he refers to as "the report"; his public offer to bet that 10 large hedge funds would not beat the S&P 500 over 10 years after fees dates from around 2006 (the Protégé bet followed in 2008); "the Dr. Khans of the world" refers to A.Q. Khan, whose nuclear network was exposed in 2004; and he speaks of "six billion people in the world".

## Part 2: The investment profession does not, and price as a signal of value ([00:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xQwJP5Tw_v8&t=23))

**Warren Buffett:** In aggregate, the investment profession does not do that. So you have a huge group of people making, I put the estimate as $140 billion a year, that in aggregate are and can only accomplish what somebody can do, you know, in 10 minutes a year by themselves. And it's hard to think of another business like that. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** I can't think of any. No.

**Warren Buffett:** But it's become a bigger and bigger business. And as I've pointed out in the report, the main thing that's been learned is that the more you charge, at least temporarily, the more money you bring in; that people have this idea that price equals value. It's useful to get into a business like that.

Sometimes, if I'm talking to the people at a business school, I ask them to name me a great business. And of course, one of the great businesses is a business school, because basically the more you charge, the more your prestige is, to some extent. And people think that a business school that charges 50,000 a year of tuition is going to be better than what charges 10,000 a year of tuition. There's some of that [...] well, there's a lot of it that's gotten into the investment field recently.

And you now have certain large portions of investment management that are charging fees that in aggregate cannot work out for investors. Now, obviously some do, you know, but you cannot be paying people 2% and 20%, where they get it in the good years and they fold their partnerships and start another one if they have a bad year, and that sort of thing. You can't have that coming out of an economy that's only going to produce, we'll say, you know, 7% or something like that a year for investors, and have people net better off. It isn't going to work.

## Part 3: The bet, and how he picked good managers in 1969 ([02:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xQwJP5Tw_v8&t=161))

**Warren Buffett:** And then the question that you will have is: how do I pick out the few exceptions? And everyone that calls upon you to sell you this will tell you that they are an exception.

And I am willing to bet a significant sum of money, [and] will put it up, to anybody who wants to name 10 partnerships that are $500 million or more of management, and pit those after fees against the S&P over a 10-year period. And, you know, it gets away from the survivorship bias and all that kind of a thing. But we [...] and it isn't going to happen. But a few will [...] a few will do well. They're bound to do well.

And actually, I think I do know how to pick a few that will do well. I mean, I did it in the past. When I wound up my own partnership in 1969, I told people to go to either Bill Ruane or Sandy Gottesman, and that would have been a very good decision, whichever place they went. So if you know enough about the person, know enough [about] how they've done in the past, know enough about their personality and honesty and a whole bunch of things, I think that occasionally you can make a very intelligent choice in picking an investment manager.

But I don't think you can do it if you're sitting running a pension fund in some state and you have 50 people calling on you. You're going to go with the ones that are the best salespeople and not the ones that are the best investors. Charlie?

## Part 5: The risk of a nuclear attack on a U.S. city ([04:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xQwJP5Tw_v8&t=291))

**Questioner:** Please gaze into your crystal ball. As an investor, I want to know how to address the risk of nuclear terrorism in the United States. Consider a scenario where terrorists have detonated a nuclear device in a major U.S. city. I know there would be a terrible cost in human lives. Gentlemen, what would happen to our economy? How would it respond? How resilient would it be? Thank you.

**Warren Buffett:** Well, it would certainly depend on the extent of it. If you're asking how to profit from that, there's probably some dealer that will sell you mortality derivatives, but I'm not sure that's what we would be thinking about. No, I agree with you. [unclear: "Enorm"] [...] I couldn't agree with you more about that being the ultimate problem of mankind. Not necessarily a terrorist-type usage, but a state-sponsored usage of weapons of mass destruction. And it will happen someday. The extent to which it will happen, where it will happen, who knows?

But we have always had evil people. We've always had people who wish evil on others. And, you know, thousands of years ago, if you were psychotic or a religious fanatic or a malcontent and you wished evil on your neighbor, you picked up a rock and threw it at them, and that was about the damage you could do. But we went on to bows and arrows and cannons and a few things. But since 1945, the potential for inflicting enormous harm on incredible numbers of people has increased, you know, at a geometric pace. So it is the problem of mankind. It may happen here. It may happen someplace else.

People say, sometimes they say, well, you know, if we solve poverty, we'd solve this. Well, I would just remind you that nuclear weapons have only been used twice, and those were by the richest country in the world, [the] United States, in 1945. So people will justify their use under some circumstances. If they feel threatened, they will justify them for religious reasons. They will do all kinds of crazy things.

And what holds it in check is the degree to which the lack of knowledge of how to do it is controlled, and the degree to which the materials are controlled, and [the degree to] which the deliverability is circumscribed. And we're losing ground on all of those fronts. The knowledge is more widespread. You know, the possibility of getting your hands on materials, you know, the Dr. Khans of the world and so on, has increased. And it's a real problem. But we won't be thinking about what Berkshire did that day in the stock market.

And I don't know [...] I don't know how money attacks that. I mean, I've always saw that as the top priority, I think [it] should be the top priority for philanthropy, in my particular case. But it's a very difficult [...] it's a worst-case problem. You know, you have six billion people in the world, and have a certain percentage of them who are one way or another a little crazy or very crazy, and some of whom [...] and that craziness would manifest itself by trying to do great harm to a lot of people. And only one of them has to succeed.

I don't know how many we've intercepted over the years. I'm sure [we've] intercepted a lot of incipient ones. But it is a worst-case problem, and one will succeed at some point, and it may be state-sponsored, it may be terrorist. But, you know, Berkshire is better set to survive than anybody else, but it won't make much difference.
