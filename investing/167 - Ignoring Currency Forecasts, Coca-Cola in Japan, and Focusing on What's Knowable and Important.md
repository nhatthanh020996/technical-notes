# Ignoring Currency Forecasts, Coca-Cola in Japan, and Focusing on What's Knowable and Important

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RJDXpJLpOeo

Recorded: most likely a Berkshire annual meeting around 1999–2005, best guess 2000 (low confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Forex Movements Are Impossible To Predict". It is roughly accurate but overstated: Buffett says "foreign exchange baffles me", that currency "might be important but we don't think it's knowable", and that "we don't understand what currencies are going to do week to week or month to month or year to year". He does not say they are impossible to predict, and most of the clip is about why Berkshire focuses on whether more people will drink Coca-Cola (vending machines and Georgia Coffee in Japan, the price of a Coke since the 1930s) rather than on currency or interest-rate moves. The clip opens with a short excerpt (about 35 seconds) that is repeated later in the clip. It is from a Berkshire annual meeting (two shareholder questions, with Charlie Munger present), but the year is uncertain (low confidence). The clues: the yen is said to have gone from 360 to "70 some" at the low and "back up to 140 some" (the yen was in the 140s in 1998), and "now I don't know 105 or whatever"; Gillette is still described as a company Berkshire invests in (it was acquired by Procter & Gamble in 2005); the questioner says a weak dollar and falling interest rates help Coca-Cola and Gillette and that "the opposite" is happening now; Buffett says the early 1980s were "in recent years"; and he says that if he thought the dollar would weaken dramatically "we won't get those kind of thoughts", which fits the years before Berkshire took large foreign-currency positions (from 2002) better than the years after. A yen around 105 fits roughly 1999–2000 or 2004–2005; my best guess is the 2000 meeting.

## Part 1: Opening excerpt, and "Do we have a dollar problem?" ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RJDXpJLpOeo&t=0))

*(The clip opens with an excerpt of Buffett's later answer, which is repeated in full in Part 3.)*

**Warren Buffett:** We always try to figure on what [...] focus on what's knowable and what's important. Now, currency might be important, but we don't think it's knowable. Other things are unimportant but knowable. But what really counts is what's knowable and important. What's knowable and important about Coca-Cola is the fact that more and more people are going to consume soft drinks around the world and have been doing so year after year after year, and [that] Coca-Cola is going to gain share, and that the product is extraordinarily inexpensive relative to the pleasure it brings to people.

**Questioner:** Do you think we have a dollar problem, or [do] the Japanese have a [unclear: "young brother"; context suggests "yen problem"]?

**Warren Buffett:** Well, I'm gonna let Charlie answer that.

**Charlie Munger:** I have no comment.

**Warren Buffett:** That's probably a very good question. But the trouble is, anytime I say that's a very good question, it's probably because I don't know the answer. And I don't know the answer to that. Foreign exchange baffles me, frankly. I mean, I think in terms of purchasing power parity, because that's a natural way to approach it, but purchasing power parity does not work very well as a guide to how exchange values will behave in any shorter [...] medium, or maybe even long term, because the world adapts in different ways. Sometimes it adapts by high rates of inflation to a sinking currency. Usually it does. It hasn't done that in respect to ours, but we're only sinking relative to a couple of other important currencies.

## Part 2: Why Berkshire wouldn't play currencies through Coca-Cola and Gillette, and the yen's huge moves ([01:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RJDXpJLpOeo&t=96))

**Questioner:** A lot of the companies that you invest in, like Coca-Cola and Gillette, seem to do better when the dollar is weak and interest rates are falling. That seems to be the opposite of what's happening now. So how is Berkshire positioning itself to take advantage of the current economic position, with that assessment in mind?

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah, well, that's a good question. But if we thought we knew what the dollar was going to do, or interest rates were going to do, we would [unclear: "we won't do it"] but we would just engage in transactions involving those commodities, in effect, or futures, directly. In other words, it would not be [...] if we thought that the dollar was going to weaken dramatically (and we won't get those kind of thoughts) but if we did, we would buy other currencies. And it might benefit Coke in dollar terms if that happened, but it would be so much more efficient directly to pursue a currency play or an interest rate play than an indirect way through companies that have big international exposure. We would probably do it directly.

We don't really think much about that, because just take currency: if you look at what the yen has traded at over the last [...] well, since World War II, from what was it, 360, down to what, 70 some, Charlie, even at the low, and back up to 140 some, and now, I don't know, 105 or whatever it may be. I mean, those moves are huge. But in the end we're really more interested in whether more people in Japan are going to drink Coca-Cola. And over time we're better at predicting that than we are predicting what the yen will do.

And if Coca-Cola satisfies people's liquid needs more for more and more people, we will probably get a reasonable percentage of their purchasing power of those people around the world for their right to drink Coca-Cola, or for shaving, or whatever it may be. So if the world's standard of living improves bit by bit over time, in an irregular fashion, and we supply something the world wants, we will get our share in dollars eventually. And quarter to quarter or year to year, how that moves around because of currency moves really doesn't make any difference to us. It makes a difference to reported earnings in that quarter, but in terms of where Coca-Cola is going to be 10 or 20 years from now, it would be a big mistake, I think, to focus on currency moves as opposed to focusing on the product itself.

And Japan offers a good example of that, because you had this [...] I mean, you really had a move from 360 or whatever it was to the high 70s or thereabouts. I mean, that is an incredible move in currency, and it can overshadow in the short run even what's happening in the business. But long range, what's really made Coca-Cola strong in Japan is the fact that the Japanese people have accepted their products in a big way, and Coca-Cola has built this tremendous, for example, vending machine presence. And the Japanese market is very different than all the rest of the markets in the world, virtually, in that such a high percentage flows through vending machines. And my memory is that we may have something like 900 and some thousand out of something over 2 million vending machines in the country. So we've got this tremendously dominant position. It's a little like billboards might be in this country. Plus we have this terrific product, Georgia Coffee, which is huge over there. And that's the sort of thing we focus on, because that's something we understand. We don't understand what currencies are going to do week to week or month to month or year to year.

## Part 3: What's knowable and important: a Coke in the 1930s and now ([04:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RJDXpJLpOeo&t=281))

**Warren Buffett:** And we always try to figure on what [...] focus on what's knowable and what's important. Now, currency might be important, but we don't think it's knowable. Other things are unimportant but knowable. But what really counts is what's knowable and important. What's knowable and important about Coca-Cola is the fact that more and more people are going to consume soft drinks around the world and have been doing so year after year after year, and [that] Coca-Cola is going to gain share, and that the product is extraordinarily inexpensive relative to the pleasure it brings to people.

Coca-Cola in the 30s, when I was a kid, I bought [unclear: "four"] six for a quarter and sold them for a nickel each. That was a six and a half ounce bottle, for a nickel, a Coke. And you can buy a 12 ounce can now, if you pick a supermarket sale, for not much more than twice per ounce what it was selling for in the 30s. You won't find any products where that kind of a value proposition has developed over the years. So that's the kind of thing we focus on. And interest rates and foreign exchange rates, important as they may be in the short term, really are not going to determine whether we get rich over time.

## Part 4: The early-1980s lesson, and willful agnosticism ([06:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RJDXpJLpOeo&t=377))

**Warren Buffett:** The best time to buy stocks, actually, in recent years, has been when interest rates were sky high, and it looked like a very safe thing to do to put your money into Treasury bills at [unclear: "Fort"] [...] well, actually the prime rate got up to 21.5, but you could put out money at huge rates in the early 80s. And as attractive as that appeared, it was exactly the wrong thing to be doing. It was better to be buying equities at that time, because when interest rates changed, their values changed even much more. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, we have a willful agnosticism on all kinds of things, and that makes us concentrate on certain other things. This is a very good way to think, if you're as lazy as we are.
