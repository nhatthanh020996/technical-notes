# Nike and Reebok vs. Brown and Dexter, Avoiding Difficult Problems, Ordinary Things for Exceptional Results, and Ignoring Sellers' Projections

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=POliree5GJ8

Recorded: most likely 1994 Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Why Warren Buffett Doesn't Own Nike". It is misleading: Buffett explicitly says he is "not expressing a negative view in any way" on Nike or Reebok and does not give a reason for not owning Nike beyond saying he hasn't done the work and finds their long-term competitive position harder to understand than that of Berkshire's own shoe companies (Brown and Dexter). The rest of the clip is about preferring easy problems ("we'd rather multiply by three than by pi"), Munger's warning against thinking you can do anything difficult by hiring "somebody with a label", Buffett's point that you don't have to do exceptional things to get exceptional results, and both of them dismissing sellers' projections ("don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut"). The clip is most likely from the **1994** Berkshire annual meeting (moderate confidence). The clues: Berkshire owns both Brown (bought 1991) and Dexter (bought late 1993); the questioner refers to "the risk factors that you mentioned in the annual report this year" alongside Coke and Gillette, which matches the discussion of risk in the 1993 shareholder letter; and Buffett's "three billion dollars pre-tax better off than we were a few years ago" (apparently about the product he is holding, presumably Coca-Cola, my guess) fits the size of the Coca-Cola gain at the end of 1993. ## Part 1: Nike and Reebok vs. Brown and Dexter ([00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=POliree5GJ8&t=0))

**Questioner:** You mentioned earlier that Berkshire's shoe business was great, but that other shoe businesses were not so good. What are the uncertainties of the global brand leaders? The Berkshire seems to like [...] they like Coke and Gillette. The global brand leaders in the shoe business being Nike and Reebok, what are their uncertainties in terms of long-term competitive advantage, business economics, consumer behavior, and the other risk factors that you mentioned in the annual report this year? Thank you.

**Warren Buffett:** So you're really asking about the future prospects of Nike and Reebok?

**Questioner:** Yeah.

**Warren Buffett:** I don't know that much about those businesses. We do have one person in this audience, at least, who owns a lot of Reebok. But I'm not expressing a negative view in any way on that. I just don't understand their competitive position and the likelihood of permanence of their competitive position over a 10 or 20 year period as well as I think I understand the position of Brown and Dexter. That doesn't mean I think that it's inferior. [It] doesn't mean that I think that we've got better businesses or anything. I think we've got very good businesses. But I haven't done the work, and I'm not sure if I did the work I would understand them. I think they are harder to understand, frankly, and to develop a fix on than our kinds. But they may be easier for other people who just have a better insight into that kind of business.

## Part 2: Rather multiply by three than by pi; fear of hiring "somebody with a label" ([01:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=POliree5GJ8&t=94))

**Warren Buffett:** Some businesses are a lot easier to understand than others, and Charlie and I don't like difficult problems. I mean, if something is hard to figure [...] I mean, we'd rather multiply by three than by pi. I mean, it's just easier for us. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger:** Well, that is such an obvious point, and yet so many people think if they just hire somebody with the appropriate labels they can do something very difficult. That is one of the most dangerous ideas a human being can have. All kinds of things just intrinsically create problems.

The other day I was dealing with a problem, and I said this thing [...] it's a new building. I said, "This thing has three things I've learned to fear: an architect, a contractor and a hill." And if you go [through] life like that, I think you at least make fewer mistakes than people who think they can do anything by just hiring somebody with a label.

## Part 3: You don't have to do exceptional things to get exceptional results ([03:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=POliree5GJ8&t=189))

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* Excuse me. You don't have to hire out your thinking if you keep it simple. We've said this before, but you don't have to do exceptional things to get exceptional results. And some people think that if you jump over a seven foot bar, that the ribbon they pin on you is going to be worth more money than if you step over a one foot bar, and it just isn't true in the investment world at all.

So you can do very ordinary things. I mean, what is complicated about this? But we're three billion dollars pre-tax better off than we were a few years ago because of it. There's nothing that I know about that product or its distribution system, its finances or anything, that really hundreds of thousands or millions of people aren't capable of [knowing], that they don't already know. They just don't do anything about it.

And similarly, if you get into some complicated business, you can get a report that's a thousand pages thick, and you've got PhDs working on it, but it doesn't mean anything. What you've got is a report, but you won't understand that business [and] what it's going to look like in 10 or 15 years.

## Part 4: "A mine is a hole in the ground owned by a liar": sellers' projections ([03:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=POliree5GJ8&t=236))

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* The big thing to do is avoid being wrong. There's some things that are so intrinsically dangerous. Another of my heroes is Mark Twain, who looked at the promoters of his day, and he said, "A mine is a hole in the ground owned by a liar." And that's the way I've come to look at projections.

I mean, basically, I can remember [unclear: "one"] and I were offered two million dollars worth of projections once in the course of buying a business, and the book was this thick [...]

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* And for nothing. And if we were given it for nothing, [unclear: "and it"] wouldn't open it. We'd almost pay 2 million not to look at it. I mean, it's ridiculous. I do not understand why any buyer of a business looks at a bunch of projections put together by a seller or his agent. I mean, you can almost say that it's naive to think that that has any utility whatsoever. We just are not interested. If we don't have some idea ourselves of what we think the future is, to sit there and listen to some other guy who's trying to sell us the business, or get a commission on it, tell us what the future is going to be, like I say, it's very naive.

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Yeah. And five years out.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. We had a line in the report one time: "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut." And it's quite applicable to projections by sellers of businesses.
