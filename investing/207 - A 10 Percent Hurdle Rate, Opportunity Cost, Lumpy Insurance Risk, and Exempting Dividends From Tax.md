# A 10 Percent Hurdle Rate, Opportunity Cost, Lumpy Insurance Risk, and Exempting Dividends From Tax

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gNEFdiNhB-8

Recorded: Parts 1–4 2003 Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence); Part 5 most likely the same 2003 meeting (moderate confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: You Should Be Getting A Ten Percent Annual Return From Stocks". It is misleading: Buffett makes no promise or recommendation about what investors "should" get. He says that Berkshire does not want to buy equities where its "real expectancy is below 10%", that this cutoff is "arbitrary" with "no scientific studies" behind it, and that 10% "is not that great after tax anyway". The rest of the clip is Munger on opportunity cost, Buffett on why Berkshire keeps cash earning 1¼% rather than locking in 5% on 20-year governments, Berkshire's willingness to take lumpy insurance risks (the Pepsi billion-dollar contest), and a separate fragment on taxing dividends. The clip is from the **2003** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence). The clues: Buffett says Berkshire's $16 billion is getting "one and a quarter percent pre-tax" (U.S. short rates were about 1–1.25% in spring 2003); he describes the Pepsi contest with "one chance in a thousand of winning a billion dollars" and a drawing "in September" (Pepsi's billion-dollar promotion, drawn in September 2003); and Buffett closes the meeting ("I hope we see you all next year"). Part 5 comes after that closing line, so it is a separate excerpt; its subject, the proposal to exempt dividends from income tax, was the live issue of early 2003, so it is most likely from the same meeting (moderate confidence).

**Warren Buffett:** *[speaker uncertain]* But so far, at least, we have hurdles in our mind which are basically [...] well, they involve implicitly future opportunity cost. **Charlie Munger:** And years ago, when Warren ran a partnership, and to some extent the partnership that I ran was operated in the same way, we implicitly did what you're suggesting, in that part of the partnership funds were in so-called event arbitrage investments, and those tended to generate returns occasionally when the market generally was in the tank, and alternative investments would more mimic the general market. So we were doing what this academic theory prescribes 40 years ago, but we didn't use the modern lingo.

## Part 4: Opportunity cost is personal ([07:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gNEFdiNhB-8&t=423))

**Charlie Munger:** Yeah, but once you're talking about opportunity cost that's personal to yourself and your own situation, you've departed from modern finance totally. And that's what we've done.
