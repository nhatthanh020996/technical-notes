# Capital-Hungry Businesses vs. Easy Decisions, Preparing for a Depression, Not Commenting on Berkshire's Price, George Burns, and Correlation with the S&P 500

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m54aCzlNZ5U

Recorded: 1998 Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence)

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett: Avoid These Stocks At All Costs!". It is misleading: there is no list of stocks to avoid. Buffett describes businesses like airlines and textiles that force you to keep spending capital without clear prospects of a return, contrasts them with See's, FlightSafety and Coca-Cola, and Munger explains the difference between a business that throws up easy decisions and one that throws up agonizing ones. The rest of the clip is unrelated Q&A: whether Berkshire is prepared for a 1929-style depression, a refusal to say whether Berkshire stock is over- or underpriced, a question about their diet (George Burns stories), and why they don't publish a correlation coefficient between Berkshire and the S&P 500. The clip is from the **1998** Berkshire annual meeting (high confidence). The clues: Buffett says "I don't care what it sells for on May 4th 1998" (the 1998 meeting was held on Monday, May 4, 1998); the A share at about $69,000 and the B share at about $2,300 match spring 1998; Buffett says he has been on the Coca-Cola board "now for 10 years" (he joined in 1989); the "area seven/eight/nine" microphone numbering fits that era.

## Part 2: Good businesses throw up easy decisions; bad ones throw up agonizing ones (USAir) ([02:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m54aCzlNZ5U&t=142))

**Charlie Munger:** I've heard Warren say since very early in his life that the difference between a good business and a bad business is [that] usually the good business just throws up one easy decision after another, whereas the bad business gives you a horrible choice where the decision is hard to make: is this really going to work, is it worth the money? If you want a system for determining which is a good business and which is a bad business, see which one is throwing the management bloopers time after time after time, easy decisions. It's not very hard for us to decide to open a new See's store in a new shopping center in California that's obviously going to succeed. It's a blooper. And you've got people there that will actually get them [...]

**Charlie Munger:** *[speaker uncertain]* Well, you're saying you get blooper after blooper.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. And then Charlie and I sat on USAir, and the decisions would come along, and it would be a question of, you know, do you buy the Eastern Shuttle or whatever it may be. ## Part 3: Is Berkshire prepared for a 1929-style depression? ([05:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m54aCzlNZ5U&t=305))

**Questioner (Bakul Patel, upstate New York):** Area seven. My name is Bakul Patel, I am from upstate New York. My question is: is Berkshire prepared for a 1929 style of depression, or a prolonged bear market like exists in Japan?

**Warren Buffett:** Well, funny, we don't expect what you're talking about, but we're probably about as well prepared as any company can be for adversity, because Berkshire has been built to last. Net, we would benefit over a 20-year period by having some periods of terrible markets periodically in that 20-year period. That doesn't mean we're wishing for them, and it doesn't mean they're going to happen. But we make our money by allocating capital well, and the lower the general stock market would be, the better we can allocate capital. So we're well prepared, but we're not necessarily expecting [it].

**Charlie Munger:** That's right.

**Warren Buffett:** Yeah. We are not going to ever sell everything and go to cash and wait for the crash so we can go back in. On the other hand, we are structured so that, I think, net, a lot of turmoil in the next 20 years will help us, not hurt us. I don't mean it'll be pleasant to go through the down cycle, but it's part of the game.

## Part 6: Why they don't publish a correlation between Berkshire and the S&P 500 ([10:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m54aCzlNZ5U&t=615))

**Warren Buffett:** Well, it could be calculated, but I don't think it would have much meaning. I mean, it would be a historical correlation coefficient, which I would be very reluctant to have people place any weight [on]. And I try to indicate even the limitations of the yearly comparison of the relative performance, because what was doable by us in the past is not doable today.

I mentioned in my annual report [that] the best decade I ever had on comparative performance by far was the 50s. Now, I don't think it was because I was a lot smarter [unclear: "than unwilling to accept that"], but I had some edge of [...] well, there's probably 40-plus points per year. But I was working with it. That is [of] no relevance to [unclear: "the day"; likely "today"] whatsoever. It would be misleading to publish it or make calculations based on it.

So I think that you would find [...] I don't know what you'd find on a specific correlation between Berkshire and the S&P. You find a lot of correlation [...] well, you might not find so much. You find it in intrinsic value between that and Coke and a few stocks like that. But I don't really think that's particularly useful information going forward. We have no objection [if] anybody wants to make the calculation, but it wouldn't be something that would be of any utility to us, and [unclear: "if we don't think it should be utility does"], we don't want to put it out for shareholders as being a possible utility.

We do think that the S&P annual calculation has some meaning, because it's an alternative for people to invest. They don't need us to buy the S&P.
