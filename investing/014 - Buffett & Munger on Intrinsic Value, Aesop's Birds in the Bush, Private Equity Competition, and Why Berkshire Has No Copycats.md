# Buffett & Munger on Intrinsic Value, Aesop's Birds in the Bush, Private Equity Competition, and Why Berkshire Has No Copycats

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SFyUT_nkLNU

Recorded: compilation of Berkshire meeting clips. Parts 3-5: roughly 2014-2016 (low-moderate confidence); Part 6: 1997 or 1998 (moderate confidence); Parts 1-2: undated

> Note: The original YouTube title is "Warren Buffett Explains How To Calculate Intrinsic Value Of A Stock". It is misleading: Buffett gives no calculation method. He defines intrinsic value (the present value of all cash a business will distribute between now and "Judgment Day"), says it is "easy to say and impossible to figure", and contrasts Graham's quantitative emphasis with Fisher's and Munger's qualitative one. About a third of the video (Parts 3 to 5) is not about intrinsic value at all: it covers private equity as a competitor and Munger on why Berkshire's model will last and why nobody copies it.
>
> The video is a compilation of clips from **at least two different meetings**:
> - Parts 3 to 5 are most likely from the mid-2010s, roughly **2014–2016** (low–moderate confidence). The clues: private equity "presently" borrowing "very cheap", Munger speaking of Berkshire's model in a long-term, looking-back way, and Buffett turning to "Carol" (Carol Loomis, a panel questioner from 2006 to 2018).
> - Part 6 is from **1997 or 1998** (moderate confidence). The clues: FlightSafety (acquired at the end of 1996) "will build a lot of simulators this year", and Andy Grove is referred to as the person running Intel (he was CEO until 1998).
> - Parts 1 and 2 could not be dated from the content. They may or may not be from the same meeting as Parts 3 to 5.

## Part 6: Coke vs. Intel, and why Berkshire's own intrinsic value is harder to compute ([06:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SFyUT_nkLNU&t=383))

*(A separate clip from a different, earlier meeting. It starts at the beginning of an answer; the question is not included.)*

**Warren Buffett:** [On] the intrinsic value question: I mean, by definition, intrinsic value is the present value of the stream of cash that's going to be generated by any financial asset between now and doomsday. And that's easy to say and impossible to figure. But it's the kind of thing that we're looking at when we look at a Coca-Cola, where we think it's much easier to evaluate the stream of cash that comes in the future than it is in a company such as Intel, marvelous as it may be. We just [...] it's easier for us. It may not [...] Andy Grove may be better at figuring out Intel than Coke.

And Berkshire [...] it is complicated by the fact that we have no business that naturally employs all of the capital that flows to us. So it is dependent to some extent on the opportunities available and the ingenuity used when that cash pours in, as it does. Some businesses have a natural use for the cash. Actually, Intel has a good natural use for the cash over time as they've expanded in their business, and many businesses do. But we do not have a natural use. We have some businesses that use significant amounts of cash, or FlightSafety will build a lot of simulators this year, and they cost real money. But in relation to the resources available, we have to come up with new uses, new ways to use cash. And that makes for a more difficult valuation job than if you've got [...] well, the classic case used to be a water or electric utility, where the cash could be deployed and the return was more or less guaranteed within a narrow range, and it was very easy to make calculations then as to the expectable returns in the future.

But that's not the case at Berkshire. We've got very good businesses, both directly and partially owned, and those businesses are going to do well for a long, long time. But we do have new cash coming out all the time, and sometimes we have good ideas for that cash and sometimes we don't. And that does make your job more difficult in terms of computing intrinsic value.
